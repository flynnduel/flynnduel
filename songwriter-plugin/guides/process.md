# Process guide

This guide covers how songs get started and kept moving: the eight entry points and how each reorders the work, the habit of receiving first and editing second, a bank for fragments, ways to get unstuck, versions and cut material, and credit splits. Most of the process evidence is interview testimony from American Songwriter (Zollo's interviews, Tweedy's book quotes), NPR transcripts and Berklee pages that were read in full. It describes what working writers say they do, not what works best, so the plugin offers each as an option. Tags show the evidence level; see sources.md.

Stages are facets, not a corridor. Any stage can be reached at any time, a song can take several sessions or years, and the plugin never assumes a song finishes in one sitting. [design]

## Entry points

Eight doors lead into one song record. Each door marks what the writer already has as given, skips what exists, and proposes the next step. The reorderings are the plugin's own inference from the interviews. [design]

- Riff, groove or track first: Simon said he learned to make the tracks first and write the songs later, with words coming from the sound and rhythm of the music. Order: feel, key and tempo; section map; scat melody; words fitted to the existing rhythm; the chart comes early. [verified]
- Guitar song, words and music together: Simon's usual way before Graceland was to write on guitar and then bring the song to musicians. Order: chords, melody and words evolve together; arrangement and band chart come last. [verified]
- Lyric first: Hunter often handed Garcia lyrics to set, and Goffin and King split words and melody. Order: the stanzas fix meter and stress; melody and chords are fitted to them, with a check for stress clashes. [verified]
- Music changes or progression first: Garcia sometimes handed Hunter finished changes to write words for. Order: the chart exists; map the sections; write words to the changes. [verified]
- Title first: Stolpe's flow starts with the title as the main message, places it in the chorus's power positions (first, last, middle line), outlines each section, then writes verses that recolor the title. Order: title, chorus, section outline, verses, then melody and chords, trying the song as verse and refrain or verse and chorus and at another tempo. [verified]
- Brief or commission: Webb's "Wichita Lineman" was a request for another place song, timeboxed to under two hours and sent off while he thought it unfinished. That was one commissioned session, not his routine. Order: constraint and concept first, then a timed draft, shippable even if it feels unfinished. [verified]
- Banked fragment: a line, riff, progression or title captured earlier is promoted into a song. Order: bank it, or expand it at once if an instrument is at hand, then build outward from the core. [design]
- Co-write exchange: Hunter described trading "starter ideas" back and forth with Garcia, with the best results when each batted work back until both agreed. Order: alternate partial drafts on the words and music, with shared history. [verified]
- Townes Van Zandt sometimes had the core of a song come all at once; treat that as a banked fragment or as a whole-song start. [verified]
- Simon tries to discover what a song says rather than plot it, and words often come from the sound of the music before they become coherent thoughts. [verified]
- No data exists on how often professionals use each entry point. [unverified]
- Steve Seskin is not a source for title-first writing; the title-first material here is Stolpe's. [verified]

## Receive, then edit

Interview accounts from Zollo's book interviews, read on American Songwriter, show a shared stance: generation is receptive and the conscious work is selecting and setting up. The plugin separates the two. [verified]

- Dylan wants an environment where he can accept unconscious material and take it down. [verified]
- Simon tries to discover a song rather than plot it, and sees his job as the editor who spots good lines and builds the first half of a thought as set-up for the second. [verified]
- Simon says the first line of a song is crucial because listener attention peaks there. [verified]
- King's advice on blocks is to not worry, get up, do something else and come back relaxed. [verified]
- Townes Van Zandt edits "by the word", down to adding a single "s", and values keeping a song bare and simple. [verified]
- Cohen said he had to work as hard as any laborer; he took years over some songs. His "Hallelujah" draft count is a range of 80 to 180 verses, of which he claimed 150, per Wikipedia. [verified]
- Newman keeps the critic smaller than the creator while drafting (via Zollo's revision column). [verified]
- Mitchell, Newman and Young could not be verified from primary text, so the plugin does not cite their habits. [unverified]
- Generation modes show no critique; an explicit editor mode does. Switching modes is the writer's call (shared rule 4). [design]
- Editor-mode offers drawn from these writers: Simon's set-up and payoff check, a word-by-word pass (Townes), a first-line check (Simon) and a simplicity check (Townes, Simon). [design]
- Dodson's creator and editor split says the same in craft terms: the creator holds about 90% of the power while drafting. [verified]

## Bank

- A quick "store it" path saves an idea in seconds; Townes stores a good line that arrives while he is driving. [verified]
- Bank typed items (title, line, core, riff or memo, progression, groove, concept) with key, tempo and mood tags and a date. [design]
- Promoting a bank item into a song makes it the song's entry point and marks its facet as given. [design]
- Banking is not drafting: the plugin saves the item and does not critique it. [design]
- A large bank is normal: Seskin banked about 150 uncut songs over three years of commuting, and Goffin called 150 early songs by him and King "bad songs" before their first big hit. [verified]
- Tweedy's one-in-five ratio supports treating most banked ideas as practice. [verified]

## Getting unstuck

Offer these as options, never as obligations, and avoid streak or guilt messaging. [design]

- Tweedy's timer: a 5 to 10 minute freewrite toward a finished song. [verified]
- Tweedy's movement prompt: get the body involved and out of the way of the mind. [verified]
- Tweedy's sleep-on-it prompt: go to bed with a half-finished idea. [verified]
- Tweedy on inspiration: it has to be invited, and a bad song leaves no permanent stain. He writes every day and says about one song in five means a lot. [verified]
- Tweedy says the hard part when low is picking up the guitar, after which the music reliably helps. [verified]
- King's guilt-free walk-away: step out, do something else, come back. [verified]
- Townes's focused session: he once took a motel room to write and came away with two or three songs, though he was skeptical of hour-by-hour scheduled co-writes. [verified]
- Townes finished "Pancho and Lefty" the day after he first played it, so a finish-tomorrow note is legitimate. [verified]
- Word ladders, stealing words from a book, cut-ups of the writer's own banked lines, a hum-while-skimming melody exercise and a three-part daily checklist are attributed to Tweedy's book but were read in summaries only. Label them as such when offered. [as reported]
- Hunter's spiral notebook on tour shows a lyricist who is not a performer still works by capture. [verified]
- Webb's routine and step-away advice come from a book summary only. [unverified]

## Versions and cuts

- No source documents how professionals name files or number drafts, so the plugin's scheme is its own. [unverified]
- A version is a snapshot plus a pool of cut verses, so a version is a chosen subset of the verses. The pattern comes from Cohen's notebooks and the 15 pages of lyrics he faxed to John Cale, who picked out verses (per Wikipedia); different performances use different subsets. [design]
- Before the lyrics change, snapshot to drafts; removed material goes to cuts and is never deleted. [design]
- A "shipped but unfinished" flag fits Webb, who sent off "Wichita Lineman" believing it unfinished and later found flaws in it. [verified]
- Zollo records Cohen cutting whole polished verses to protect a song's tone. [verified]
- Most songs being practice is normal, so a catalogue view should not treat unfinished songs as failures. [design]
- Draft counts for famous songs are ranges, not facts; never state a single number. [verified]

## Co-writing and splits

- Hunter was a formal member of the Grateful Dead as a non-performing lyricist from 1967 to 1995, per Wikipedia, so a non-performing lyricist is a full member of a split sheet. [verified]
- The Hunter and Garcia percentages were not found, and no Brill Building split data was found. [unverified]
- Goffin and King divided the work between words (mostly Goffin) and melody (mostly King). [verified]
- Carole King describes Brill-era writers squeezed into small rooms each day, with the publisher playing one writer against another. [as reported]
- The Nashville default is an equal split among everyone in the room unless agreed beforehand; this is secondhand. [as reported]
- Split sheet design: start it at the first co-writing session; roles are words, music or both; percentages are arbitrary and editable and default to equal; a non-performing lyricist is allowed. Keep a shared draft history for back-and-forth writing. [design]
- The plugin records the split; it does not give legal or contractual advice. [design]
