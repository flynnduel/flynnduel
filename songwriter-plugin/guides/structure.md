# Structure guide

This guide condenses what the plugin knows about song form: the forms a writer can choose from, the job of each section, how sections contrast, where hooks and titles sit, and how to deviate on purpose. The form research rests on peer-reviewed corpus and theory work (Summach, Nobile, Stroud, de Clercq, Temperley, Osborn) plus Pattison's own pages, so most of it is verified. Timing norms and hook taxonomies are secondhand and appear only under their own heading. Tags show the evidence level; see sources.md for what was actually read.

The plugin offers a menu, never a template. No form is "the correct one", and the writer picks by what the song needs to do. [design]

## Form menu

Each entry names the form, what it is, a source, and when it tends to fit. The "when it fits" lines are this plugin's synthesis; the sources describe the forms, not when to choose them.

- Strophic or verse-refrain (AAA): repeated strophes, each a point where the song could satisfyingly end, more common in blues, early hip-hop and early rock and roll (Open Music Theory). [verified]
- When each form fits is the plugin's synthesis, not a finding: strophic for narrative songs where the story is the point, AABA for melody-led songs with a title refrain, simple verse-chorus for groove or vamp songs, contrasting verse-chorus for songs that build toward a goal. [design]
- AABA (32-bar): the bridge withholds A, stresses non-tonic harmony, often ends on V and must be followed by A; a full AABA is normally repeated whole or as BA with no new lyrics after the first cycle (Open Music Theory). [verified]
- Simple verse-chorus: verse and chorus share harmonic content; the label is Covach's, as reported by de Clercq. [verified]
- Contrasting verse-chorus: verse and chorus differ in harmony and design; the type is Covach's, but his five-type list was read only secondhand. [as reported]
- Verse, prechorus, chorus, postchorus: the order is fixed, the four make one cycle, and not all four need be present (Open Music Theory). [verified]
- Compound AABA with an abridged last A (written AABA-prime): Temperley's default layout, as quoted by Hudson, is two verse-chorus pairs, then an instrumental or bridge, then a third pair or just a chorus; the final A is often shortened or altered, and Hudson proposes the prime label for that. [verified]
- Compound AABA, scope: Temperley holds that virtually all verse-chorus songs are compound AABA, while Covach treats it as one subtype. [verified]
- So present the scope of compound AABA as an open disagreement, not a settled fact. [design]
- Verse-hook: the form of much hip-hop. Open Music Theory supports its paradigms; the corpus count behind it (Duinker) was read secondhand. [as reported]
- Through-composed rock (Osborn): no discrete, identifiable section is recapitulated; the climax is often held back to the end. [verified]
- Terminal climax (Osborn): an independent climax arrives once at the song's end and balances against the chorus. The type list comes from search summaries, not the paper. [as reported]
- Drop forms: the chorus withholds the peak and a drop or instrumental postchorus supplies it; see Asymmetry below for Nobile's anti-telos chorus. [verified]
- Head plus open section ("jam" form): a stated head and an open improvised section. No peer-reviewed source exists for jam-band form, so label it a heuristic whenever it is offered. [unverified]
- Module size: modules typically span 8 to 24 bars, a four-bar invariant passage is a refrain, not a chorus, and a new module cannot begin until the current pattern completes. [verified]
- Dating: verse-chorus dominates the Billboard Top 20 from the 1980s, and Temperley says simple verse and AABA became much rarer after 1970. [verified]
- Use dating to describe a form's era, never to rank it. [design]
- Corpus limits: Summach drew norms from 700 Billboard Top-20 songs (1955 to 1989) and warns they may not hold for genres weak on the charts or rising later. [verified]
- Label ambiguity: de Clercq scores a section against weighted attributes and allows blends such as prechorus/chorus, chorus/link and bridge/chorus. [verified]
- Report a blend when two roles overlap instead of forcing one label. [design]

## Section jobs

- Verse: lyric-variant, advances the narrative or situation, keeps intensity lower so the chorus sounds climactic against it. [verified]
- Chorus: lyric-invariant, carries the title or hook, and shows heightened intensity through denser or more active texture, prominent backing vocals or a higher melody. [verified]
- Prechorus: builds momentum toward the chorus. Summach lists changes of groove, lyric phrasing, unit length, dynamics, register, instrumentation, timbre and harmonic progression, with faster harmonic rhythm as one lever. [verified]
- Prechorus, signs to look for: fragmentation into one- or two-bar units, rising register, an emphasised subdominant, and an open ending that does not cadence on tonic. [verified]
- Prechorus, accompaniment drop-out: since the 1960s the accompaniment often thins in the prechorus's last bar to spotlight the chorus arrival. [verified]
- Prechorus, incidence: about a third of verse-chorus hits in 1965-69 had one and about 60% by the late 1980s, with dips around 1970 and in 1979 when disco was strong; the dating of its first appearance is inconsistent inside Summach's own article. [verified]
- Prechorus and length: prechorus songs were no longer than verse-chorus songs without one. [verified]
- So do not treat adding a prechorus as a length decision. [design]
- Nobile's telos principle: with a prechorus, verse, prechorus and chorus map onto initiation, buildup and arrival, and the goal is the start of the chorus; without a prechorus the goal tends to arrive at the end of the chorus. [verified]
- Nobile, harmony: an initiating verse does not complete a harmonic circuit and must lead to a chorus, while a sectional verse closes its own circuit. [verified]
- Nobile, eras: harmonic teleology in the 1960s to 80s, texture and voice as the drivers from 1991 to 2010, and subversions such as anti-telos choruses and drops in the 2010s. [verified]
- Postchorus, codetta type (Stroud): the chorus is the climax and the postchorus adds closure, often repeating short motives or vocables on tonic. [verified]
- Postchorus, anthem type (Stroud): a memorable, climactic section after a building chorus, close to the dance chorus; the climax lives after the chorus, not in it. [verified]
- Postchorus, rule of thumb: repeated short motives or vocables are typical enough to decide the label. [verified]
- Bridge: usually appears once, before the last (pre)chorus, replacing a verse or prechorus in that cycle; in verse-chorus songs it has more freedom to contrast than an AABA bridge, and may be absent. [verified]
- Bridge, option: Stolpe notes that a partial repeat of the chorus or prechorus can be the strongest bridge late in a song. [verified]
- Bridge, optional in modern pop: Osborn reports (via Cobb) that Top-40 EDM almost never has a viable contrasting bridge. [verified]
- So offer a groove or texture switch as an alternative to a bridge. [design]
- Climb: a one-phrase, prechorus-function ending of a verse. [verified]
- Refrain versus chorus: a refrain is a lyric-invariant passage inside a section, usually a tail refrain; a chorus is a full section. [verified]
- Intro, outro, coda: intros are short and mostly instrumental and preview core material; outros reuse earlier material and lose energy; a coda brings new material. [verified]
- Closing rhetoric: immediate repetition of a core section, thinning texture, late intensification, fadeout, or closing an open section harmonically. [verified]
- Statement, restatement, departure, conclusion (srdc, Everett's labels): the most common strophe pattern, and the ancestor of verse, prechorus and chorus per Summach and Nobile. [verified]
- Sentential share: more than a quarter of Summach's 700 songs contain a sentential module and strophes are sentential almost half the time. [verified]
- "Energy gain" is Open Music Theory's summary term; Summach's own words are momentum and teleology. [verified]
- Use "energy gain" only as that summary term. [design]

## Contrast

- Pattison's form follows function has two parts. Prosody: design form from what the section must do. Contrast: same design, same function; different design, different function. [verified]
- Verses share a form; a chorus changes at least one of rhyme scheme, phrase lengths, number of phrases or phrase rhythms, sometimes all four. [verified]
- The contrast must start at the new section's first phrase. A bridge that finally contrasts after verse and chorus have matched arrives after listeners have drifted. [verified]
- Check: compare the chorus's first line with the verse norm on stress count, rhythm, rhyme sound and length; a match means contrast arrives late or not at all. The toolkit's `stability` subcommand does this comparison. [design]
- Pattison's failure case is two sections in Common Meter (4 and 3 stresses, alternating rhyme, same phrase count) back to back, which he describes as having no contrast. [verified]
- Fix by re-proportioning: for example, six lines against two instead of two matching four-line halves. [verified]
- Contrast carrying meaning: in his reading of one published song the chorus speeds up with shorter phrases, and the last chorus line takes one bar to enact "they go so fast". [verified]
- Stolpe's levers for contrast inside sections: phrase length, phrase start relative to the downbeat, and chord-tone versus non-chord-tone starts. Her worked case runs one-bar post-downbeat phrases in the verse, a four-bar downbeat phrase in the prechorus and two-bar pickup phrases in the chorus. [verified]
- Stolpe, bridge levers: if the second chorus was the peak, the bridge can exceed it (new or borrowed chord, faster changes, higher pitches, wider intervals) or drop below it (slower harmonic rhythm, lower vocal, more rests). [verified]
- If a bridge will not come, the problem may lie upstream: a verse and chorus that already contrast leave nothing to contrast with, or the song lacks a clear idea or repeated motif. [verified]
- Perricone's bridge example uses five levers: starting chord, plainer rhythm, plainer contour, more uniform phrase lengths, and a middle-register tessitura; it still belongs to the song through key, earlier harmonies and point of view. [verified]
- Perricone, prechorus and bridge claims from Great Songwriting Techniques (a prechorus moves away from tonic and avoids a tonic cadence; a bridge matters less after many cycles) were read only in an unofficial copy. [as reported]

## Hooks and titles

- Title spotlight: first line, last line, or both, per Pattison; see lyrics.md for the title-mining and recoloring tools. [verified]
- Blume's table of contents lists making it obvious where the title goes without the lyric, signature licks, nonsense syllables, symmetrical phrases and the dreaded second verse; only the headings were read, not the content. [verified]
- Melodic title test: the title line should stand out as a peak, a long note, a rhythmic break or a strategic position. This inference from Blume's heading is the research team's own. [design]
- Perricone raised the melody of his title line to agree with a positive lyric, and paid close attention to title placement in his own song. [verified]
- Blume's class advice (title written first, almost always in the chorus, verse sets up the title so the chorus feels like the answer) comes from a transcription. [as reported]
- Blume's numbered hook taxonomy and BMI column details (signature lick as an instrumental hook, rhythmic hooks, hooks in any section) are secondhand. [as reported]
- Stroud's postchorus vocables count as a second kind of hook. [verified]
- Stroud notes that recent tracks increasingly open on the chorus, which makes chorus-first an option, not a norm. [verified]
- The interviewer in a verified Blume interview disputes his title-in-the-chorus rule with counterexamples. [verified]
- So treat title placement as a choice with options. [design]
- Always offer the placement options side by side: first line, last line, both, or a melodic outlier mid-chorus. [design]

## Asymmetry

Symmetry is the baseline that makes a deviation audible. The plugin suggests one meaningful deviation, tied to what the song is saying, never decoration. [design]

- Mismatched line lengths and rhymes create pull: unequal lengths such as 3, 3 and 5 stresses feel unstable and tell the listener to keep going. [verified]
- Principle of Sequence: once line 3 mimics line 1 we expect line 4 to mimic line 2, and a break in that sequence is felt. [verified]
- An interrupted bridge can enact an unresolved story: Pattison reads a three-line bridge that stops and jumps back to verse 1 as a character stuck going backwards. [verified]
- Truncated final line, as in the one-bar last line above (Pattison). [verified]
- Extended final lines are a natural counterpart but no source was read for them. [design]
- Prechorus fragmentation: lyric and rest units shortening from 2+2 to 1+1, or an extended pause before the chorus. [verified]
- Anti-telos chorus: the build projects a climax and the chorus withholds it at the song's lowest energy, then a drop or instrumental postchorus supplies the peak; sometimes the chorus plays twice, first low and then full. [verified]
- Anthem postchorus, as above, moves the climax out of the chorus. [verified]
- Telescoped sections: a prechorus/chorus blend or a chorus/link blend compresses the form. [verified]
- Abridged or altered final cycle in compound AABA. [verified]
- Terminal climax placed once at the end. [as reported]
- Odd versus even line counts (even settled, odd unstable) and the ranking of abab against abba appear in third-party summaries of Pattison's book; only the unmatched-length logic is verified from his own pages. [as reported]
- False endings and double choruses are craft folklore without a source. [unverified]
- No study compares listener preference for symmetric and asymmetric forms, so never claim an asymmetric form is better. [unverified]

## Market heuristics

These apply only when the writer has declared a commercial goal (shared rule 6). Present them as market observations, never craft laws.

- Léveillé Gauvin's study of 303 US top-10 singles (1986 to 2015) is reported to show intros shrinking from about 20 seconds to about 5, titles arriving earlier and getting shorter, tempos rising, and no trend in lyrical self-focus; the paper was not read and figures differ across reports. [as reported]
- Murphy's finding that the title should arrive within 60 seconds is known from summary pages only. [as reported]
- Median time to first chorus (about 32 seconds in 1958-69, about 45 seconds for 2024 number ones) comes from an industry blog citing a third party. [as reported]
- Summach's verified datum on length: median Top-20 length rose from about 150 to about 250 seconds between the early 1960s and late 1980s. [verified]
- Title in the chorus, early title and short intros are heuristics for the radio market and are disputed even by the writers who teach them. [as reported]
- NSAI evaluation criteria could not be read; do not cite them. [unverified]
- When a commercial goal is declared, state the heuristic, its source status and the alternative side by side, and let the writer decide. [design]
