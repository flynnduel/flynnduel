# Style-specific findings

This is the one index of what the plugin's research says about particular styles. A skill that has a declared style (`style:` in `song.md`, or the writer naming one) reads this file and uses nothing else as style material (shared rule 6). Each entry paraphrases a finding already in another guide, points to that guide's section, names the source and keeps the guide's tag. "Tied by the research" says whether the source itself links the finding to the style; only those entries count as style findings. Tags show the evidence level; see sources.md.

## How to use this index

- Match the writer's style word against each entry's label and its spellings, ignoring case, hyphens and spaces ("hip hop", "Hip-Hop" and "hiphop" are one label). The spellings lists are this plugin's own. [design]
- A style with no entry here, or whose entry has no usable finding, gets a plain statement: the research behind this plugin has nothing specific to that style on this. Then give the style-neutral guidance. [design]
- Use a finding only for the kind of question its guide answers: a harmony finding is not arrangement advice, and a form finding is not a feel. When the entry has nothing for the question asked, say that too. [design]
- Never widen a finding: one about 1970s soul or early hip-hop describes that, not the whole style. [design]
- Each guide's own Don'ts still apply, for example harmony.md's ban on citing a genre rate that was not read. [design]
- Quote a finding in one line with its source and its tag; never present it as a rule for the style. [design]
- General corpora are not style findings. The de Clercq and Temperley base rates in harmony.md come from a rock corpus, and Summach's form figures in structure.md from Billboard Top-20 hits of 1955 to 1989; both are the plugin's general base rates and are never presented as what a style does. [design]

## Index

### Blues
Spellings: blues, blues rock, blues-based rock.
- Everett's Type 4 covers blues-based rock: minor-pentatonic-inflected major-mode systems, with the structural harmony in the backing rather than the vocal blue notes (harmony.md, Tonal systems; Everett). Tied by the research: yes. [verified]
- Strophic or verse-refrain form is more common in blues, early hip-hop and early rock and roll (structure.md, Form menu; Open Music Theory). Tied by the research: yes. [verified]

### Rock
Spellings: rock, rock and roll, rock'n'roll.
- Everett's Type 5: triad-doubled or power-chord minor-pentatonic systems unique to rock, where common practice is often irrelevant at the surface (harmony.md, Tonal systems; Everett). Tied by the research: yes. [verified]
- Through-composed rock: no discrete section is recapitulated, and the climax is often held back to the end (structure.md, Form menu; Osborn). Tied by the research: yes. [verified]
- Strophic form is more common in early rock and roll (structure.md, Form menu; Open Music Theory). Tied by the research: yes, to early rock and roll only. [verified]
- Not a style finding: harmony.md's base rates come from a rock corpus but are general rates (see How to use). [design]

### Metal
Spellings: metal, heavy metal.
- Everett's Type 6a: chromatically inflected systems of early metal (harmony.md, Tonal systems; Everett). Tied by the research: yes, to early metal. [verified]

### Pop
Spellings: pop, chart pop.
- The greatest share of chart pop falls in Everett's Type 1, major-mode systems with common-practice harmony (harmony.md, Tonal systems; Everett). Tied by the research: yes. [verified]
- In recent pop minor, flat-VI, flat-VII, i has largely replaced iv, V, i, and pop chords are almost always in root position (harmony.md, Tonal systems; Open Music Theory). Tied by the research: yes. [verified]
- Not a style finding: Summach's chart-form figures describe hits of an era, not a pop style (see How to use). [design]

### EDM-influenced pop
Spellings: EDM, EDM pop, electronic dance music, Top-40 EDM.
- Top-40 EDM songs are reported almost never to have a viable contrasting bridge (structure.md, Section jobs; arrangement.md, Energy; Osborn via Cobb). Tied by the research: yes. [as reported]

### Hip-hop
Spellings: hip-hop, hip hop, hiphop, rap.
- Verse-hook is the form of much hip-hop; Open Music Theory supports its paradigms, and the corpus count behind it was read secondhand (structure.md, Form menu; Open Music Theory, Duinker). Tied by the research: yes. [as reported]
- Strophic form is more common in early hip-hop (structure.md, Form menu; Open Music Theory). Tied by the research: yes, to early hip-hop. [verified]

### Soul
Spellings: soul.
- The soul dominant, IV over the fifth degree in the bass, blends subdominant and dominant and is especially common in 1970s soul (harmony.md, Tonal systems; Spicer). Tied by the research: yes, to 1970s soul. [verified]

### Funk
Spellings: funk.
- In funk the strumming hand keeps a steady sixteenth motion while the fretting hand damps strokes (guitar.md, Feel; Wikipedia). Tied by the research: yes. [verified]
- Funk often swings sixteenths (guitar.md, Feel; Wikipedia). Tied by the research: yes. [verified]

### Jazz
Spellings: jazz.
- V-2 and V-4 voicings hold most stock jazz chords, in Hober's account of Greene's V-System (guitar.md, Voicings; Greene and Hober). Tied by the research: yes. [verified]

### Jam band
Spellings: jam band, jam-band, jam.
- Head plus open section has no peer-reviewed source and is offered only as a labelled heuristic (structure.md, Form menu). Tied by the research: no source exists. [unverified]
- Jam-band harmony has no source at all (harmony.md, Base rates). Tied by the research: no source exists. [unverified]

### Country
Spellings: country, country and western.
- No usable finding. The country corpus figures in harmony.md were read secondhand, and harmony.md's Pitfalls forbid citing a genre rate that was not read. [design]
- Not a country finding: the Nashville head-session practice and the Nashville Number System (arrangement.md) describe session practice and a chart system in one city; the research does not tie them to a style. [design]

## Not style findings

These mention a style word but are not findings about the style. Never offer them as style material. [design]

- The 70% agreement figure for the prosody model comes from English folksong data; it is a limit of the model, not advice about folk (melody.md, Limits). [verified]
- Prechorus incidence dipped in 1979 when disco was strong on the charts; this is a chart-history fact about prechoruses, not about disco (structure.md, Section jobs; Summach). [verified]
- The "amen" gloss for a plagal close is this plugin's plain-English label, not a gospel finding (harmony.md, Notation). [design]
- The "jam bloom" energy shape is this plugin's own shape name, not jam-band research (arrangement.md, Energy). [design]
