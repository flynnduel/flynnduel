# Build the plugin on verified craft lenses

The authorities agree on one thing that should shape the whole plugin: good songwriting craft is a set of **named mechanisms with predictable listener effects**, not a set of rules. A professional co-writer therefore has to name the mechanism, show options, and leave the choice to the writer. Pat Pattison's model is the backbone. It is now verified from his own pages and columns, and it is precise enough to automate: four structural elements, with line length counted in stressed syllables; a rhyme-stability gradient built on consonant families; four points of view with a pronoun test; three metaphor types; title spotlights and "recoloring"; and stress-to-beat prosody. Popular-music scholarship (Summach, Nobile, Stroud, de Clercq, Everett, Temperley, Spicer, Richards, Lavengood, Cobb, Hayes, Smith) adds corpus base rates and form models the plugin can use to rank options. Two examples: IV→I is the most common chord move in classic rock, yet V–I still ends more choruses, and a contrasting bridge is optional in modern pop. Liz Lerman's Critical Response Process, Marty Dodson's rewrite checks and Zollo's interviews with writers supply the critique etiquette and the process model. The evidence is uneven. Sheila Davis, Jimmy Webb's *Tunesmith*, Jack Perricone's *Great Songwriting Techniques*, Rikky Rooksby, Kostka & Payne and much of Bobby Owsinski could be read only as tables of contents, snippets or citations by others. So their specific rules have to be labelled "as reported" or left out. The offline toolkit is feasible at about **15 MB** with permissive licences, but rhyme classification, prosody scoring, ChordPro handling and capo logic all have to be written in-house. No library does them, and two common assumptions about licences and offline use turned out to be wrong.

A note on labels. Throughout this report, **verified** means the full primary text was read. **Secondhand** means the claim comes from a scholar's citation, a review, a summary, an unofficial copy or a search snippet. **Unverified** means no readable source confirmed it. The plugin's user-facing attributions should follow the same three tiers.

The practical consequence is a **lens system**. Every critique note names its basis: "Pattison: …", "Webb (strict rhyme): …", "Davis (as reported): …", "corpus: …". The writer can switch lenses on and off. Notes from a lens backed by secondhand evidence are labelled as such.

## Lyrics: Pattison's toolkit is verified enough to automate, Davis's is not

**Rhyme is a stability dial, not a pass/fail test.** In his 2021 American Songwriter Q&A, Pattison orders rhyme types by how far they "open the gate." Perfect rhyme closes it, and the remoter types create "uncertainty and longing." His examples run hat → hack/nap/sad/grab → rats → laugh → spot: perfect, then family, additive, assonance and consonance ([American Songwriter, Q&A on Rhyme](https://americansongwriter.com/q-and-a-on-rhyme-with-pat-pattison/)). He calls the belief that all rhyme must be perfect the "most onerous" misconception. He also says finding a rhyme is not a creative act, which makes rhyming-dictionary "worksheets" legitimate. He names cliché pairs: love/dove/above, fire/desire, hand/understand, eyes/realize ([same](https://americansongwriter.com/q-and-a-on-rhyme-with-pat-pattison/)), plus home/roam and home/alone. **Family rhyme** keeps the vowel and swaps the following consonant for a relative within one of three families: plosives, fricatives or nasals. A relative is either a *partner* (same place of articulation, opposite voicing: t/d, f/v) or a *companion* (same family, same voicing) ([American Songwriter, "Meeting the Family"](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-surviving-rhyme-meeting-the-family/)). **Correction:** the closest-relative order differs by family. For plosives the partner comes first. For fricatives, Pattison's worked example goes to the companions first (f→s, sh, ch) and then to the partner v. The earlier single rule, "voiced/unvoiced partners are closest," was wrong. He also teaches assonance through diphthong components, so long *i* hides an /ä/ and an /ē/ ([American Songwriter, "Knitting a Sonic Fabric"](https://americansongwriter.com/knitting-a-sonic-fabric/)).

**Structure has four "balls," and line length is counted in stresses.** A Berklee excerpt of *Lyric Form and Structure* names the four elements Pattison juggles: number of phrases, length of each, rhythm of each, and rhyme arrangement ([Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/lyric-elements-the-great-juggling-act/)). He measures length in stressed syllables. Unmatched lengths such as 3/3/5 "tell us to keep going" ([patpattison.com, Structure Creates Expectations](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations)). aabb stops motion at every couplet, abab is a stable four-line journey, and abba and xxaa stay open ([American Songwriter, Motion Creates E-Motion](https://americansongwriter.com/motion-creates-e-motion-in-songwriting/); [Rhyme Schemes: Same Song, Different Journeys](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-rhyme-schemes-same-song-different-journeys/)). Prosody is his one exception to "only tools, no rules." He traces the idea to Aristotle's unity, so it is not his coinage, and he assesses it by asking whether each song, verse and line is stable or unstable ([Berklee Online, Prosody](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/prosody-in-music-and-songwriting/)). Rhyme type is not one of the four balls, but he uses it for the same purpose. The plugin should model it as a labelled fifth dial.

**Titles go in spotlights, and verses must recolor them.** Pattison puts the title first, last, or both. He mines it with who/what/when/where/why/how. Every chorus line must stay "global" enough to absorb each verse's new angle ([patpattison.com, Developing Verse Ideas](https://www.patpattison.com/developing-verse-ideas)). Separate ideas belong in separate "systems." The I–you–we split for three verses is a useful guideline that he warns can harden into habit. "Second Verse Hell" is the stagnation that recoloring cures ([StrongWriter, Verse Development](https://strongwriter.com/verse-development-with-pat-pattison/)). The *middle* chorus line as a power position comes from Andrea Stolpe's write-up of his course, not from his own words, so it counts as a secondary spotlight ([Berklee Online, Writing from the Title](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/simple-tools-for-writing-from-the-title/)). **Productive repetition** turns a fragment into a different grammatical type, such as a question or a command, so the repeat adds meaning instead of filling space ([patpattison.com, Productive Repetition](https://www.patpattison.com/productive-repetition)).

**There are four points of view, not three.** **Correction:** Pattison's taxonomy is Direct Address (I to you), 2nd Person Narrative (you, no I), 1st Person Narrative (I and third persons, no you) and 3rd Person Narrative (neither) ([American Songwriter, 3rd Person Narrative](https://americansongwriter.com/the-point-of-point-of-view-3rd-person-narrative/)). The pronoun rules are explicit enough to run as code, provided quoted speech is excluded and generic "you" is flagged. His **POV check** is to try all four before finishing ([patpattison.com, POV](https://www.patpattison.com/pov)). A mixed POV has to beat a consistent version in an A/B test, and "most of the time, consistency will win out" ([American Songwriter, Mixing Point of View](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-mixing-point-of-view/)).

**Metaphor has three types, and simile behaves differently.** Pattison defines metaphor as a collision that must be literally false. Its types are expressed identity (x is y, the y of x, x's y), qualifying metaphor (adjective–noun *or adverb–verb* friction; correcting the earlier "adjective metaphor") and verbal metaphor (a verb clashing with its subject or object). Simile does not transfer focus to the second term, so it suits one-off comparisons, while a metaphor should be sustained. His "Playing in Keys" drill collides words "diatonic" to a key idea, and he finds noun×verb lists most productive ([patpattison.com, Making Metaphors](https://www.patpattison.com/pat-s-lyric-tips)). Object writing opens *Writing Better Lyrics* ([archive.org TOC](https://archive.org/details/writingbetterlyr0000patt)). Its seven senses, including organic and kinesthetic, come only from a Writer's Digest snippet and Berklee material, and the ten-minute timing is secondhand ([iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/object-writing-your-secret-to-vivid-song-lyrics)). **Misattributions to correct:** destination writing and "toggling" are Andrea Stolpe's ([Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/songwriting-tips-try-destination-writing/)). The co-writing "No-Free Zone" comes from Nashville writer **Stan** Webb, not Jimmy Webb ([patpattison.com, Co-Writing](https://www.patpattison.com/co-writing)).

**Webb's strict-rhyme stance is verified and directly contradicts Pattison.** On Fresh Air, Webb called the line/time pairing in "Wichita Lineman" a false rhyme he would have fixed. He noticed it only while analysing his songs for *Tunesmith* ([NPR transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/128696937)). He later changed "golden sails across the sky" to "golden sail across the skies" to get a perfect rhyme with "flies" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/128544514)). Line/time differs only in its final nasal, so Pattison would class it as a "pretty strong" family rhyme. This is the cleanest documented disagreement among the authorities, and it justifies a rhyme-lens toggle: Pattison by default, Webb strict mode as an opt-in that lists every non-perfect rhyme and offers inflection or plural fixes before abandoning an image. Webb's rhyming-dictionary reviews, his warnings against eye rhymes and his reported favourite dictionary (Clement Wood's) remain secondhand ([Gearspace](https://gearspace.com/board/songwriting/739236-using-rhymes-dictionary-cheating-2.html)).

**Davis can be cited by title, not by rule.** Her three verified books are *The Craft of Lyric Writing* (1985), *Successful Lyric Writing* (1988) and *The Songwriter's Idea Book* (1992) ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/craftoflyricwrit0000davi)). **Correction:** "Writing Hit Lyrics" does not appear under her name in the catalogue, and Wikipedia's "Sheila Davis" article is about a different person. Her consistency principles reach us only through unofficial copies: a single time frame, a consistent viewpoint and an unchanging setting; particularizing rather than generalizing; and titles identifiable after one hearing ([silo.pub copy](https://silo.pub/successful-lyric-writing-a-step-by-step-course-amp-workbook.html)). Her figures-of-speech catalogue is unverified. Pattison's verified POV advice backs the same consistency defaults, so the plugin should cite Pattison for those checks and treat Davis as corroboration "as reported."

**Implications for the plugin.** The lyric engine should run attributed checks, each returning *location → observation → lens → question*. The rhyme checks classify every end-rhyme pair, report a stability profile per section, flag cliché pairs and offer family candidates in partner/companion order. The structure checks cover phrase count, stresses per line, matched or unmatched lengths and the motion each scheme predicts. The title checks cover spotlight position and recoloring after each verse. The POV checks run the four-way classifier and offer an A/B rewrite in one POV. A tense, time and setting drift check carries Davis's defaults with a "was this deliberate?" framing. An imagery check suggests moving the strongest image to line 1. A figure classifier flags dead metaphors and unsustained ones. The plugin co-writes lyrics, so it may draft lines, but it should do so as Pattison does in his master classes: several variants side by side (aabb, abab, xxaa and abba versions of the same verse; the same verse in all four POVs), with the writer choosing by feel. The data it needs is an ARPAbet consonant-family table that stores both relations; CMUdict stress plus singer overrides for sung pronunciation; a vowel inventory with diphthong decomposition; and a seeded cliché list. Still to verify against the books: Pattison's own definitions of additive, subtractive and partial rhyme (secondary sources disagree on "subtractive"); the "boxes" mechanics; Davis's checklist; and Webb's lyric chapter "It's only words."

## Structure and hooks: the first chorus line must already be different

**Pattison's form-follows-function idea is really two principles.** *Prosody* means designing form from what it must do. *Contrast* means that sections with the same function, such as verses, share a form, while a section with a new function changes the rhyme scheme, phrase lengths, phrase count or phrase rhythms. The contrast has to land at the new section's first phrase ([StrongWriter, Form Follows Function](https://strongwriter.com/building-the-perfect-beast-form-follows-function/)). His "Southern Comfort" case shows the failure: verse and chorus both in Common Meter, with no contrast, so a late bridge arrives after listeners have wandered off. His "Valentine" analysis shows asymmetry carrying meaning. An interrupted three-line bridge snaps back to verse 1 and leaves the character stuck ([patpattison.com](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations)). The plugin can test this directly by comparing the chorus's first line with the verse norm on stress count, rhythm, rhyme sound and length. A match means the contrast arrives late or not at all.

**The form catalogue rests on verified scholarship.** Summach's 700-song Billboard corpus (1955–89) shows strophic, AABA and verse-chorus forms dominating the charts. Prechoruses appear in about a third of 1965–69 verse-chorus hits and about 60% by the late 1980s, with dips around 1970 (funk, relaxed singer-songwriter styles) and 1979 (disco) ([Summach, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)). Temperley's default verse-chorus layout, quoted by Hudson, is two verse-chorus pairs, then an instrumental or bridge, then a final pair or chorus. Virtually all verse-chorus songs are compound AABA, usually with an abridged final A, hence Hudson's label "AABA′" ([Hudson, MTO 27.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.1/mto.21.27.1.hudson.html)). Open Music Theory defines each section's job ([OMT, Verse-Chorus Form](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/verse-chorus-form/); [AABA and Strophic](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/aaba-form/)). Verses are lyric-variant. Choruses carry the title with denser texture, backing vocals or higher register. The bridge appears once, replacing a verse or prechorus. A "climb" is a one-phrase prechorus function at the end of a verse. An AABA bridge withholds A and often ends on V. Nobile's telos principle maps verse, prechorus and chorus onto **initiation, buildup and arrival**. With a prechorus, the goal is the chorus downbeat; without one, it is the chorus end. After 2010, anti-telos choruses withhold the peak for a drop ([Nobile, MTO 28.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.3/mto.22.28.3.nobile.html)). Stroud's 1,335-song Top-40 corpus separates *codetta* postchoruses, which add closure after a climactic chorus, from *anthem* postchoruses, which are the climax after a building chorus ([Stroud, MTO 28.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.2/mto.22.28.2.stroud.html)). de Clercq argues that section labels should be scored against weighted attributes, with "blends" such as prechorus/chorus allowed ([de Clercq, MTO 23.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.de_clercq.html)). Osborn defines through-composed rock as having no recapitulated section ([Osborn, MTO 17.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.osborn.html)). His later "terminal climax" types are secondhand. Everett's srdc labels (statement, restatement, departure, conclusion) are verified through Summach and OMT.

**Corrections.** "Energy gain" is OMT's summary term. Summach's own words are momentum and teleology. Summach's dating of the prechorus is internally inconsistent: it "started to appear" in the early 1960s, the abstract says mid-1960s, and consistent use dates from the mid-to-late 1960s. The "listen closely" gloss on Nobile's continuation chorus is unverified and should be dropped; his telos-chorus "rock out" gloss is verified ([Barna review, MTO 28.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html)).

**Hooks are better sourced for placement than for taxonomy.** Blume's verified table of contents includes "making it obvious where the title goes without the lyric," signature licks, symmetrical phrases, nonsense syllables, modulations and the "dreaded second verse" ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/6stepstosongwrit0000blum_i5d1)). That implies a melodic title test: the title line should stand out as a peak, a long note or a rhythmic break. Stroud's postchorus vocables count as a second hook type. Blume's numbered hook taxonomy and his BMI column details are secondhand ([BMI mirror](https://www.wearemusicnation.ae/en/knowledge-hub/34/hooks:-how-many-are-enough)). The commercial timing data is secondhand too. Léveillé Gauvin's 303 top-10 singles (1986–2015) reportedly show intros shrinking from about 20 to about 5 seconds, earlier titles and faster tempos, with no trend in lyrical self-focus ([Ohio State release](https://news.osu.edu/has-music-streaming-killed-the-instrumental-intro/)). Ralph Murphy's title-within-60-seconds finding is summary-only ([NSAI blog](https://www.nashvillesongwriters.com/node/344)). Stroud verifies chorus-first openings as a recent option. Hip-hop's verse-hook and strophic/cypher paradigms are supported by OMT (verified) and by Duinker's corpus (secondhand) ([Duinker, Current Musicology](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/currentmusicology/article/download/7177/3992/14952)). Jam-band form has no peer-reviewed source.

**Implications.** The plugin should offer a form menu rather than a template: strophic or verse-refrain; AABA; simple verse-chorus; contrasting verse-chorus; verse-prechorus-chorus-postchorus; compound AABA′; verse-hook; drop forms; terminal climax; and a head-plus-open-section "jam" form, with that last one labelled heuristic. Its structure checks should cover function match, Common-Meter monotony, an expectation audit, prechorus buildup (fragmentation, faster harmonic rhythm, rising register, an open ending), bridge contrast, climax location, and a symmetry audit that suggests one *meaningful* deviation. Timing norms appear only when the writer declares a commercial goal, and they are labelled as market heuristics. Still unread: Perricone's prechorus and bridge chapters, Blume's checklists, and the Léveillé Gauvin paper itself.

## Melody and prosody: ranked constraints make stress-checking feasible, but only as scoring

**Perricone's verified checks are about singability, not style.** In the *Melody in Songwriting* excerpt, a phrase ends by resting, holding or resolving and is tied to breath. Most vocal melody is conjunct, while leaps give a memorable profile. He calls singing your own melody "absolutely essential" and sets three checks: how many leaps, whether there is room to breathe, and whether the range is too wide or changes too fast *within a section* ([Berklee Online excerpt](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/conjunct-disjunct-melody-basic-definitions/)). His analysis of his own song "It Doesn't Get Any Better Than This" adds four things. A motive unfolds in phrase lengths of 1, 1, 2, 4 and 4 bars, repeated over a re-harmonized chord and then extended. The title melody rises to match a positive lyric. An unstable scale degree 4 resolves to 3 at the return. The bridge contrasts on five levers: it starts on IV, uses plainer rhythm and contour and more uniform 2+2+4 phrasing, and sits in the middle of the range against an A section spanning an octave and a sixth ([Berklee Today, 2008](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2355531)). **Correction:** the verifiable definition of front-heavy and back-heavy phrases is Pattison's. Phrases starting on or picking up to the downbeat feel stable; phrases starting after it feel in motion ([patpattison.com, Art of Phrasing](https://www.patpattison.com/art-of-phrasing)). Perricone uses the same terms in his tables of contents, but his definition was not read. His "octave and a fifth" range guideline exists only in unofficial copies of *Great Songwriting Techniques*, and his own octave-and-a-sixth A section shows it is not a cap.

**Pattison's prosody rules form a three-tier stress lexicon.** Match stressed syllables to stressed positions. A mismatch breaks no rule but sounds unnatural and deflates the line. Polysyllables carry a fixed dictionary stress. Monosyllables take stress from their job: content words "work hard," while function words direct traffic. Polysyllabic prepositions such as "before" belong on medium positions ([patpattison.com, Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match)). **Correction:** "traffic signs, not cargo" is a third-party paraphrase, not his wording. The 4/4 strong-weak hierarchy repeats at every subdivision, and an anticipation strengthens the beat it anticipates. His Berklee syllabus adds four other things that create stressed positions: isolation, a preceding rest, shorter notes before, and anticipation ([Berklee course page](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/lyric-writing-writing-lyrics-to-music)). The melody's phrase "roadmap" has to match the lyric's idea breaks. If it doesn't, the writer can change the lyric, change the music, or repeat a word across the break ([patpattison.com, Lyric and Melodic Phrases](https://www.patpattison.com/lyric-and-melodic-phrases)). His critique of "Unconditionally" works as a ready-made test suite: "the" and "did" on downbeats, the secondary stresses of "insecurities" on strong beats, "-ly" on a downbeat ([patpattison.com, Setting Words to Music](https://www.patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music)).

**Textsetting research supplies the scoring engine and its limits.** Bruce Hayes's optimality-theory grammar uses ten ranked constraints. Among them, *LAPSE forbids three empty positions in a row, and a stressed syllable is "far" better on a medium position than a weak one. The grammar matched native speakers' favourite setting **70.6%** of the time, against **23.1%** for Halle & Lerdahl's algorithm, and Hayes himself calls that "not good enough." A 16-position line has at most about **13,000** possible settings, so exhaustive search is cheap ([Hayes 2005 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf)). Good settings routinely sacrifice a stress match to avoid a lapse. Hayes & Kaun add four-level stress, prosodic-phrase effects and duration matching, using a folk corpus ([Hayes & Kaun 1996](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf)). Smith's 2024 "prosodic dissonance" article makes three points. Phrasal stress is the only way to explain mismatches on monosyllables. Syncopation (text against the meter) is different from dissonance (text against the melody's own durational and pitch accents): Billie Eilish's "bad guy" anticipates constantly without sounding dissonant. And dissonances cluster in verses more than choruses. She also cautions against strict numeric scoring ([Smith, MTO 30.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.2/mto.24.30.2.smith.html)). CMUdict gives citation stress only. It has no phrasal stress and no function-word demotion, which matches Pattison's observation that the dictionary leaves one-syllable words unmarked.

**Webb contributes nothing verifiable on melody.** The *Tunesmith* melody chapter ("Give us a tune then, Jim") was unreadable, and no Webb statement on range was found. Interviews in which he says interesting chords compel interesting melodies were not re-read ([American Songwriter](https://americansongwriter.com/melody-tips-for-songwriters-paul-simon-leonard-cohen-dave-brubeck/)). Use him only as a "change what's under it" harmony prompt. **Key choice** rests on Wikipedia's definition of tessitura as the "heart" of the range rather than its extremes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessitura)). The duration-weighted ranking method below is the research team's own inference, and the capo advice comes from non-primary sources.

**Implications.** The plugin coaches melody and never writes or "corrects" notes. Its questions draw on Perricone's three singability checks, phrase-ending stability (1/3/5 versus 2/4/6/7), contour against meaning, motif development, Pattison's phrase placement and roadmaps, and the five-lever bridge contrast. The prosody checker combines a metric-strength model (beat level plus anticipation, duration, isolation and pitch peak), a three-tier stress lexicon and soft, ranked flags. The strongest flag is a lexical stress inversion inside a word. It should score the writer's setting against the best enumerated alternative and say plainly that the model agrees with listeners about 70% of the time, on folk data. Smith's distinction means it must not flag a syncopation that the melody itself accents. The writer can always mark contrastive focus. Flags in the chorus weigh more than flags in verses. Key fitting collects comfortable low, comfortable high and a best-tone ceiling, then ranks keys and capo positions by tessitura per section.

## Harmony: IV→I rules the loop, but V–I still ends the chorus

**Everett's taxonomy is the right first classifier.** His Table 1 has six numbered systems with nine sub-types, ordered by how far each departs from common practice. 1a and 1b are common-practice major and minor. 2 is diatonic modal. 3a is major with modal mixture. 3b covers progressive structures and modulation, including the truck-driver's lift. 4 is blues-based. 5 is the triad-doubled or power-chord minor-pentatonic system unique to rock. 6a and 6b are chromatic and early-metal ([Everett, Table 1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.4/w_everett_table1.html)). He insists that ♭VII–IV–I, the "double-plagal cadence," is a chromatic descending-fourths function. It is often wrongly given a modal basis, and extended fourth chains such as "Hey Joe" imply no mode at all. He locates blues structure in the backing, not the vocal ([Everett, MTO 10.4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.4/mto.04.10.4.w_everett.html)). **Correction:** his footnote dates the term to *The Beatles as Musicians* (2000), not 2008. He also warns that a truck-driver's modulation can be an arranger salvaging a weak idea.

**Corpus base rates should rank the plugin's options.** **Correction:** the de Clercq–Temperley corpus has **99** songs, not 100. Its root shares are I 32.8%, IV 22.6%, V 16.3%, ♭VII 8.1%, VI 7.2% and ♭VI 4.0%. IV→I (1,162) is the single most common transition, and V→IV (392) runs close to IV→V (514) ([Temperley, MTO 17.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.1/mto.11.17.1.temperley.html)). **Correction:** at section-ending chorus cadences, **V is still the most common pre-tonic chord (32% of 200 songs) and IV second (18.5%)**, so the plugin must not over-promote plagal endings. Only 2 of 37 plagal cadences use minor iv. Temperley names teachable devices: the plagal stop cadence ("Barracuda"), the grand plagal near a song's end, the deceptive IV, and the tonicized IV. **Correction:** Doll's "rogue dominant" is a dominant with lowered 7̂ (♭VII, v), not IV. Doll ties function to scale-degree content: 6̂ moves away, 7̂ and 2̂ pull home ([de Clercq review of Doll, MTO 24.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.18.24.1/mto.18.24.1.declercq.html)). Spicer adds fragile, emergent and absent tonics, the soul dominant (IV over the 5 in the bass) and Tagg's shuttles and loops. **Correction:** "Last Friday Night" is not among his examples ([Spicer, MTO 23.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.2/mto.17.23.2.spicer.html)). Richards shows that Axis-loop tonality depends on melodic emphasis and a major-mode listener bias. The G-form appears in only 10 of 2,715 Billboard songs, and letter labels suit truly ambiguous loops ([Richards, MTO 23.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.richards.html)). OMT reports that ♭VI–♭VII–i has largely replaced iv–V–i in recent minor-key pop, and that pop chords are almost always in root position ([OMT, pop/rock harmony](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony.html); [minor](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-minor.html)). Nobile keeps T–PD–D–T as a section-level "functional circuit," with initiating verses that need the chorus to close ([Barna, MTO 28.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html)).

**Kostka & Payne and Rooksby are structural guides, not quotable sources.** The research did not read any Kostka & Payne text. Its keep/drop list therefore rests on rock-specific evidence. Keep the function vocabulary, cadence names, secondary dominants and mode mixture. Drop as rules "V must go to I," SATB voice-leading bans, "melody notes must fit the chord" (melodic–harmonic divorce is idiomatic) and harmonic minor as the default. Rooksby's *Songwriting Sourcebook* table of contents is verified and works as a coaching ladder: three-chord songs, four, five and six, flat-degree chords, "reverse polarity," majors and minors, minor keys, chord types, key changes ([archive.org](https://archive.org/metadata/songwritingsourc0000rook)). His definition of reverse polarity (swapping a chord's major/minor quality) is secondhand. The country-corpus figures are secondhand too: about 82% I/IV/V and 98% major tonics ([de Clercq SMT text](https://www.midside.com/presentations/declercq_2021_smt_miig_text.pdf)). Jam-band harmony has no source at all.

**Implications.** The plugin should classify each section by Everett sub-type. It labels chords with three notations at once: a Roman numeral with case for quality, a Nashville number and the chord symbol. Internally it stores a quality-free root layer for schema matching. It offers dual readings with confidence scores for ambiguous loops. It coaches with menus, and each option says why it works, how it feels and how common it is in the corpus. Examples: change the chorus's first chord or its harmonic rhythm; withhold I until the chorus; end on V–I or IV–I; save a deceptive IV for the last chorus; use a key lift sparingly. The pitfalls to code against are over-claiming modes from ♭VII, forcing common-practice rules on Types 4–6, flagging melodic–harmonic divorce as an error, and single-answer prescriptions.

## Guitar: Greene's V-System labels band voicings, and tunings need Joni codes

**The V-System is now verified and is straightforward to implement.** Hober's tedgreene.com PDFs show that it classifies **four-note chords with four distinct pitches** by spacing, not by string set, into 14 groups, in standard tuning only. Method 1, the one Greene considered most important, labels the voices B-T-A-S, walks the chord tones in ascending chromatic order, and looks the resulting formula up in a master table. In the source's example, first-inversion Emaj7 on strings 4-3-2-1 is ABST, which is V-2 ([Hober, Method 1](https://tedgreene.com/images/lessons/v_system/03_Method1_HowToRecognize.pdf)). String transfer, transposition, systematic inversion and spacing-preserving quality changes all keep a chord in its group. Greene preferred "drop alto" to "drop 2," counted 43 four-note qualities, treated pairs such as m7 and 6 as homonyms, and wrote add9 as "/9" ([Hober, Introduction](https://tedgreene.com/images/lessons/v_system/02_V-System_Introduction.pdf); [Conversion](https://tedgreene.com/images/lessons/v_system/20_Conversion_.pdf)). By the research team's own derivation from close position, jazz drop-2, drop-3 and drop-2&4 map to V-2, V-4 and V-5. The system excludes doublings and alternate tunings and treats open strings with caution. That makes it a tool for **band-context upper-string voicings**, not six-string open strums.

**Tunings and capos are well documented on mechanics, weak on attribution.** Wikipedia verifies the seed tunings (Drop D, DADGAD, Open G, D, E and C) and Joni Mitchell's notation: a bass letter followed by fret offsets between adjacent strings, so standard tuning is E55545 ([Wikipedia, Guitar tunings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar_tunings); [List of tunings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_guitar_tunings)). Tunings with the same code are transpositions of each other. Nick Drake's use of tunings to make cluster chords is verified ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Drake)). His song-by-song tunings are contested: sources for "Pink Moon" give CGCFCE with capo 2, DADGDF# and Berklee's open D. A capo works like retuning every string up by the same amount, and only open notes change. It brightens the timbre, and partial capos can clamp any of 63 string combinations ([Wikipedia, Capo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capo_(musical_device)); [Partial capo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_capo)). **Correction:** no source supports a fret-7 ceiling. Present it as a soft heuristic. ChordPro keeps `{key}` as the *shape* key under `{capo}` ([ChordPro docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ChordPro/chordpro/master/docs/content/Directives-capo.md)). Berklee verifies the "fret two strings, let the rest drone" approach to open-tuning shapes ([Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/acoustic-guitar-techniques-to-move-past-beginner-level/)). **Rooksby's guitar advice is entirely unverified.** Only the *Chord Master* contents are confirmed, including a 37-entry chord matrix that makes a good parser-coverage checklist ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/chordmasterhowto0000rook)).

**Feel can be described in counts and words, with verified vocabulary.** The terms: counting "1 e & a" ([Wikipedia, Counting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counting_(music))); patterns made by subtracting strokes from a straight-eighth base, with funk keeping a steady 16th hand ([Wikipedia, Strum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strum)); swing labelled soft, triplet, hard or dotted ([Wikipedia, Swing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_(jazz_performance_style))); Travis picking as alternating bass under a syncopated melody; and Berklee's sul tasto ("sweeter and rounder") touch vocabulary. **Data correction:** the GuitarSet repository shows no licence, so the earlier "MIT" claim is unconfirmed. The CC BY 4.0 notice found applies to the paper. chords-db (MIT; 828 chords and 3,283 positions) is safe to bundle ([chords-db LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tombatossals/chords-db/master/LICENSE)).

**Implications.** Guitar is the writer's main instrument, so the plugin's guitar role is to **read, verify and describe** what the writer plays. It is not there to compose parts. It takes shapes as `x32010`, ChordPro `{define}` or chords-db JSON. It computes sounding pitches from tuning plus capo, checks them against the chord name and returns all candidates on ambiguity (Am7 versus C6). It labels four-note voicings by V-group and computes Joni codes. It suggests capo positions and shape keys ranked by open-string count, and gives a concert-pitch chart for the band. When it offers "same chord, new colour" moves such as string transfer or drop-alto conversion, or second-guitar register contrasts, it presents them as a menu of options grounded in the writer's existing chord.

## Band arrangement: four elements at once, with parts left to the players

**Owsinski's vocabulary now has scholarly backing; his numbers still rest on snippets.** Lavengood's peer-reviewed article confirms that *The Mixing Engineer's Handbook* names five arrangement elements: foundation, pad, rhythm, lead and fills. She maps them onto Moore's four functional layers (explicit beat, functional bass, harmonic filler, melodic) plus her own optional **novelty layer**. That layer carries pitch content but a non-melodic function, works best sparingly in intros, outros and interludes, and resists blending ([Lavengood, MTO 26.3](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.lavengood.pdf)). Owsinski's limit of "usually four" simultaneous elements, rarely five, with same-rhythm instruments counting as one, is known only from blog snippets, and his posts vary between four and five ([Owsinski blog](https://bobbyowsinskiblog.com/song-arrangement-elements/)). His "Tension and Release" section is unread, so the plugin can use the general idea but not his content. "Change something every 4 bars" is not his; it comes from Deviant Noise ([Deviant Noise](https://deviantnoise.net/education/music-production/arrangement/)). Rooksby's *Arranging Songs* is readable only as a chapter list, which runs guitar first and then keys, bass and drums.

**Energy trajectories have a scholarly basis, but bridges are optional.** Cobb's 2025 article situates section-by-section "sonic energy" in Butler, Nobile (initiation, buildup, arrival) and Osborn (riser, drop). It cites Osborn's finding that Top-40 EDM songs "almost never" have a viable contrasting bridge, and it studies late "groove switches" as an alternative source of contrast. It also quotes Osborn on balancing sonic contrast against thematic memorability ([Cobb, MTO 31.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.25.31.2/mto.25.31.2.cobb.html)). Specific 1–5 or percentage energy scales are practitioner conventions. Carter's "rebuild" (a breakdown at the bridge position, then layers returning toward the climax) is known only from his abstract ([Carter, Integral](https://theory.esm.rochester.edu/integral/?p=9827)). Berklee's core arranging course frames arranging as shaping a song's "emotional contour" through dynamics, articulation and texture ([Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/arranging-1-rhythm-section)).

**The Nashville head session is the model for "guidance, not parts."** Wikipedia verifies three things ([Wikipedia, Nashville Number System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System)). First, Neal Matthews Jr. developed the Number System in the late 1950s, and Charlie McCoy adapted it for rhythm sections in the early 1960s. **Correction:** Chas Williams is its historian and author (11th ed., 2023), not its originator. Second, the symbols are explicitly not standardized. Third, Nashville and Southern sessions built arrangements collectively by ear, with charts giving the framework and players improvising voicings, so musicians could rely on their ears rather than a written arrangement. That is exactly the stance the plugin needs. Spoken cue examples such as "kick it off from the 5" are verified. The rest of a cue vocabulary is general usage. Demo advice such as "leave about 20 percent for the studio" is a snippet ([Spotify for Artists](https://artists.spotify.com/en/blog/the-dos-and-donts-of-pre-production-demoing)). No authoritative genre-by-genre role table exists, so idiom sketches are synthesis.

**Implications.** The plugin should produce a one-page road map. Each section records its function (initiation, buildup, arrival, contrast or ending), an energy level, who is in by role, who is waiting, feel and dynamics, hits, pushes and holds, the transition, an element count against the ceiling of four, and an explicit "left to the players" field. Its critique pass checks for more than four elements, rhythm duplicated across guitar, keys and bass, register clashes with the vocal, a first chorus already at peak, missing contrast (offering a groove or texture switch when there is no bridge), fills outside the vocal gaps, unmarked transitions, open-ended solos and an undecided ending. An optional NNS chart must carry a symbol legend. The plugin never outputs notation, tab or written parts.

## Critique and revision: Lerman's permission gate governs every taste note

**The Critical Response Process supplies the protocol.** Lerman's official page verifies four steps ([Liz Lerman, CRP](https://lizlerman.com/critical-response-process/)). Step 1 is statements of meaning. Step 2 is questions from the artist, and **correcting the earlier notes, responders may give opinions in direct answer to those questions.** Step 3 is neutral questions with no opinion couched in them. Step 4 is permissioned opinions ("I have an opinion about ___, would you like to hear it?"), and the artist may decline. Optional steps include next steps and "working the work." So the plugin can answer "is the bridge weak?" directly, and it gates only *unsolicited* taste.

**Pattison's workshops show diagnose-and-demonstrate.** His seminar pages say critique "focuses on the strengths" with next-step suggestions. Master classes target prosody between intent and structure, rhyme, rhythm, POV and phrasing ([patpattison.com, Seminars](https://www.patpattison.com/seminars)). In the Bellbird Hill write-up, the class swapped lines so an image came first, noted that aabb "stops motion four times," and tried abab, xxaa and abba side by side so the writer could choose by feel ([patpattison.com](https://www.patpattison.com/copy-of-pov-verse-development)). Marty Dodson's four verified rewrite checks are pronouns on the loose, time jumpers, missing backstory and twin verses. He also describes a creator/editor split: the creator holds about 90% of the power while drafting and the editor holds the red pen afterwards ([American Songwriter, Rewriting Lyrics](https://americansongwriter.com/rewriting-lyrics-behind-the-rhymes-songwriter-u/)). Stolpe's verified feedback questions work as a listener-simulation pass. Could you hear the words? Could you tell where the chorus started? Can you still sing any part? Does the music's mood match the words? ([Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/how-to-get-better-feedback-on-your-songs/)).

**Revision depth is the writer's call.** Zollo's verified revision column records several positions. Some writers hold to perfect rhyme and strict meter and others value expression, and "that determination is up to you." Paul Simon removes "the irritant." Cohen cut polished verses to protect a song's tone. Randy Newman keeps the critic smaller than the creator while drafting. Jackson Browne loosened his perfect-rhyme rule under Warren Zevon's influence ([American Songwriter, The Art of Revision](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-art-of-revision/)). Blume, in a verified interview, calls assuming your first melody is the strongest "just arrogance," advises workshopping a work tape before paying for a demo, and frames songs as communication. His interviewer disputes his title-in-the-chorus rule with counterexamples such as "The Gambler" ([American Songwriter, Blume](https://americansongwriter.com/jason-blume-offers-steps-to-success/)). That makes title placement and Murphy's timing data **market heuristics**, not craft laws. NSAI's evaluation criteria could not be read, and the "encourage, equip and empower" framing is summary-only ([NSAI](https://www.nashvillesongwriters.com/node/5861)). Webb's rewriting rules and Davis's checklist are unverified. AI-sounding tells (neon, echoes, "we rise above") are summary-only commentary ([JG BeatsLab](https://www.jgbeatslab.com/ai-music-lab-blog/your-lyrics-are-the-tell)). The safe rule is to flag stock language as a craft issue and never accuse the writer.

**Implications.** The `/critique` order should be: meaning and one to three strengths; the writer's own questions answered directly; neutral intent questions; then about three ranked notes. Song-level issues (idea, title, development) come before section-level issues (clarity, prosody, melody) and line polish. Every note should state location, observation, mechanism, listener effect, basis and options, and offer two to four variants rather than an imposed rewrite. Taste notes need permission. A "kept by choice" register records the writer's rejections so the plugin never re-argues them. A craft note names a mechanism and its effect. A taste note rests on a convention the writer may reasonably reject. `/finalcheck` should end with an "irritant scan" and hand-off. The writer, never the plugin, declares the song finished, and writing done is a separate milestone from demo done.

## Songwriting process: separate receiving from editing, and bank everything

**Zollo's interviews show a shared "receive, then edit" stance.** These accounts are verified on American Songwriter. Dylan wants an environment for accepting unconscious material and "taking it down" ([Zollo Blog, Dylan](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-bob-dylan/)). Simon tries to discover a song rather than plot it, and sees his job as the editor who builds set-up halves for payoff lines ([Zollo Blog, Simon](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-paul-simon/)). **Correction:** Simon's usual method was to write on guitar and then bring the song to musicians. *Graceland* reversed that for about twenty years ([American Songwriter, Simon](https://americansongwriter.com/interview-paul-simon-discusses-songwriter-and-songwriting/)). Carole King beats block by not worrying, walking away and coming back. She and Goffin wrote about 150 "bad songs" before "Will You Love Me Tomorrow?" ([Zollo Blog, King](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-carole-king/)). Townes Van Zandt kept songs bare, stored lines while driving, edited "by the word" down to a single added "s," and finished "Pancho and Lefty" the day after he first played it ([Zollo Blog, Townes](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-townes-van-zandt/)). Cohen said he had to work as hard as any laborer ([Zollo, Cohen memorial](https://americansongwriter.com/?p=176756)). The draft count for "Hallelujah" is a range, 80 to 180 verses, of which he claimed 150 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallelujah_(Leonard_Cohen_song))). Mitchell, Newman and Young could not be verified, so the plugin should not cite them.

**Tweedy, Hunter and Webb add practice, collaboration and commission models.** American Songwriter verifies that Tweedy writes songs every day. It also quotes his book: inspiration "has to be invited," a 5–10 minute freewrite toward a finished song, physical movement, sleeping on a half-finished idea, and roughly one song in five meaning a lot ([American Songwriter, Tweedy tips](https://americansongwriter.com/how-to-write-one-song-jeff-tweedy-book-interview/)). His cut-ups, word ladders and three-part daily checklist are secondhand ([No Depression](https://nodepression.org/the-reading-room-jeff-tweedy-offers-insight-and-encouragement-in-how-to-write-one-song/)). Robert Hunter described a two-way exchange with Garcia: sometimes lyrics set to music, sometimes words written to finished changes, best when "starter ideas" were batted back and forth ([NPR transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/764237225)). **Correction:** Webb's "hour and 45 minutes or two hours" describes one commissioned song, "Wichita Lineman," which he sent off believing it unfinished. It is not a routine ([NPR transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-6010858)). **Correction:** no source shows Steve Seskin advocating title-first writing. Title-first craft is documented by Stolpe ([Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/?p=3032)). No source documents how professionals name files or number drafts. Hunter's formal band membership as non-performing lyricist is verified ([Wikipedia, Grateful Dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grateful_Dead)). The Nashville equal-split default is secondhand ([Cliff Goldmacher](https://www.cliffgoldmacher.com/a-discussion-about-co-writing-percentages/)).

**Implications.** The plugin should offer several doors into one song record: riff or groove, guitar song, lyric, music changes, title (Stolpe's flow), brief or commission (a constraint plus a timer), banked fragment, and co-write exchange. Each door reorders the next steps. Generation modes show no critique, and an explicit editor mode does, following Simon, Dodson and Newman. A quick "store it" bank holds typed fragments. Versions are snapshots plus a cut-verse pool, so a version is a chosen subset of verses (the Cohen/Cale pattern), with a "shipped but unfinished" flag (Webb). Unsticking tools that rest on verified sources: Tweedy's timer, movement and sleep-on-it prompts; King's guilt-free walk-away; Townes's focused-session mode. The secondhand ones should be labelled as such. A split sheet supports roles and arbitrary percentages, defaults to equal, and allows a non-performing lyricist as a full member.

## Offline toolkit: a lean 15 MB stack beats the 584 MB kitchen sink

**Two licence assumptions were wrong, and one offline assumption broke.** Hands-on testing found the following. `pronouncing` 0.3.0 is MIT, but it is only a thin wrapper over the `cmudict` PyPI package, which is **GPL-3.0-or-later**. Only the CMU data itself (126,052 entries, stress-marked) carries a BSD-style licence ([PyPI cmudict](https://pypi.org/project/cmudict/); [PyPI pronouncing](https://pypi.org/project/pronouncing/)). Out-of-vocabulary words return nothing. The usual fallback, g2p_en 2.1.0 (2019, unmaintained), **downloads NLTK data at first import**, which breaks the no-network rule ([PyPI g2p-en](https://pypi.org/project/g2p-en/)). wordfreq works offline but weighs 58 MB. Its data is frozen: the author announced in 2024 that it will not be updated, citing generative-AI pollution of web text. It is also **CC BY-SA 4.0** ([wordfreq SUNSET.md](https://github.com/rspeer/wordfreq/blob/master/SUNSET.md)). `pronouncing.rhymes()` handles only perfect rhyme and returns junk proper names. The testing found no permissively licensed library that classifies slant rhyme or Pattison's families.

**For chords, music21 is the most capable library and pychord is the practical core.** music21 10.5.2 (BSD-3) parsed every test chord, gave mode-aware Roman numerals (F as ♭VII in G major) and detected keys. It needs "B-" for B-flat, though; "Bb" raises an error. It also costs about 100 MB plus matplotlib and numpy ([PyPI music21](https://pypi.org/project/music21/)). pytheory 0.57.13 failed on Cadd9, G/B, Dsus4, C6 and E7#9, spells Bb as A#, and pulls in scipy and sounddevice ([PyPI pytheory](https://pypi.org/project/pytheory/)). pychord 1.4.3 (MIT, 64 KB) parsed everything but transposed G/B to "A/Db" ([PyPI pychord](https://pypi.org/project/pychord/)). reportlab 5.1.0 (BSD) handled chord-over-lyric layout, while fpdf2 is LGPL and rejects em dashes in its core fonts ([PyPI reportlab](https://pypi.org/project/reportlab/); [PyPI fpdf2](https://pypi.org/project/fpdf2/)). mido 1.3.3 (MIT, no dependencies) wrote valid MIDI ([PyPI mido](https://pypi.org/project/mido/)). The ChordPro reference implementation is Perl, version 6.101.0 under the Artistic License 2.0. No maintained Python parser exists ([ChordPro README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ChordPro/chordpro/master/README.md)).

| Purpose | Choice | Licence | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pronunciation | vendored `cmudict.dict` data | CMU BSD-style | avoids the GPL wrapper; ~50 lines of lookup code |
| Chord parsing | pychord 1.4.3 | MIT | add a key-aware enharmonic fix |
| Theory (optional) | music21 10.5.2 | BSD-3 | only if Roman numerals and key detection justify ~150 MB |
| Word frequency | derived English rank file from wordfreq | CC BY-SA 4.0 data | keep attribution and share-alike |
| PDF | reportlab 5.1.0 + an OFL TTF | BSD | full Unicode coverage |
| MIDI | mido 1.3.3 | MIT | chord-pad and click demos |

**Packaging follows the Claude Code plugin reference.** `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}` is versioned and must not hold state. `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_DATA}` persists across updates and suits a venv. These variables are substituted in SKILL.md bodies but are absent from the Bash environment. A `bin/` directory blocks claude.ai and Cowork installation ([Plugin reference](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugins-reference)). The recommended invocation is therefore `uv run --frozen` against a committed lock, with the project environment under the data directory, called from SKILL.md rather than a `bin/` wrapper ([uv docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/astral-sh/uv/main/docs/concepts/projects/sync.md)). Python must be at least 3.11. The team has not yet run the exact combination of `UV_PROJECT_ENVIRONMENT` with `--project`. The in-house build list is: all non-perfect and multisyllabic rhyme scoring over CMUdict phones; an "estimated" out-of-vocabulary stress fallback; the Hayes-style prosody scorer; a ChordPro subset parser and writer; key-aware enharmonic spelling; pop Roman numerals relative to the parallel major; the V-System labeller; and capo and tuning logic.

## Conclusion

The research changes the design question from "what rules should the plugin enforce?" to "which mechanisms can it measure, and whose lens is speaking?" The verified core is Pattison's stability model, Hayes's ranked constraints, Everett's systems, Temperley's base rates, Greene's V-System, the Owsinski–Moore–Lavengood layers and Lerman's protocol. All of it is quantitative or procedural enough to compute, and almost none of it is phrased as law. That makes the plugin's guardrails (co-write lyrics, coach music, guide arrangement, never decide) more than courtesy: they are the only stance consistent with the sources. The sources disagree in documented ways, as with Webb and Pattison on rhyme or Blume and his critics on title placement. Modern pop has also moved its own goalposts: chorus-ending V–I persists even though IV→I dominates loops, and bridges have given way to groove switches. A plugin that reports its basis and base rate, and records what the writer kept by choice, will age better than one that encodes a single school.

The biggest remaining risk is attribution, not capability. Many of the most quotable "rules" in songwriting folklore trace back to books this research could not open: Davis's checklists, Webb's dictionary reviews, Perricone's range figure, Rooksby on capos and arranging, Owsinski on tension and release. Before release, someone should borrow those books and verify them by hand. Until then, the plugin should cite those authors only by title or "as reported," and should state its measured limits honestly: prosody agreement of about 70% on folk data, contested Drake tunings, no fret-7 rule, and no maintained offline rhyme library.
