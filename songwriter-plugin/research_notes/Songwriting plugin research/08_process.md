## 1. Verbatim-verified Zollo material and cross-writer patterns

### Takeaway
Zollo's interviews show a shared stance. Writers report that songs are *received or discovered* rather than planned (Dylan, King, Simon, Cohen). Their actual craft role is editing and persistence: Simon edits, Cohen grinds for years, Townes revises down to adding an "s", and King walks away and comes back. The pre-Graceland "write it on guitar" habit, the Goffin–King division of labour and the Brill cubicle routine are all now verified. Mitchell, Newman and Young remain unverified.

### Cited Findings
- **Bob Dylan (1991, Zollo):**
  - The aim is an environment where you can "accept all the unconscious stuff" that comes, then "take it down" and get the thoughts out. **[verified]**
  - Rhyming is "a game" that "gives you a thrill"; rhymes "doesn't have to be exact anymore" (example: "represent"/"ferment"). **[verified]**
  - — [AS, Zollo Blog: Q&A with Bob Dylan](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-bob-dylan/)
  - A separate archive.org item holds Zollo's full SongTalk Dylan interview. It could not be opened (blocked host). — [archive.org item](https://archive.org/details/songtalk-interview)
- **Paul Simon (1990/1993, Zollo):** "I consciously try not to think about what a song should say." **[verified]**
  - He wants to "discover it rather than plot it out." His job is to "be the editor": spot good lines, then build "the first half of a thought" as set-up for the second half. **[verified]**
  - — [AS, Zollo Blog: Q&A with Paul Simon](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-paul-simon/)
- **Paul Simon (later AS Q&A, interviewer not named on page):**
  - "The music always precedes the words." **[verified]**
  - Words "often come from the sound of the music" and later become coherent thoughts. **[verified]**
  - Rhythm is crucial to lyric-making. Finding words is "like a puzzle." **[verified]**
  - "The first line of a song is crucial," because listener attention peaks there. **[verified]**
  - He learned on Graceland to "make the tracks first and write the songs later." **[verified]**
  - **Correction to earlier note:** his *usual* way was to sit with guitar and write the song, then bring it to musicians. Graceland "reversed that process" for about twenty years, "Until So Beautiful Or So What". "Cecilia" was made up on a home recorder. **[verified]**
  - — [AS, Paul Simon discusses Songwriter and songwriting](https://americansongwriter.com/interview-paul-simon-discusses-songwriter-and-songwriting/)
- **Carole King (1989, Zollo):**
  - Goffin and King wrote with King generating most melodies and Goffin most words. They wrote about 150 "bad songs" (Goffin's phrase) before "Will You Love Me Tomorrow?" **[verified]**
  - On block: "the key to not being blocked is to not worry about it." If nothing comes, get up, do something else, and come back relaxed. It returned "an hour later" or after days, weeks or months. **[verified]**
  - "You've Got a Friend" was almost all inspiration, "very little if any perspiration". **[verified]**
  - Song form is "limitless" and the time limit is "liberating." **[verified]**
  - — [AS, Zollo Blog: Q&A with Carole King](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-carole-king/)
- **Townes Van Zandt (Zollo):**
  - Priority is to keep a song "as perfectly bare and simple as possible." **[verified]**
  - Capture rule: if a good line comes while driving he stores it; near a guitar he writes "right away". Sometimes "the core of the song will come through all at once." **[verified]**
  - He usually writes start to finish, but songs "get real changed around." Small edits matter, down to "putting an 's' on a word." **[verified]**
  - Many Nashville writers write "by phrase"; he writes "by the word". **[verified]**
  - He sometimes forces it: he took a Nashville motel room to write and got two or three songs. **[verified]**
  - Wrote "Pancho and Lefty" in a Dallas hotel in a day and played it that night. Told it wasn't finished, he wrote the last verse the next day. **[verified]**
  - He was skeptical of hourly scheduled co-writes ("Mr. X from 4:30 to 5:30"). **[verified]**
  - — [AS, Zollo Blog: Q&A with Townes Van Zandt](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-townes-van-zandt/)
- **Leonard Cohen (Zollo):**
  - "If I knew where the good songs came from, I would go there more often." **[verified]**
  - A song "begins with an appetite." Inspiration-as-easy is a distracting notion; "I have to work hard as any stiff." **[verified]**
  - Once a song "enters the mill," he applies everything (thought, insomnia, sobriety, intoxication); "Nothing works." **[verified]**
  - Zollo's own summary: Cohen would take seven years and "forty or more" verses if needed, and kept working songs in his head at Mt. Baldy. **[verified as Zollo's paraphrase]**
  - — [AS, In Memory of Leonard Cohen (Zollo)](https://americansongwriter.com/?p=176756)
- **"Hallelujah" draft counts (resolved as a range, not a single figure):**
  - Wikipedia says Cohen reputedly wrote 80 to 180 draft verses. He claimed 150, and his notebooks substantiate many. Only 7 were officially released. **[verified on Wikipedia, with its sources]**
  - The 2022 documentary makers say about five years. **[verified on Wikipedia]**
  - He faxed John Cale 15 pages of lyrics, and Cale "picked out the cheeky verses." **[verified on Wikipedia]**
  - — [Wikipedia, Hallelujah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallelujah_(Leonard_Cohen_song))
  - The *Various Positions* article cites Cohen to Zollo in *Songwriters on Songwriting* (on his deepening voice), confirming Cohen is in the book. — [Wikipedia, Various Positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Various_Positions)
- **Brian Wilson (1988, Zollo):** first song was "Surfer Girl" at about 19, and he wrote because his father was a songwriter. The page has little process detail. **[verified]** — [AS, Zollo Blog: Q&A with Brian Wilson](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-brian-wilson/)
- **Book facts:** the 4th edition has 62 songwriters ("From Bob Dylan to Frank Zappa, Merle Haggard to R.E.M."). **[verified on AS boilerplate]** — [AS, Zollo Blog: Q&A with Carole King](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-carole-king/)
- **Joni Mitchell, Randy Newman, Neil Young, soul writers:** no verified Zollo text was reachable. Earlier characterizations remain **[unverified]**: Mitchell's open tunings as a generator, Newman's office-hours routine, Young letting songs "come". Search found no Zollo–Mitchell interview on AS. — [WebSearch result list, AS Joni Mitchell tag](https://americansongwriter.com/tag/joni-mitchell/page/4)

### Inferences
- **Recurring pattern 1, "receive then edit":** Dylan, Simon, King and Cohen all frame generation as receptive, and their conscious work as *selection and setting up* (Simon's set-up/payoff line, Townes's word-level edits). A tool should treat generation and editing as separate jobs.
- **Recurring pattern 2, "walk away is a technique":** King steps away when blocked, and Townes finishes "Pancho and Lefty" the next day. Neither treats it as failure.
- **Recurring pattern 3, "simplicity as the goal":** Townes keeps songs bare and simple, and Simon values clarity first. This matches Gauthier's "getting to simple" (§3, secondhand).

### Gaps
- Verbatim Zollo text for Mitchell, Newman, Young, Mayfield, Dozier and Haggard. This needs the book or a working AS URL.
- The full SongTalk Dylan interview (archive.org file host blocked).

## 2. Hunter's words vs music; Webb's routine; Tweedy's practice and cut-ups; Seskin on title-first

### Takeaway
- **Hunter:** his own 1988 account is now verified. It is "true collaboration": he sometimes handed over finished lyrics and sometimes wrote to Garcia's finished changes, but it worked best batting ideas back and forth daily.
- **Webb:** the "1¾–2 hours" figure describes one commissioned song ("Wichita Lineman"), not a daily routine. No Webb routine is verified.
- **Tweedy:** says he writes songs every day and frames the book around a daily creative practice. Timeboxed freewriting, movement and sleeping on ideas are verified. Cut-ups and the three-part daily checklist are still secondhand.
- **Seskin:** no title-first source exists. Title-first craft is documented by Andrea Stolpe at Berklee instead.

### Cited Findings
- **Robert Hunter (Fresh Air, 1988):**
  - Asked which comes first, he said Garcia was then "coming over to the house every day." Hunter gave him "starter ideas" and they "batter it back and forth." **[verified]**
  - Both orders happened: times "I've handed him lyrics and he's set" them, and times Garcia "handed me accomplished changes" for words. **[verified]**
  - It works best when they "battle it back and forth" until mutually agreed. **[verified]**
  - On tour as the non-performer: "All I had was my spiral notebook." **[verified]**
  - — [NPR transcript, Remembering Robert Hunter](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/764237225)
- **Hunter, other verified context (Wikipedia):**
  - He wrote "China Cat Sunflower," "St. Stephen" and "Alligator" lyrics in New Mexico.
  - He wrote the "Dark Star" lyrics at a Rio Nido concert.
  - He worked with McKernan ("Easy Wind"), Lesh ("Box of Rain") and Weir ("Sugar Magnolia"), then decided he could only work with Garcia.
  - — [Wikipedia, Robert Hunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hunter_(lyricist))
  - The "Uncle John's Band" 40-minute tape and "Truckin'" line-by-line details remain **[secondhand]**. — [Far Out](https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/truckin-the-grateful-dead-song-robert-hunter-fed-one-line-at-a-time/)
- **Jimmy Webb (Fresh Air 2004, rebroadcast 23 Oct 2026):**
  - The label/A&R wanted another place song after "Phoenix." Webb didn't like the idea ("not someone who... creates that way") but sat down and "spent maybe an hour and 45 minutes or two hours" on it. **[verified]**
  - He built a concept from memories of linemen around Liberal, Kansas and sent it saying "I don't really think it's finished". It was released as is ("Wichita Lineman"). **[verified]**
  - **Correction:** this is one commissioned session, not a routine.
  - Writing *Tunesmith* was the first time he analyzed his own songs. He found a "false rhyme" in "Wichita Lineman." **[verified]**
  - — [NPR transcript nx-s1-6010858](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-6010858); same 2004 material in [NPR transcript 128696937 (2010)](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/128696937)
  - *Tunesmith*'s routine/"step away" advice remains **[secondhand: Blinkist]**. — [Blinkist](https://www.blinkist.com/en/books/tunesmith-en)
- **Jeff Tweedy:**
  - The book came from "a sincere belief" in "a daily creative endeavor". He says "I write songs every day" and brings Wilco the ones that get a response. **[verified]**
  - During the pandemic he wrote country-style songs daily, and with his sons recorded "a song a day." **[verified]**
  - — [AS, A Tweedy Treatise](https://americansongwriter.com/tweedy-treatise-jeff-tweedy-album-interview/)
- **Tweedy book quotes as reported by AS:**
  - "inspiration has to be invited." **[verified on AS]**
  - One exercise sets a 5–10 minute limit to freewrite toward a finished song. **[verified on AS]**
  - Use physical movement to get "your body involved and out of the way of your mind." **[verified on AS]**
  - Go to bed with a half-finished idea to "put yourself... in the path of your subconscious." **[verified on AS]**
  - "for every five or so songs I write, I'm going to have one that means a lot." **[verified on AS]**
  - A bad song won't leave "a permanent stain on your record." **[verified on AS]**
  - He wrote most of "Can't Stand It" on a plane after the label "didn't hear a single". **[verified on AS]**
  - "No one writes songs—plural." **[verified on AS]**
  - — [AS, 5 Songwriting Tips from Tweedy's Book](https://americansongwriter.com/how-to-write-one-song-jeff-tweedy-book-interview/)
  - Fresh Air 2020 adds little craft detail. He says the hard part when low is just picking up the guitar, after which music reliably helps. **[verified]** — [NPR transcript 949583300](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/949583300)
- **Tweedy, still secondhand:**
  - The table of contents reportedly includes "Word Ladder," "Stealing Words from a Book," "Cut-Up Techniques" and "Have a Conversation."
  - Cut-ups are described as rearranging existing lyrics into new combinations.
  - A daily checklist reportedly has three parts: stockpile words, stockpile music (demos, practice), and pair them, aiming to sing at least a verse and chorus into his phone.
  - A melody exercise reportedly has you hum a melody while skimming a page so words "attach" to the tune.
  - **[secondhand: search summaries of Blinkist/Befreed/library record]** — [Blinkist](https://www.blinkist.com/es/books/how-to-write-one-song-en); [ICPL record](https://search.icpl.org/Record/1673464); [No Depression review](https://nodepression.org/the-reading-room-jeff-tweedy-offers-insight-and-encouragement-in-how-to-write-one-song/)
  - "Both and neither" on words vs music. **[secondhand: Rolling Stone excerpt, not re-read]** — [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/jeff-tweedy-songwriting-book-excerpt-1070889/)
- **Steve Seskin:** no title-first source was found.
  - Verified craft context: he arrived in Nashville never having co-written. He learned co-writing "by appointment" with pros.
  - He banked about 150 uncut songs over three years of commuting.
  - He still makes five to eight trips of a week to 12 days a year, "booked solid with two-a-day writing sessions."
  - Quote: "a heartfelt song well-crafted is pretty desirable."
  - **[verified]** — [AS, Makin' Stuff Up: Outside Looking In (Rand Bishop)](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-makin-stuff-up-outside-looking-in/)
  - Chorus-first advice is still from podcast notes. **[secondhand]** — [What's in a Song](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/steve-seskin-on-writing-a-great-story-song/id1593031703?i=1000543686312)
- **Title-first craft (Andrea Stolpe, Berklee):**
  - Starting with the title means "starting with the main message."
  - The title sits in the chorus's "power positions" (first, last, middle line).
  - Sensory titles (noun plus verb image) develop more easily than general ones.
  - Outline each section before drafting.
  - "Recoloration": stress different title words so verse 2 reinterprets the title.
  - Try the song as verse/refrain vs verse/chorus, and at another tempo.
  - **[verified]** — [Berklee Take Note, Simple Tools for Writing from the Title](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/?p=3032)

### Inferences
- Hunter's verified account argues for a co-write mode where two parties trade partial drafts ("starter ideas"), not a strict lyricist-to-composer handoff.
- Webb's story adds a "brief/commission" starting point: an outside constraint (a place name) plus a timebox, shipped while it still felt unfinished.

## 3. How pros version drafts

### Takeaway
No source documents file-naming or numbered-draft systems. What the sources do show:
- **Multi-day completion:** Townes's next-day last verse.
- **Keeping large draft corpora:** Cohen's notebooks and 15-page fax.
- **Shipping while "not finished":** Webb.
- **Large banks of uncut songs:** Seskin's 150, King and Goffin's 150 "bad songs".
- **Tweedy's selection ratio:** about one in five songs matters.

### Cited Findings
- Cohen's notebooks substantiate many draft verses. He faxed Cale 15 pages, and different performances use different subsets. — [Wikipedia, Hallelujah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallelujah_(Leonard_Cohen_song))
- Townes played "Pancho and Lefty" the night he wrote it, was told it was unfinished, and added the last verse the next day. — [AS Zollo Blog, Townes](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-townes-van-zandt/)
- Webb sent "Wichita Lineman" over believing it wasn't finished. Decades later, analysing it for *Tunesmith*, he found flaws. — [NPR transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-6010858)
- Tweedy: about one in five songs means a lot, and it "wouldn't have come" without the other four. — [AS](https://americansongwriter.com/how-to-write-one-song-jeff-tweedy-book-interview/)
- Gauthier's memoir prints early drafts. **[secondhand]** — [BookPage](https://www.bookpage.com/reviews/26418-mary-gauthier-saved-by-song-nonfiction/)
- Pain point from the earlier pass: memos named "New Recording." Apple Music Memos is closed to new users. **[secondhand, not re-checked]** — [songdrafts guide](https://www.songdrafts.com/guides/what-is-a-voice-memo/); [Apple support](https://support.apple.com/en-kw/guide/musicmemos/welcome/web)
- Capture habits: David Bazan's daily 10–15 minute voice memos; Fruit Bats' song written on a run. **[secondhand, from earlier pass]** — [Songwriters on Process](https://www.songwritersonprocess.com/blog/2019/2/21/david-bazan-pedro-the-lion-2019); [Bluegrass Situation](https://thebluegrasssituation.com/read/fruit-bats-eric-d-johnson-loves-a-left-turn/)

### Inferences
- The versioning model should be snapshot plus a cut-verse pool (the Cohen/Cale pattern: many verses, choose a subset per version). It should also allow a "shipped but unfinished" flag (Webb), rather than linear v1/v2/v3 files.

## 5. Starting-point taxonomy and how each reorders the work

### Takeaway
Verified sources now support seven starting points:
1. Music or track first (Simon, Graceland era).
2. Guitar song, words and music together (Simon pre-Graceland).
3. Lyric first (Hunter handing lyrics to Garcia; Goffin to King).
4. Music changes first (Garcia to Hunter).
5. Starter idea traded back and forth (Hunter–Garcia).
6. Title first (Stolpe).
7. Brief or commission (Webb).

Two more are partly verified: a captured line or "core" arriving at once (Townes), and words-as-sound (Simon verified, Tweedy secondhand).

### Cited Findings
- Track first: Simon said "make the tracks first and write the songs later"; words come from the music's sound and rhythm. — [AS](https://americansongwriter.com/interview-paul-simon-discusses-songwriter-and-songwriting/)
- Guitar song, then arrange with musicians (Simon's usual pre-Graceland way). — [AS](https://americansongwriter.com/interview-paul-simon-discusses-songwriter-and-songwriting/)
- Lyrics handed over to be set, or music changes handed over for words, or starter ideas batted back and forth (Hunter). — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/764237225)
- Title first: the title carries the main message and anchors the chorus power positions, then you outline the sections. — [Berklee](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/?p=3032)
- Brief or commission: an A&R request for a place song, timeboxed and delivered unfinished (Webb). — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-6010858)
- Captured line or whole core at once (Townes); time-pressured writing to a perceived need (Tweedy's plane song). — [AS Townes](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-townes-van-zandt/); [AS Tweedy](https://americansongwriter.com/how-to-write-one-song-jeff-tweedy-book-interview/)
- Discovery rather than a planned message (Simon). — [AS Zollo Blog, Simon](https://americansongwriter.com/the-paul-zollo-blog-qa-with-paul-simon/)

### Inferences (how each entry point reorders the work)
- **Track or groove first:** feel, key and tempo, then section map, then scat melody, then words fitted to the existing rhythm. The chart is early.
- **Guitar song:** chords, melody and words co-evolve. Arrangement and band chart come last.
- **Lyric first:** stanzas fix meter and stress. Melody and chords are fitted to them, with a check for stress clashes.
- **Music changes first:** the chart exists. Map sections, then write words to the changes.
- **Starter-idea exchange (co-write):** alternate partial drafts on lyric and music tracks with shared history.
- **Title first:** title, then chorus (title in power positions), then section outline, then verses with recoloration, then melody and chords. Form and tempo get tried as variants.
- **Brief or commission:** constraint and concept first, then a timeboxed draft. Ship-ready even if it feels unfinished.
- **Captured line or core:** bank it, or expand it at once if an instrument is at hand, then build out from the core section.

### Gaps
- No data on how often pros use each entry point.

## Implications for the plugin

**Entry points.** Offer doors that all lead into one song record. Each door marks the facets it supplies as "given" and suggests the next step from the reorderings in §5:
- riff/groove/track
- guitar song (words and music together)
- lyric
- music changes/chart
- title (Stolpe flow: power positions, section outline, recoloration prompts)
- brief/commission (constraint plus timer)
- banked fragment
- co-write exchange (Hunter model: trade "starter ideas" on separate words and music tracks)

**Separate receiving from editing.** Generation modes show no critique; a chosen "editor" mode does. This follows Simon's "be the editor," Dylan's "take it down," and Rubin's phase split (secondhand). The editor mode can offer Simon's set-up/payoff line check, Townes's word-level pass ("by the word" vs "by phrase"), first-line emphasis (Simon), and a simplicity check (Townes, Simon).

**Capture and bank.** Use typed items (title, line, core, riff/memo, progression, groove, concept) with key, BPM and mood tags. Add a quick "store it" path, from Townes's "if I'm driving, I'll store it away," and a one-tap "promote to song."

**Versioning.** Per-song snapshots plus a cut-verse pool, so a version is a chosen subset of verses (Cohen's 80–150 drafts and the Cale fax). Add a "shipped but unfinished" flag (Webb), a "finish next day" reminder (Townes), and a catalogue view that accepts most songs being practice (Tweedy's one in five; Goffin–King's 150 "bad songs"; Seskin's 150 uncut songs).

**Unsticking.** Offer these tools:
- a 5–10 minute "finish a song" freewrite timer (Tweedy, verified)
- "walk away and come back, don't worry" with no guilt messaging (King, verified)
- a move-your-body prompt and a "sleep on it" bedtime note (Tweedy, verified)
- word ladder and steal-words generators (secondhand)
- a cut-up shuffler over the user's own banked lines (secondhand)
- a hum-while-skimming melody-to-words exercise (secondhand)
- daily-practice tracking split into words, music and pairing, without streak pressure (secondhand)
- a motel-room "focused session" mode (Townes)

**Co-writing and credits.** Start a split sheet at session start, with roles (words, music, both), editable percentages defaulting to equal, PRO fields, and support for a non-performing lyricist as a full member (the Hunter model). Keep shared draft history for back-and-forth writing.

**Staying non-linear.** Treat stages as facets, not a corridor. Allow multi-session and multi-year songs (Cohen), loop back freely, and never assume a song is finished in one sitting.
