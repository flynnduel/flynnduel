## Q1. Pattison: object writing (method, timing, senses, variations, how it feeds drafting)

### Takeaway
Object writing is Pattison's sense-based warm-up. The **seven senses** (sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch, organic, kinesthetic) are now confirmed by a Writer's Digest excerpt snippet plus Berklee material. *Writing Better Lyrics* (2nd ed.) opens with the chapter "Object writing: the art of the diver," and *Songwriting Without Boundaries* is built on four challenges centred on object writing and metaphor (both confirmed from TOCs). "Destination writing" is **Andrea Stolpe's** technique (confirmed from her own Berklee article, which credits object writing to Pattison). The exact 10-minute timing remains confirmed only by secondary or snippet sources.

### Cited Findings
- *Writing Better Lyrics* (2nd ed., Writer's Digest Books, 2009). Chapter 1 is "Object writing: the art of the diver." Chapter 2 is "Rusty's collar: a lesson in showing and telling" — [archive.org catalogue record, TOC](https://archive.org/details/writingbetterlyr0000patt) [P-meta].
- *Song-writing Without Boundaries: Lyric Writing Exercises for Finding Your Voice* (Writer's Digest, 2011). Its four challenges are: object writing; metaphor; object writing with metaphor; writing in rhythm & rhyme — [archive.org record, TOC](https://archive.org/details/songwritingwitho0000patt) [P-meta]. Wikipedia describes it as "four fourteen-day challenges" focused on object writing and metaphor, and dates it 2012 (the catalogue says 2011) — [Wikipedia, Pat Pattison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pat_Pattison) [S-read].
- **Destination writing is Stolpe's.** Andrea Stolpe's Berklee Online article is excerpted from her course *Commercial Songwriting Techniques*. It defines destination writing as starting from "a place, a person, or a time, instead of an object." It refers readers to *Writing Better Lyrics* (Pattison, 1995) for object writing. It tells writers "Do NOT edit your paragraphs." — [Berklee Online, "Songwriting Techniques Using Destination and Object Writing"](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/songwriting-tips-try-destination-writing/) [P-read, Stolpe].
- Stolpe's sense list in that article ends with "movement," her plain-English label for kinesthetic — [same](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/songwriting-tips-try-destination-writing/) [P-read].
- A Berklee Online article says two courses are "built on a foundation of object writing": Stolpe's *Commercial Songwriting Techniques* and Pattison's *Lyric Writing: Tools and Strategies*. It describes object writing as sensory brainstorming with no judging, followed by a separate critical phase ("Toggling," Stolpe's term for lifting lines from destination writing into the lyric) — [Berklee Online, "Lyric Writing Roadblocks and How to Overcome Them" (2015)](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/common-lyric-writing-roadblocks-and-ways-to-overcome-them/) [S-read; the author writes in the first person about *Commercial Songwriting Techniques*, so it is evidently Stolpe].
- The seven senses (sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch, organic, kinesthetic) — [Writer's Digest excerpt "Generate Ideas Through Object Writing"](https://writersdigest.com/wd-books/writing-better-lyrics-excerpt) [P, **snippet only**; the page was unreachable this session]; [iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/object-writing-your-secret-to-vivid-song-lyrics) [S-snippet].
- Ten minutes, first thing in the morning, stop when the timer goes; "sense-bound" free association — [iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/object-writing-your-secret-to-vivid-song-lyrics) [S-snippet]; [Writer's Digest excerpt](https://writersdigest.com/wd-books/writing-better-lyrics-excerpt) [P snippet].
- Pattison on show versus tell: images that "SHOW rather than TELL" create pictures in our own heads. "The earlier in the song you see them, the earlier the song becomes about you." An image placed first "drips its colors" onto the lines below it — [patpattison.com, "Journeys with Rhyme Scheme"](https://www.patpattison.com/copy-of-pov-verse-development) [P-read]; same text in [American Songwriter, "Rhyme Schemes: Same Song, Different Journeys" (2016)](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-rhyme-schemes-same-song-different-journeys/) [P-read].
- Co-writing ethic, which applies to free writing too: "SAY EVERYTHING," make the room a "NO" FREE ZONE (advice Pattison credits to Nashville writer Stan Webb), and "I hereby grant you permission to write crap" — [patpattison.com, "Co-Writing"](https://www.patpattison.com/co-writing) [P-read].

### Inferences
- Treat **seven senses** as canonical for Pattison. "Organic" means inner bodily sensation and "kinesthetic" means motion or body position. Stolpe's "movement" label is an acceptable plain-English alias.
- Object writing feeds drafting as raw sensory "show" material. The no-judging generation phase is kept separate from a later critical phase. This two-phase model is documented in the Berklee article and matches Pattison's "no-free zone" and "write crap" stance.
- [UNVERIFIED] *Songwriting Without Boundaries* adds shorter timed variations (about 5 minutes and 90 seconds). The TOC confirms the four challenges, not the timings.

## Q2. Pattison: rhyme types, consonant families, stability spectrum, rhyme as brainstorming, cliché rhyme

### Takeaway
Now confirmed from Pattison's own columns:
- **Rhyme is a stability dial.** Perfect rhyme "slams the gate shut." Family rhyme is "pretty strong," and the more remote types "open the gate further," creating "uncertainty and longing."
- **Families.** Family rhyme keeps the vowel and swaps the following consonant for a phonetic relative, drawn from three families: **plosives, fricatives, nasals**.
- **Partners and companions.** He names relatives as *partners* (same place of articulation, opposite voicing, e.g., t/d, f/v) and *companions* (same family, same voicing, e.g., t/k/p, f/s/sh/ch).
- **Rhyming dictionaries are fine.** He calls "all rhyme should be perfect" the "most onerous" misconception. He also says finding a rhyme is *not* a creative act, so using a rhyming dictionary (a "worksheet") is legitimate.

Exact book definitions of **additive/subtractive** and **partial** rhyme remain unread; the books are lending-restricted.

### Cited Findings
- **Stability gradient, using cat as the example.** Cat/hat is perfect and "fully resolved." Cat/hack is "pretty strong," but not quite as strong, "because of the phonetic relation between t and k." Cat/nap, cat/sad and cat/grab are given as further near-stable options. Less stable types "open the gate further, creating uncertainty and longing": cat/rats, cat/laugh, cat/spot — [American Songwriter, "Lyrically Speaking: A Q&A on Rhyme with Pat Pattison" (2021)](https://americansongwriter.com/q-and-a-on-rhyme-with-pat-pattison/) [P-read]. These examples are family (hack, nap, sad, grab), additive (rats), assonance (laugh) and consonance (spot), listed from most to least stable.
- **Harmony analogy.** Perfect rhyme is like the tonic voiced with the root on the bottom; less stable rhyme is like a 3rd or 5th in the bass. Rhyme can close a section while harmony asks to move on, or the reverse — [same Q&A](https://americansongwriter.com/q-and-a-on-rhyme-with-pat-pattison/) [P-read].
- **Misconceptions.** The "most onerous" is "that all rhyme should be perfect rhyme." English has 17 vowel sounds, and relying only on perfect rhyme "dramatically limits your ability to say what you mean." The second misconception is "that finding a rhyme is a creative act. It isn't." He recommends a rhyming-dictionary "worksheet" (citing Sondheim and Eminem). The perfect-rhyme "rule" leads to clichés: "love/dove/above, fire/desire, hand/understand, eyes/realize" — [same Q&A](https://americansongwriter.com/q-and-a-on-rhyme-with-pat-pattison/) [P-read].
- He recommends listening to Randy Newman's "Feels Like Home" for rhyme types in the pre-choruses, and Warren Zevon's "Hasten Down the Wind" for consonance rhyme in the chorus — [same](https://americansongwriter.com/q-and-a-on-rhyme-with-pat-pattison/) [P-read].
- **Family rhyme (definition and procedure).** The consonant sounds after the vowel "belong to the same phonetic family." The examples are:
  - plosives: rub/up/thud/putt/bug/stuck;
  - fricatives: love/buzz/judge/fluff/fuss/hush/touch;
  - nasals: strum/run/sung.

  Justification: "since rhyme is a vowel connection," and sung vowels are exaggerated, family rhyme can replace perfect rhyme — [American Songwriter, "Songwriter U: Surviving Rhyme; Meeting the Family" (2021, by Pat Pattison)](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-surviving-rhyme-meeting-the-family/) [P-read].
- **Fricatives, worked example (safe).** First look under f's **companions** (-ace, -ash, -ach, i.e., s, sh, ch). Then "cross the line to f's partner, v" (-aze). Fricative family rhymes usually "quadruple your choices" — [same](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-surviving-rhyme-meeting-the-family/) [P-read].
- **Plosives, worked example (rut).** "D is t's closest relative. (Partners are the closest relatives for plosives.)" Next come t's companions k and p, then b and g. "Consonants can have several companions — but only one partner." Caution: g can sometimes change the vowel sound, especially after short vowels — [same](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-surviving-rhyme-meeting-the-family/) [P-read].
- **Nasals, worked example (home).** Families include -one words (bone, throne, zone), i.e., m→n. Home/roam and home/alone are flagged as clichés — [same](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-surviving-rhyme-meeting-the-family/) [P-read].
- **The table itself.** The column's family table is an image that could not be loaded. The prose examples confirm these memberships:
  - plosives: b, p, d, t, g, k;
  - fricatives: f, v, s, z, sh, j, ch (judge and touch are listed as family rhymes of love);
  - nasals: m, n, ng.

  The voiced/unvoiced grouping (b d g / v TH z zh j voiced; p t k / f th s sh ch unvoiced) is standard phonetics. It is consistent with the "partner" examples t–d and f–v — [same](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-surviving-rhyme-meeting-the-family/) [P-read, inferred grouping]; [SongChops chart](https://songchops.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/rhyme-families-CHART-songchops.pdf) [S-snippet].
- **Data error to avoid (carried over).** The BYU CS wiki notes swap voicing and list p, t, k as voiced — [BYU CS wiki](https://cswiki.cs.byu.edu/mind/identifying-rhymes) [S-snippet].
- **Assonance (Pattison's definition).** "the repetition of vowel sounds in stressed syllables, close enough to each other to be heard." He also teaches "hidden assonance" through shared diphthong components: long a = ĕ + ē, long i = ä + ē, long o = ŏ + ū, ou = ä + ū — [American Songwriter, "Knitting A Sonic Fabric, Part 1"](https://americansongwriter.com/knitting-a-sonic-fabric/) [P-read; the column is in Pattison's "Lyrically Speaking" series listed on his author page].
- **Vowel placement.** He uses a "vowel triangle" (front/tongue vowels versus back/lip vowels) and "voice-leading" between adjacent vowels to judge smoothness — [American Songwriter, "Lyrically Speaking: Choosing Sounds for Reasons"](https://americansongwriter.com/lyrically-speaking-choosing-sounds-for-reasons/) [P-read].
- **Internal rhyme.** In the abba version of "Bellbird Hill," the internal rhymes van/dam/swam blur the motion and create "a floating, nostalgic feel." In "Building the Perfect Beast," consecutive end rhymes plus internal rhymes (nerves/curves/blurred, shot/got) "put us in overdrive" to support an intense moment — [patpattison.com "Journeys with Rhyme Scheme"](https://www.patpattison.com/copy-of-pov-verse-development) [P-read]; [strongwriter.com, "Building The Perfect Beast: Form Follows Function," by Pat Pattison (2012)](https://strongwriter.com/building-the-perfect-beast-form-follows-function/) [P-read].
- ***Songwriting: Essential Guide to Rhyming*** (Berklee Press; 2nd ed. 2014). Its TOC: "Rhyme is your friend — Exchanging business cards — Getting references — Family friends — Friendly relatives — Kissin' cousins — The fruits of friendship — Sonic bonding — Craft and rhyme types" — [archive.org record](https://archive.org/details/songwritingessen0000patt) [P-meta]; [Hal Leonard](https://www.halleonard.com/product/124366/pat-pattisons-songwriting-essential-guide-to-rhyming-2nd-edition) [P publisher, earlier pass].
- **Additive rhyme (secondary only).** A vowel match plus an extra consonant after the vowel (feels/steals; go/boat). The less you add, the closer to perfect — [iZotope "5 Types of Rhymes"](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/5-types-of-rhymes-you-can-use-in-your-song) [S-snippet]; [RhymeGenie](https://rhymegenie.com/additive-rhyme.html) [S-snippet]. Secondary sources disagree on whether "subtractive" is defined by the direction of the sequence (the first word carries the extra consonant) or is simply the same relationship — [search-result summary of Graham English wiki and others](https://wiki.grahamenglish.net/index.php/Talk:Rhyme) [S-snippet, conflicting].
- **Feminine and mosaic rhyme** (secondary only) — [Berklee "Effective Rhyming" handout (Scribd)](https://www.scribd.com/doc/306660128/Berklee-Effective-Rhyming) [S-snippet].

### Inferences
- **Order of the spectrum.** perfect → family → additive/subtractive → assonance → consonance. This is now supported by Pattison's own ordered examples (hat → hack/nap/sad/grab → rats → laugh → spot) in the Q&A, so it no longer depends only on secondary sources.
- **Family-substitution order in the plugin.**
  - For **plosives**: partner first (t→d), then companions (t→k, p), then the rest of the family (b, g).
  - For **fricatives**: Pattison's worked example goes to the companions first (f→s, sh, ch), then the partner (v). He calls companions "the closest relatives for fricatives" and partners the closest for plosives.

  So the closest-relative rule **differs by family**: for plosives the partner comes first, for fricatives the companions do. This corrects the first pass's single "voiced/unvoiced partners are closest" rule.
- The Essential Guide TOC titles ("Family friends," "Friendly relatives," "Kissin' cousins") suggest one chapter per rhyme type, ordered by closeness. [UNVERIFIED] Mapping each title to a specific type is guesswork.
- [UNVERIFIED, high confidence] **Partial rhyme** in Pattison is a stressed syllable rhymed with an unstressed one (e.g., sing/waiting). It is a modifier that makes any type more remote.
- [UNVERIFIED] **Subtractive** is the mirror of additive: the first word in sequence carries the extra consonant. Adding unvoiced plosives or fricatives disturbs the match less than adding voiced or multiple consonants.
- Cliché rhyme pairs that Pattison names (verified): love/dove/above, fire/desire, hand/understand, eyes/realize, home/roam, home/alone. Seed the cliché list with these.

### Gaps
- No Pattison-authored definition of additive, subtractive or partial rhyme was readable. Archive.org search-inside is blocked and the Writer's Digest pages loop.

## Q3. Pattison: stability factors, line length, matched/unmatched lines, prosody

### Takeaway
**Confirmed from a Berklee excerpt of *Lyric Form and Structure*.** Pattison's lyricist must "juggle four balls":
- the number of phrases;
- the length of each phrase;
- the rhythm of each phrase;
- the arrangement of rhymes.

**Confirmed from patpattison.com.** Line length is "measured by the number of stressed syllables." **Prosody** is the one exception to "only tools, no rules." It is assessed through the lens **stable vs. unstable**. Odd line counts, unmatched lengths and open schemes (abba, xxaa) create instability. Matched couplets and aabb or abab create stability. Rhyme type is a further stability dial (Q2).

### Cited Findings
- **The four elements.** *Songwriting: Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (excerpt): "you must learn to juggle four balls." The elements are how many phrases, how long each is, what rhythm each has, and how the rhymes are arranged. "Phrases are sentences or natural pieces of sentences" — [Berklee Online, "Elements of Lyrics: The Great Juggling Act" (2015), excerpted from Pattison's book](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/lyric-elements-the-great-juggling-act/) [P-read].
- **Line length in stresses.** Line lengths are "measured by the number of stressed syllables." His worked example scans 3, 3 and 5 stresses and shows how unmatched lengths "feel unstable and tell us to keep going" — [patpattison.com, "Structure Creates Expectations"](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations) [P-read]. "The number of stressed syllables, as usual, controls line length. The number of unstressed syllables creates the feel." A tetrameter line has four stressed syllables — [American Songwriter, "Lyrically Speaking: Rhythm Schemes"](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-lyrically-speaking-rhythm-schemes/) [P-read].
- **Principle of Sequence.** If line 3 mimics line 1, we expect line 4 to mimic line 2. Patterns create expectations, and breaking them creates effects such as disappointment or longing — [patpattison.com, "Structure Creates Expectations"](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations) [P-read].
- **Rhyme scheme as motion.**
  - aabb "stops motion" at every couplet (a tonic function).
  - abab is a stable four-line journey: line 2's non-rhyme acts "subdominant," and line 3 drives forward through sequence.
  - abba "feels sub-dominant, like it's still going somewhere."
  - An odd number of lines "doesn't feel finished."

  "Motion creates e-motion" — [American Songwriter, "Motion Creates E-Motion In Songwriting" (2012)](https://americansongwriter.com/motion-creates-e-motion-in-songwriting/) [P-read; the article's bio box identifies Pattison as author].
- **Four schemes compared on "Bellbird Hill."** aabb gives four units of two lines, "very stable." abab gives two units of four, still stable. xxaa is "less stable, more open," with a surprise rhyme at the end. abba is open and unstable, giving a "wistful feeling" — [American Songwriter, "Rhyme Schemes: Same Song, Different Journeys" (2016)](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-rhyme-schemes-same-song-different-journeys/) [P-read].
- **Targeting.** An odd line count, a shorter third line, and a run of repeated vowel sounds each create an "expectation" that a following word satisfies. This turns on "extra spotlights." "Targeting is a spotlighting technique" — [American Songwriter, "Lyrically Speaking: Great Expectations"](https://americansongwriter.com/lyrically-speaking-great-expectations/) [P-read].
- **Escaping matched couplets.** The exercise works with rhyme scheme and "# stresses" side by side: shorten a line to three stresses, insert five-stress rhyming lines, or shorten the fourth and eighth lines. The result is a section that "doesn't end until the final line, which is bathed in spotlights" — [American Songwriter, "Lyrically Speaking: Managing Couplets"](https://americansongwriter.com/lyrically-speaking-managing-couplets/) [P-read].
- **Prosody and stable vs. unstable.** "I believe that there are only tools, no rules," with prosody as the exception. Prosody is all elements working together to support the central message; Pattison traces it to Aristotle's "unity." "Stable vs. unstable covers everything." Ask of each song, verse and line whether it is stable or unstable — [Berklee Online, "Prosody in Music and Songwriting" (2022)](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/prosody-in-music-and-songwriting/) [P-read; page labels it "Study songwriting with Pat Pattison"].
- **Form follows function.**
  - *Principle of prosody:* design form to support meaning. Short phrases plus consecutive rhymes create speed.
  - *Principle of contrast:* sections with the same function (verses) share a form. A section with a different function (chorus) should change "the rhyme scheme, phrase lengths, number of phrases, or rhythms of phrases. Maybe all four."

  Source: [strongwriter.com, "Building The Perfect Beast: Form Follows Function," by Pat Pattison](https://strongwriter.com/building-the-perfect-beast-form-follows-function/) [P-read]. Contrast "must make a difference immediately" at the first phrase of the new section — [same](https://strongwriter.com/building-the-perfect-beast-form-follows-function/) [P-read].
- **Natural stress.** "Preserve the natural shape of the language." Variation should come from arranging natural stresses, not from mis-stressing words — [patpattison.com, "Rhythms and Variations"](https://www.patpattison.com/rhythms-and-variations) [P-read].
- **How to recognise stress.** Stressed syllables are higher, louder, longer. One-syllable content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) are stressed. Function words (prepositions, articles, conjunctions, auxiliaries, personal and relative pronouns) are unstressed unless used for contrast. In 4/4, beats 1 and 3 are strongest; the meter is "architectonic." "A song is simply natural speech, exaggerated" — [patpattison.com, "Looking for the Perfect Match"](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match) [P-read].
- The earlier-pass snippet claim that "even number of lines = stable, matched lengths = stable" was attributed to "Motion Creates E-Motion." The full article states only the odd-line half explicitly. The matched/unmatched-length principle is fully supported by "Structure Creates Expectations" and "Managing Couplets" — [Motion](https://americansongwriter.com/motion-creates-e-motion-in-songwriting/); [Structure](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations) [P-read].
- **Books.** *Managing Lyric Structure* (Berklee Press, 1991) is the earlier title of the form-and-structure book. It is now *Songwriting: Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* — [archive.org record](https://archive.org/details/managinglyricstr0000patt) [P-meta]; [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pat_Pattison) [S-read].

### Inferences
- The plugin's "stability = number of lines, line length, rhythm, rhyme scheme" model is **verified as Pattison's own four elements**. He calls them phrases, not lines.
- Measure line length in **stressed syllables**. The unstressed-syllable count is a separate "feel" variable.
- Rhyme type is not one of the four "balls," but Pattison uses it explicitly for the same stable/unstable purpose (Q2). Model it as a fifth dial, labelled as such.
- Contrast between verse and chorus is checkable: compare the four elements across sections and flag a chorus that matches the verse on all four.
- [UNVERIFIED] Pattison's claim that the pre-chorus is the classic place to build instability that the chorus resolves. Pattison does recommend the Randy Newman pre-choruses for rhyme types, but the general rule was not found in readable text.

### Gaps
- *Lyric Form and Structure* chapter text beyond the Berklee excerpt was not read.

## Q4. Pattison: verse development, boxes, recoloring, title placement, power positions and spotlights

### Takeaway
**Verified from his own pages:**
- **Title placement.** Put the title in "prominent (spotlighted) positions": "title first, title last, title both first and last."
- **Mine the title.** Use the "who, what, when, where, why, how" routine to read the title's "DNA."
- **Develop the verses.** Each verse must move the idea to a new place so the repeated chorus or refrain takes on a new **color**.
- **Separate systems.** Put separate ideas in separate "systems" (verse(s) + chorus). The I–you–we formula is one way to divide the verses' jobs.
- **Spotlights.** First lines are in a "big spotlight," and expectation-satisfying words are "targeted."

"Power positions" is confirmed as a chapter title, but its exact list was not read in Pattison's words. A Berklee article by Stolpe, on Pattison's *Writing From the Title* course, names the first, last and middle chorus lines.

### Cited Findings
- **Reading a title's DNA.** For "Heartbreak Hotel," ask who would live there, what goes on there, when, where, why, how. This is "the ol' 'who, what, when, where, why, how' routine" — [patpattison.com, "Developing Verse Ideas"](https://www.patpattison.com/developing-verse-ideas) [P-read].
- **Title placement.** "always try to place the title in prominent (spotlighted) positions": title first, title last, or both. The title also gives the other sections "a target" to aim at — [same](https://www.patpattison.com/developing-verse-ideas) [P-read].
- **Recoloring with verses.** The title must be "flexible enough" to connect with each verse, and verses move "the title from color to color." Worked example: "Time to Let Go" (Keys/Pattison) has three perspectives, one each for verse 1, verse 2 and the bridge. "Start your song with a title whenever you can" — [same](https://www.patpattison.com/developing-verse-ideas) [P-read].
- **Chorus lines must stay global.** Every chorus line must be able to absorb each verse's perspective. Verse lines cannot do this "global" job — [same](https://www.patpattison.com/developing-verse-ideas) [P-read].
- **Verse development.** Verses "control the angle of entry" into the chorus, and each should focus on a separate idea. Stagnant verses make a refrain stagnant: "you don't fix stagnation by adding to it." The refrain "takes its color from what it attaches to" — [strongwriter.com, "Verse Development With Pat Pattison" (2012)](https://strongwriter.com/verse-development-with-pat-pattison/) [P-read].
- **Systems.** "PUT SEPARATE IDEAS IN SEPARATE SYSTEMS." A system is a set of verse(s) plus chorus. "Second Verse Hell" is the problem of where to go after the first chorus. The **"I-you-we formula"** gives verse 1 to me, verse 2 to you and verse 3 to we. Pattison calls it a useful guideline but warns it can become a habit — [same](https://strongwriter.com/verse-development-with-pat-pattison/) [P-read].
- **Productive repetition.** Repeat a sub-unit of a line so it changes grammatical type ("can you win" → "Can you win?"). Three methods:
  1. Drop the interrogative pronoun so the auxiliary leads ("Where do you go? Do you go?").
  2. Isolate the verb to form a command; this works only in 1st and 2nd person.
  3. Use the infinitive to make a command out of past or future tense.

  Source: [patpattison.com, "Productive Repetition"](https://www.patpattison.com/productive-repetition) [P-read].
- **Spotlights.** The first image is seen "in a big spotlight" — [patpattison.com "Journeys with Rhyme Scheme"](https://www.patpattison.com/copy-of-pov-verse-development) [P-read]. Syncopation against an established meter creates "a spotlight by surprising us" — [patpattison.com "Rhythms and Variations"](https://www.patpattison.com/rhythms-and-variations) [P-read]. Satisfying a built-up expectation "targets" a word and turns on "extra spotlights" — [AS "Great Expectations"](https://americansongwriter.com/lyrically-speaking-great-expectations/) [P-read].
- **Ambiguity versus vagueness.** For Pattison, ambiguity is positive: two or more meanings that all work. Vagueness is negative and "doesn't commit to anything specific" — [patpattison.com, "Structure Creates Expectations"](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations) [P-read].
- **Power positions (Stolpe, on the *Writing From the Title* course).** The title "is usually in the power positions of the first, last, and even the middle line of the chorus section." The same article describes "recoloration" (re-interpreting the title as the song progresses) and stressing a different title word per verse ("MISSING you now" / "missing YOU now") — [Berklee Online, "Simple Tools for Writing from the Title," Andrea Stolpe (2015)](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/simple-tools-for-writing-from-the-title/) [S-read; Stolpe on Pattison-originated course material].
- **Chapter titles.** *Writing Better Lyrics* 2nd ed. includes "Verse development and power positions," "Travelogues: verse continuity," "Stripping your repetition for repainting," "Spotlighting with common meter" — [archive.org TOC](https://archive.org/details/writingbetterlyr0000patt) [P-meta]. Pattison's site lists "Verse Development and Power Positions" among his Performing Songwriter magazine articles — [patpattison.com, Quotes, Books, Articles](https://www.patpattison.com/quotes-books-articles) [P-read].
- **Title characteristics** (from Pattison's *Writing From the Title* course). Effective titles:
  - "Distill and embody the central idea";
  - define a foundation all sections move toward;
  - can be developed over several sections;
  - are engaging.

  Interesting features: specific or sensory words, transformed clichés, metaphor or simile, clever twists, "sonic bonding." He contrasts a "DNA title" ("Tangled Up in Blue") with a "parasitic title" ("All of Me") — [Berklee Online, "How to Choose a Song Title" (2022), excerpted from Pattison's course](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/how-to-choose-a-song-title/) [P-read].
- **"Boxes."** The 2nd ed. introduces boxes that grow progressively larger as the verse ideas advance — [pdfcoffee copy of WBL 2nd ed.](https://pdfcoffee.com/pat-pattison-writing-better-lyrics-2nd-edition-2010-pdf-free.html) [S-snippet, unofficial copy]. Pattison's own POV page confirms the term: finding the POV early can "precede setting up your boxes" — [patpattison.com, "POV & Verse Development"](https://www.patpattison.com/pov) [P-read].

### Inferences
- **Power positions for the plugin.** Use first line, last line, and (for the chorus) middle line. Also count rhymed line-ends and expectation-satisfying ("targeted") positions as spotlights. The first two positions are verified in Pattison's words; the middle line is Stolpe's gloss of his course.

## Q5. Pattison: point of view and the POV check

### Takeaway
Fully verified. Pattison has **four** points of view, not three:
1. **Direct Address** (I talking to you; the closest intimacy, a "close-up");
2. **2nd Person Narrative** (no I; narrator "just outside" the song world, speaking to "you");
3. **1st Person Narrative** (I and he/she/they, no you; narrator inside the world, "a middle distance shot");
4. **3rd Person Narrative** (omniscient, "you are a god," no 1st or 2nd person pronouns).

The **POV check** is to try all four before finishing a song. Mixing POV is allowed but must win an A/B test against a consistent version.

### Cited Findings
- **The four POVs.** "Point of view is the single most important decision you make early." The three questions are "Who is talking? To whom? And why?" The four possibilities: "1) Direct Address 2) 2nd Person Narrative 3) 1st Person Narrative or 4) 3rd Person Narrative" — [American Songwriter, "The Point of Point of View: 3rd Person Narrative" (2019)](https://americansongwriter.com/the-point-of-point-of-view-3rd-person-narrative/) [P-read].
- **3rd Person Narrative.** The narrator stands outside the song world, knows everything (past, future, all characters' minds), and "always tell[s] the truth." There is no intimacy with the audience. Outside of quotes there are no 1st or 2nd person pronouns — [same](https://americansongwriter.com/the-point-of-point-of-view-3rd-person-narrative/) [P-read]; also [AS "Point of View: Developing Your Ideas" (2020)](https://americansongwriter.com/point-of-view-developing-your-ideas/) [P-read].
- **1st Person Narrative.** Narrator "wearing blinders." It mixes 1st and 3rd person pronouns, with no 2nd person except in quotes. Pattison gives 10 characteristics, including: the narrator "can lie, can be ironic, can be wrong"; has no access to other minds; is "locked in time," with only one now; and the singer's characteristics are relevant — [AS "The Point of Point of View: First-Person Narrative" (2019)](https://americansongwriter.com/the-point-of-point-of-view-first-person-narrative/) [P-read].
- **2nd Person Narrative.** "NO 1st Person pronouns." The narrator is outside the world but intimate. "You" can be singular, plural, generic ("All you need is love") or self-address. Commands become available and increase intimacy. It sits next to Direct Address on Pattison's "spectrum of intimacy" — [AS "The Point of Point of View, Part III: Second-Person Narrative" (2019)](https://americansongwriter.com/the-point-of-point-of-view-part-iii-second-person-narrative/) [P-read].
- **The POV check.** "try all four points of view before they put the song to bed." Worked example (Jim Brand's "When She Plays With Fire"):
  - The song was blocked in 1st Person Narrative.
  - Switching to Direct Address made the next ideas obvious.
  - "1st Person Narrative is a middle distance shot. Direct Address is a close-up."
  - Finding POV early can "precede setting up your boxes."
  - A 1st Person Narrative works best when the story is useful to the audience, e.g., "The Gambler": "The song should, somehow, always be about them!"
  - The contrasting pronouns you and I must be stressed, so they belong on strong beats.

  Source: [patpattison.com, "POV & Verse Development"](https://www.patpattison.com/pov) [P-read].
- **Mixing POV.** "Rewrite it in a consistent Point of View and A/B test the results." Mid-song turns, such as 1st Person Narrative to Direct Address, usually confuse. One cited example, a singer seeing her younger self in 3rd person, wins the A/B. "Change Point of View at your own peril" — [AS "Songwriter U: Mixing Point of View" (2020)](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-mixing-point-of-view/) [P-read].
- **POV shapes development.** The same title develops differently per POV. 3rd person can take a "cosmic view" and jump forward in time; 1st person cannot see past its own now — [AS "Point of View: Developing Your Ideas" (2020)](https://americansongwriter.com/point-of-view-developing-your-ideas/) [P-read].
- **Book chapters.** "Point of view: second person and the hangman," "Point of view: second person as narrative," "Dialogue and point of view" — [archive.org TOC, WBL 2nd ed.](https://archive.org/details/writingbetterlyr0000patt) [P-meta].
- A 2026 Berklee Online article (Erin Chase, *Lyric Writing 2* course) uses the same taxonomy: direct address as an "I"–"you" conversation, distinct from 1st, 2nd and 3rd person narrative — [Berklee Online, "Direct Address in Songwriting" (2026)](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/direct-address-in-songwriting-writing-lyrics-that-feel-like-conversations/) [S-read].

### Inferences
- **POV classifier.** The pronoun rules are explicit enough to implement directly:
  - I + you (outside quotes) = Direct Address;
  - you without I = 2nd Person Narrative;
  - I without you = 1st Person Narrative;
  - neither I nor you = 3rd Person Narrative.

  Quoted speech must be excluded, and generic "you" (= "one") flagged.
- The first pass's "three persons plus direct address" framing is corrected to **four POVs**.

### Gaps
- The Direct Address installment of the AS series was not located (the 2nd-person part promises it "next issue"). Pattison's own list of Direct Address characteristics is unread.

## Q6. Pattison: metaphor types, simile, imagery, cliché

### Takeaway
Verified on patpattison.com. Metaphor is "a collision between ideas that don't belong together," and all metaphors "must be literally false." There are **three types**:
- **Expressed Identity**: noun = noun, in three forms: "x is y," "the y of x," "x's y";
- **Qualifying Metaphor**: friction between an adjective and its noun, or an adverb and its verb ("hasty clouds," "to sing blindly");
- **Verbal Metaphor**: conflict between a verb and its subject and/or object ("clouds sail," "he tortured his clutch").

**Simile** differs because it does *not* transfer focus. Pattison's worksheets ("Playing in Keys," four collision exercises) are documented.

### Cited Findings
- **Definitions.** Metaphor is "a collision between ideas that don't belong together." "All metaphors must be literally false." Identical terms are definition, not metaphor. "Conflict is essential for metaphor" — [patpattison.com, "Making Metaphors"](https://www.patpattison.com/pat-s-lyric-tips) [P-read].
- **The three types.**
  - Expressed Identity "asserts an identity between two nouns," in three forms (fear is a shadow / the shadow of fear / fear's shadow).
  - Qualifying Metaphor: "Adjectives qualify nouns; adverbs qualify verbs." Friction inside that relationship creates metaphor.
  - Verbal Metaphor is "formed by conflict between the verb and its subject and/or object."

  Source: [same](https://www.patpattison.com/pat-s-lyric-tips) [P-read].
- **"Playing in Keys."** Words "diatonic" to a fundamental tone (e.g., the key of "power": Muhammad Ali, avalanche, army, Wheaties, socket, tide) are combined into collisions. The two guiding questions: "What characteristics does my idea have? What else has those characteristics?" — [same](https://www.patpattison.com/pat-s-lyric-tips) [P-read].
- **Exercises.**
  1. Adjective list × noun list.
  2. Noun list × verb list (Pattison notes it gives better results: "Verbs are the power amplifiers of language").
  3. Noun × noun in all three expressed-identity forms.
  4. A five-step solo drill: adjective→noun, noun→verb, verb→noun, noun→adjective, noun→noun. Participles count as adjectives. Pattison mostly skips adverbs in favor of strong verbs.

  Source: [same](https://www.patpattison.com/pat-s-lyric-tips) [P-read].
- **Simile versus metaphor.** "Simile doesn't transfer focus." Metaphor shifts attention to the second term, so you must commit to it. Simile keeps focus on the first term and suits one-off comparisons. Rule of thumb: several comparisons → simile; one comparison you will sustain → metaphor. Worked example: the car metaphor in Kurt Thompson's lyric compared with its simile version — [same](https://www.patpattison.com/pat-s-lyric-tips) [P-read].
- **Cliché.** *Writing Better Lyrics* 2nd ed. has the chapter "Clichés: the sleeping puppy (a case study)" — [archive.org TOC](https://archive.org/details/writingbetterlyr0000patt) [P-meta]. Pattison's dead-metaphor examples include "break my heart" and "feel the emptiness inside" — [patpattison.com](https://www.patpattison.com/pat-s-lyric-tips) [P-read]. Titles can "Transform Clichés" (the roller-coaster example) — [Berklee "How to Choose a Song Title"](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/how-to-choose-a-song-title/) [P-read].
- A related AS column, "Is This The Best Simile Or Metaphor Ever?", is in Pattison's author list — [AS](https://americansongwriter.com/simile-metaphor-ever/) [P-read, only skimmed].

### Inferences
- **Metaphor classifier for M1.** Noun-noun identity (copula, "of," possessive) = expressed identity. Adjective-noun or adverb-verb semantic mismatch = qualifying. Verb vs subject/object mismatch = verbal. "Like/as" = simile, which gets the focus-transfer note.
- The **simile vs metaphor commitment rule** is a concrete critique check: a metaphor's second term should be sustained (extended imagery); a simile can be a one-off.
- [UNVERIFIED] The contents of the "sleeping puppy" cliché case study.

### Gaps
- The chapter "Learning to say no: building worksheets" was not read, so the exact rhyme-dictionary worksheet procedure is known only from the Q&A summary.

### Inferences

**Corrections to the first-pass notes (apply these)**
1. **POV = four categories**: Direct Address, 2nd Person Narrative, 1st Person Narrative, 3rd Person Narrative. This replaces "1st/2nd/3rd plus direct address." Pronoun rules make it automatable (Q5).
2. **The family "closest relative" order depends on the family.**
   - Plosives: partner (opposite voicing, same place: p/b, t/d, k/g) first, then companions (same voicing), then the rest.
   - Fricatives: companions (same voicing: f→s, sh, ch; v→z, j…) first, then the partner (f→v).
   - Nasals: m, n, ng as one family.
   - Caution: g can change preceding short vowels.
3. **Stability elements are verified as Pattison's own four** (number of phrases, phrase length in **stressed syllables**, phrase rhythm, rhyme scheme). Rhyme type is a separate fifth dial. Add a contrast check: a chorus should differ from the verse in at least one element, and immediately at its first phrase.
4. **Qualifying metaphor covers adverb+verb as well as adjective+noun.** Verbal metaphor covers a verb clashing with its subject *or object*. Add a **simile check**: simile keeps focus on the first term (good for one-offs); metaphor commits to the second term (sustain it).
5. **Destination writing and "toggling" are Stolpe's.** The "No-Free Zone" is from Stan Webb via Pattison.
6. **Never cite "Writing Hit Lyrics."** Davis's titles: *The Craft of Lyric Writing* (1985), *Successful Lyric Writing* (1988), *The Songwriter's Idea Book* (1992).
7. **Webb lens = strict perfect rhyme,** documented with primary sources. Even family rhymes like line/time get flagged.

**Critique checks** (each outputs: location → observation → lens → question to the writer)

*Rhyme*
- **R1.** Classify each end-rhyme pair (perfect / family / additive-subtractive / assonance / consonance; modifiers: partial [UNVERIFIED definition], feminine, mosaic). Report a stability profile per section. Ask whether it matches the section's intended stable or unstable feel (Pattison prosody).
- **R2** (Webb lens, opt-in). List every non-perfect rhyme, including family rhymes, for justification. Suggest inflection or plural tweaks that make the rhyme perfect (Webb's sail/skies move). Never auto-reject.
- **R3.** Flag Pattison-named cliché pairs and eye rhymes. Offer family candidates using the partner/companion order, then remoter types, each labelled with its stability.

*Structure*
- **S1.** Per section: phrase count (odd/even), stresses per line, matched or unmatched lengths, and rhyme scheme (aabb = stops every couplet; abab = stable four-line journey; abba and xxaa = open). State the predicted motion and ask if it matches intent.
- **S2.** Natural-stress check: strong beats should land on naturally stressed syllables. Flag contrastive pronouns that need strong beats (Pattison's you/I example).
- **S3.** Contrast check: if the chorus matches the verse on all four elements, ask whether the chorus should change scheme, line lengths, line count or rhythm. Check the first chorus phrase especially.

*Title and development*
- **T1.** Is the title in a spotlight position (first and/or last line of the chorus; the middle line is a secondary option per Stolpe)? Does it meet Pattison's title tests (distills the central idea, develops across sections, engaging)? Davis's title test (one hearing, one meaning, summarizes) is tagged as snippet-sourced.
- **B1.** Recoloring: summarize what each verse adds, and ask what the title or chorus means after each one. Flag restated verses ("Second Verse Hell"). Suggest separate ideas for separate systems, or the I–you–we split.
- **RP1.** Productive-repetition suggestions: derive question-repeats, command-repeats (1st/2nd person only) and infinitive-commands from existing lines.

*Point of view and consistency*
- **P1.** Classify POV by pronoun rules, excluding quotes and treating generic "you" carefully. Flag mid-song POV changes and offer an A/B rewrite in a consistent POV. Offer the full POV check (rewrite in all four).
- **C1** (Davis default, agreed by Pattison). Tense, time-frame and setting drift. Ask whether each shift is motivated.
- **C2** (Davis, snippet-sourced). Does line one grab? Are there weak adjectives, or two weak words that one strong one could replace?

*Imagery and figures*
- **I1.** Concreteness and sensory density per section. Verses low on sensory detail get an object-writing or destination-writing prompt. Pattison's "image first" reorder suggestion: put the strongest image as line 1 to color what follows.
- **M1.** Classify figures (expressed identity in its three forms, qualifying, verbal, simile). Flag dead metaphors. Check that a metaphor's second term is sustained, or suggest converting it to a simile.

**Co-writing prompts**
- Object write (Pattison): seven senses, including organic and kinesthetic, no editing. The 10-minute timing is tagged as secondary-sourced.
- Destination write (Stolpe): a place, person or time; senses plus some commentary.
- Title DNA: run who, what, when, where, why, how on the title, then assign perspectives to verses so the chorus recolors.
- Playing in Keys: pick the song's key word, list words diatonic to it, and collide them.
- Metaphor drills: noun×verb lists first (Pattison says they work best), then the three expressed-identity forms.
- Rhyme worksheet: rhyming-dictionary pass by vowel and family (Pattison's "Rhyme is your friend").
- Rhyme-scheme journeys: show one verse as aabb / abab / xxaa / abba and let the writer pick by feel (the "Bellbird Hill" method).
- POV check: the same verse in all four POVs.

**Script rules**
- The human decides. Every finding is an observation plus a question, attributed to an author lens. Pattison is the default lens; Webb strict rhyme is opt-in; Davis consistency defaults are on, with the "is this motivated?" framing. Pattison's own line: "only tools, no rules" (prosody aside).
- Quote authors in under 15 words, only from [P-read] text. Attribute Davis items as "Davis (as reported) …" until verified, and Webb's dictionary or eye-rhyme items as "Webb is reported to …".
- Classify rhyme by the singer's pronunciation (CMUdict plus overrides), not by spelling.
