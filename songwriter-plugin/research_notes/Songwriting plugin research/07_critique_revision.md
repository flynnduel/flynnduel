## 1. Liz Lerman's Critical Response Process (CRP): the four steps

### Takeaway
All four steps are now confirmed from the official page. Step 4 is **Permissioned Opinions**: responders offer an opinion only if the artist agrees to hear it, and the artist may decline. Optional steps can follow: discussing next steps, a separate subject-matter discussion, and "working the work".

### Cited Findings
- **[verified]** Liz Lerman devised CRP in 1990 as a feedback method for creative work in progress. It aims to leave the maker "eager and motivated to get back to work". — [Liz Lerman, Critical Response Process](https://lizlerman.com/critical-response-process/)
- **[verified]** There are three roles. The **artist/maker** offers a work in progress and is ready to question it. **Responders** are committed to the artist's intent to make excellent work. The **facilitator** starts each step, keeps the process on track and helps people frame useful questions and responses. — [lizlerman.com CRP](https://lizlerman.com/critical-response-process/)
- **[verified] Step 1, Statements of Meaning.** Responders say what was meaningful, evocative, interesting, exciting or striking in the work. — [lizlerman.com CRP](https://lizlerman.com/critical-response-process/)
- **[verified] Step 2, Artist as Questioner.** The artist asks questions. Responders stay on topic and *may give opinions in direct answer to the artist's questions*. (This corrects the first pass, which implied that opinions appear only in Step 4.) — [lizlerman.com CRP](https://lizlerman.com/critical-response-process/)
- **[verified] Step 3, Neutral Questions.** Responders ask questions and the artist answers. A question is neutral when it has no opinion "couched in" it. — [lizlerman.com CRP](https://lizlerman.com/critical-response-process/)
- **[verified] Step 4, Permissioned Opinions.** Responders give opinions subject to the artist's permission. The usual form is "I have an opinion about ___, would you like to hear it?" The artist can say no. — [lizlerman.com CRP](https://lizlerman.com/critical-response-process/)
- **[verified] Optional extras.** After Step 4 the artist can talk about planned next steps. A separate "subject-matter discussion" step handles social or aesthetic controversy without pulling the core steps away from the work. A session can also move into "labbing" (working the work directly). — [lizlerman.com CRP](https://lizlerman.com/critical-response-process/)
- **[verified]** Two books exist: a concise primer by Lerman and John Borstel, and a longer collection of essays on applications and variations (*Critique Is Creative*). — [lizlerman.com CRP](https://lizlerman.com/critical-response-process/)
- **[summary-only]** CRP is used in music teaching, for example with groups of singing students at the Norwegian Academy of Music. — [NMH Praxis](https://praxis.nmh.no/en/l-and-t-music-performance/samarbeidslaering-i-en-gruppe-sangstudenter/critical-response-process)

### Inferences
- CRP maps directly onto a critic mode. Step 1 becomes "what this song seems to be about and what lands". Step 2 becomes "what do you want me to look at?", where direct answers to the writer's questions are allowed. Step 3 becomes neutral intent questions. Step 4 becomes ranked opinions that are offered, not imposed. The "next steps" extra matches a closing "suggested next revision pass".
- Because Step 2 allows opinions in reply to the artist's own questions, a plugin can answer a writer's direct question ("is the bridge weak?") straight away. Unsolicited taste opinions are what the permission gate is for.

### Gaps
- The exact wording of CRP's underlying values was not on the page read. Only the role descriptions were there, including responders' commitment to the artist's intent.

## 2. Pat Pattison's workshop method and recurring checks

### Takeaway
Pattison's own pages confirm three things about his sessions. They focus on strengths and then the next step. They run the participant's song through his tools. And, as the Bellbird Hill master class write-up shows, they **try several concrete alternatives side by side** (swap lines, re-scheme the rhyme) and let the writer choose by feel. His recurring checks are: title placement in spotlighted positions; verses that develop, with the chorus "recolored" by each verse; prosody, meaning structure (line count, line length, rhyme scheme, rhythm) that creates stable or unstable motion matching the emotion; and stressed syllables set on strong beats. All of these are now verified.

### Cited Findings
- **[verified]** A critique session can be part of any seminar or a whole rewriting session. It applies the seminar tools to show how they improve a lyric's impact. It focuses "on the strengths of the lyric", with suggestions for the next step. — [Pat Pattison, Seminars](https://www.patpattison.com/seminars)
- **[verified]** Seminar schedule: seminar from 10:00 to 1:00, then a critique session from 2:00 to 4:00. Participants' songs are "a springboard" for conversations about rewriting. — [Pattison Seminars](https://www.patpattison.com/seminars)
- **[verified]** Master classes take already strong songs and work out kinks. They focus on prosody "between the intent of the song and structure, rhyme, rhythm, point of view, phrasing". — [Pattison Seminars](https://www.patpattison.com/seminars)
- **[verified]** His list of areas covered gives a ready-made check taxonomy. Structure: number of lines, line lengths, rhyme schemes, line rhythms, symmetry or asymmetry, motion, contrast between sections, prosody through structure. Rhyme: types controlling motion and closure, and surprise and contrast through rhyme structure. Verse development: POV, tense, "recoloring repetition", and neutralising tense and POV in a chorus. — [Pattison Seminars](https://www.patpattison.com/seminars)
- **[verified]** "Verse Development" targets "Second Verse Hell". It teaches advancing ideas so the song gains interest, and writing a chorus that can repeat without changing its words. — [Pattison Seminars](https://www.patpattison.com/seminars)
- **[verified]** "Writing Lyrics to Melody" warns that a bad setting of a word pulls the listener's attention from *what* is said to *how* it is said. — [Pattison Seminars](https://www.patpattison.com/seminars)
- **[verified] Title.** Place the title in spotlighted positions: title first, title last, or both. The title gives the other sections a target. It must be flexible enough to connect with each verse, and verses move the title "from color to color". The first verse should set up a situation or emotion that leads naturally to the title. Pick a title as early as possible. — [Pattison, Developing Verse Ideas](https://www.patpattison.com/developing-verse-ideas)
- **[verified] Chorus test.** If the title sits with other chorus lines, each line should absorb the new meanings each verse brings. He calls this a "stern test" for any line auditioning for the chorus. His worked example is "Time to Let Go", written with Scarlet Keys, where the chorus is heard from three perspectives. — [Developing Verse Ideas](https://www.patpattison.com/developing-verse-ideas)
- **[verified] Stable vs. unstable structure (corrects first-pass "unverified" tag).** Line lengths, measured in stressed syllables, and rhyme schemes create expectations. The Principle of Sequence says a repeated pattern sets up what comes next. Odd or unmatched lengths and unrhymed lines feel unstable and push forward, and a short or rhyming close can feel like disappointment or resolution. — [Pattison, Structure Creates Expectations](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations)
- **[verified] Master-class method in practice (Bellbird Hill, Perth).** They checked that images *show* rather than tell, and that an image placed first "colors" the lines after it, so they swapped lines 1 and 2. They noted that an aabb rhyme scheme across two verses "stops motion four times". They then tried abab, xxaa and abba versions, each giving a different emotional journey with the same words. His conclusion: lay out the choices, choose what feels best, and always try the options. — [Pattison, POV / Verse Development](https://www.patpattison.com/copy-of-pov-verse-development)
- **[verified] Stress/beat matching (corrects first-pass "unverified" tag).** Match stressed syllables to stressed notes and unstressed to unstressed. Poor matching makes the line sound unnatural and deflates emotion. A song is "natural speech, exaggerated." Content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) usually take stress. Function words usually don't, unless contrast is involved. — [Pattison, Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match)
- **[verified] Line-level mis-setting diagnosis.** In a published critique of Katy Perry's "Unconditionally", he identifies specific failures: reversed stress on "Oh no"; unstressed words ("did", "the") on downbeats; secondary stresses of "insecurities" on downbeats while the primary stress falls on a weak beat; and musical phrases that split one lyric idea, so that "two musical phrases should equal 2 lyric ideas". His method is to say the line aloud several times, listen to your own spoken pitches, then compare with the melody. — [Pattison, Setting Words to Music](https://www.patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music)
- **[verified] Productive repetition.** Repeat part of a line so it changes grammatical type (statement to question, or to a command) and adds new meaning, rather than repeating for filler. — [Pattison, Productive Repetition](https://www.patpattison.com/productive-repetition)
- **[verified] Metaphor and simile.** Metaphor shifts focus to its second term, so commit to it throughout the song. Simile keeps focus on the first term, so it suits a one-off comparison. With several comparisons, use simile. Verbs are "the power amplifiers of language". — [Pattison, Lyric Tips](https://www.patpattison.com/pat-s-lyric-tips)
- **[verified]** A testimonial on his site says he is "a master at asking the right questions" that reveal small but important tweaks. (Testimonial, so self-selected.) — [Pattison, Quotes/Books/Articles](https://www.patpattison.com/quotes-books-articles)
- **[summary-only]** Drafting exists to give you something to rewrite. "Rewriting is the gold standard." — [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/pat-pattison-writing-better-lyrics) (this pass: redirected to the blocked www. host)
- **[secondhand]** An attendee says the second half of each seminar day was critique of attendees' songs. This is consistent with the verified schedule above. — [Musicgoat](https://musicgoat.com/songwriting-instructors-needs)
- **[summary-only]** His Coursera course uses peer review, and learners call its quality "luck of the draw". — [Coursera reviews](https://www.coursera.org/learn/songwriting-lyrics/reviews?page=2)

### Inferences
- The Pattison workshop model for a plugin is: **strengths first → diagnose by naming the mechanism (stress, line length, rhyme motion, POV, spotlight) → generate two to four concrete variants of the same lines → let the writer pick by feel.** That is diagnose-and-demonstrate, not "here is your rewrite".
- His spoken-pitch test ("say it aloud, listen to your pitches") can be turned into a text procedure. Mark the natural stresses in the lyric, then check them against the beat or melody positions the writer supplies.

### Gaps
- "Boxes" (each chorus recontextualised by a widening verse frame) is described here in his "recoloring repetition" and Time to Let Go terms. The specific word "boxes" was not seen on any page read this pass. **[unverified as a term]**
- No Pattison article on americansongwriter.com or online.berklee.edu turned up via site search this pass. The "Form Follows Function" and "Down the Rabbit Hole" phrasing articles are listed on his site but were not opened.

### Cited Findings
- **[summary-only]** In Murphy's No. 1-hit analysis, every US No. 1 of the year studied reached the title within 60 seconds, with an average of about 54 seconds including the intro. — [NSAI blog, Murphy](https://www.nashvillesongwriters.com/node/344); [Octopus Media interview](https://octopusmediaink.com/ralph-murphy-every-day-i-want-to-write-the-ultimate-song/)

- **[verified] Blume's six steps:** (1) developing successful song structures; (2) writing effective lyrics; (3) composing memorable melodies; (4) producing great demos; (5–6) the business side, meaning persistence, networking, pitching and publishers. — [American Songwriter, Jason Blume Offers Steps to Success](https://americansongwriter.com/jason-blume-offers-steps-to-success/)
- **[verified]** Blume stresses *melody* rewriting. He calls assuming your first melody is the strongest "just arrogance". — [American Songwriter, Blume](https://americansongwriter.com/jason-blume-offers-steps-to-success/)
- **[verified]** He advises bringing a work tape to workshops before paying for a demo, since the song itself may need work. — [American Songwriter, Blume](https://americansongwriter.com/jason-blume-offers-steps-to-success/)

- **[summary-only]** The book includes lyric, melody and demo checklists. — [Penguin Random House](https://penguinrandomhouse.com/books/15113/six-steps-to-songwriting-success-revised-edition-by-jason-blume)

## 7. Rewriting checklists, revision practice and feedback ordering

### Takeaway
Dodson's American Songwriter article, read in full, gives four checks: pronouns, time, backstory and twin verses. "Believability" from the first pass came from a different SongTown page and isn't in this article. Dodson also describes a creator/editor split. Zollo's revision article confirms that the amount of revision is a personal call and supplies writer testimony on how revision decisions feel. Berklee's Stolpe article gives concrete listener questions that work as a structured feedback prompt set.

### Cited Findings
- **[verified] Dodson's four checks.** (1) "Pronouns on the loose": talking *to* someone in the verse and *about* them in the chorus. (2) "Time jumpers": future, past and present in consecutive lines; keep time linear unless there's a reason. (3) Missing backstory: the writer knows the story, but the song doesn't tell it. (4) "Twin" verses: if verse 2 restates verse 1, the song "isn't going anywhere". He says checking these fixes a large share of the major problems. — [American Songwriter, Rewriting Lyrics (Marty Dodson)](https://americansongwriter.com/rewriting-lyrics-behind-the-rhymes-songwriter-u/)
- **[verified] Creator/editor split.** The creator gets about 90% of the power while drafting. The editor gets the "red pen" during rewriting, and the creator then returns to fix what was flagged. The editor also stops the creator arguing for "really cool" lines that only make sense to the writer. — [American Songwriter, Dodson](https://americansongwriter.com/rewriting-lyrics-behind-the-rhymes-songwriter-u/)
- **[summary-only]** The SongTown version adds believability. — [SongTown](https://songtown.com/on-songwriting/how-to-master-the-art-of-rewriting-lyrics/)
- **[verified] Zollo on revision.** How much revision a song needs differs for every writer. Some hold to traditional craft (perfect rhyme, meter, syllabic stress), and others value expression more. "That determination is up to you." — [American Songwriter, The Art of Revision (Paul Zollo)](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-art-of-revision/)
- **[verified]** Leonard Cohen wrote and then cut whole polished verses (from "Democracy") to protect the song's hymn-like tone. He said you must finish cutting the gem to see whether it shines. — [Zollo, American Songwriter](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-art-of-revision/)
- **[verified]** Paul Simon: "I remove the irritant." He cuts anything that sounds irritating. — [Zollo](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-art-of-revision/)
- **[verified]** Randy Newman: don't let the critic get bigger than the creator while drafting, then go back and "futz with it". — [Zollo](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-art-of-revision/)
- **[verified]** Jackson Browne moved away from insisting on perfect rhyme, influenced by Warren Zevon's false rhymes. — [Zollo](https://americansongwriter.com/songwriter-u-art-of-revision/)
- **[verified] Berklee feedback method (Andrea Stolpe).** Play the song through once. Then go section by section, stopping after a verse or verse plus chorus. Provide a lyric sheet and good audio. Ask: what feeling do the words and music give; what is the singer feeling; do you like the character; did the singing sound natural with the music; could you hear and make sense of the words; could you tell where the chorus started; can you remember any part; does the music's mood match the words; which part of the melody was your favourite, and can you still sing it? She notes that listeners lack songwriting vocabulary, so the writer must supply the questions. — [Berklee Online, How to Get Better Feedback](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/how-to-get-better-feedback-on-your-songs/)
- **[verified]** Stolpe's starting point is that generic praise ("I really like it!") and biographical questions don't tell a writer whether a song is landing. — [Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/how-to-get-better-feedback-on-your-songs/)
- **[secondhand]** TAXI forms ask what the screener liked most, what could improve, and why the song was forwarded or returned. Categories are Style, Composition, Performance and Recording/Production. Members complain about vague notes. — [TAXI feedback page](https://www.taxi.com/members/NgzgvadjQCKD0dzeeow3ig-casey/feedback/XZBtTW8PRwOumREztqHMFw); [TAXI forum](https://forums.taxi.com/post255354.html)
- **[summary-only]** BMI's ten words to circle as chances for imagery: beautiful, handsome, sexy, nice, sweet, special, kind, angry, ugly, sad. Also: filler words, clichés, over-cleverness. — [BMI, 10 Words](https://www.bmi.com/news/entry/10-words-to-avoid-in-your-lyrics); [BMI, 4 More Tips](https://www.bmi.com/news/entry/4-more-tips-for-instantly-better-lyrics); [BMI, Clever](https://www.bmi.com/news/entry/when-clever-is-too-clever-in-songwriting)
- **[summary-only]** TAXI: not every line needs to show. — [TAXI](https://www.taxi.com/articles/writing-better-songs-by-showing-not-telling)

### Inferences
- **Order of feedback** with the strongest support: meaning and strengths first (CRP Step 1; Pattison "focus on the strengths"; NSAI, summary-only), then the writer's own questions (CRP Step 2), then big-picture function before line-level detail (Pattison's title and development before setting), then permissioned taste opinions (CRP Step 4).
- **Taste vs. craft.** A note is *craft* if it names a mechanism and a listener effect, for example Pattison's "unstressed 'did' on the downbeat makes the line sound unnatural". It is *taste* if it rests on preference or on a convention a writer may reasonably reject, such as perfect rhyme (Browne) or the title in the chorus (Blume counterexamples). Craft notes are stated plainly. Taste notes are offered with permission.
- Stolpe's questions work as a **listener-simulation pass**: the plugin can answer them as a first-time listener would ("I could not tell where the chorus starts").

### Gaps
- No empirical study compares critique orderings for songwriting.

## 8. Common recurring weaknesses in drafts

### Takeaway
Verified sources now back most of the list. Pattison: mis-set stresses, musical phrases splitting lyric ideas, structure or rhyme motion that fights the emotion, verses that don't recolor the chorus, telling instead of showing, and images buried below abstract lines. Dodson: pronoun drift, time jumping, missing backstory, twin verses.

### Cited Findings
- **[verified]** Stress mis-setting and phrase splitting. — [Pattison, Setting Words to Music](https://www.patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music); [Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match)
- **[verified]** Rhyme scheme creating the wrong motion (for example, aabb stopping too often). — [Pattison, Bellbird Hill](https://www.patpattison.com/copy-of-pov-verse-development); [Structure Creates Expectations](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations)
- **[verified]** Second-verse dead ends; choruses that can't absorb each verse's new meaning. — [Pattison Seminars](https://www.patpattison.com/seminars); [Developing Verse Ideas](https://www.patpattison.com/developing-verse-ideas)
- **[verified]** Image placed after the abstract line instead of first. — [Pattison, Bellbird Hill](https://www.patpattison.com/copy-of-pov-verse-development)
- **[verified]** Stale metaphors such as "break my heart" and "feel the emptiness inside", which he calls "total snores". — [Pattison, Lyric Tips](https://www.patpattison.com/pat-s-lyric-tips)
- **[verified]** Pronoun drift, time jumps, missing backstory, twin verses. — [Dodson, American Songwriter](https://americansongwriter.com/rewriting-lyrics-behind-the-rhymes-songwriter-u/)

## 9. How writers decide a song is finished

### Takeaway
Writers describe it as a felt judgment: the song tells you what still irks you, and you remove the irritant. Practical tests: nothing still sticks out on replay; further tweaks would go unnoticed; the writer, not a rule, sets the bar. This pass verified the Zollo testimony. The other sources remain summary-only.

### Cited Findings
- **[verified]** Zollo describes the judgment as feeling "made for me". ## Implications for the plugin

### A. Prioritised critique rubric (evaluate top-down; report highest impact first)
Severity: **P1** song-level, **P2** section-level, **P3** line or polish level. Each item gives its basis and verification status.

**1. Idea / title (P1)**
- Can the central idea be stated in one sentence? Was the title chosen to carry it? — Pattison, Developing Verse Ideas [verified]; Davis [unverified]
- Is the title in a spotlighted position (first or last line of its section)? — Pattison [verified]
- Is the title flexible enough to take a new "color" from each verse? — Pattison [verified]
- Commercial-radio goal only: does the title arrive within about 60 seconds, and is it in the chorus? Label this as market heuristic, with counterexamples. — Murphy [summary-only]; Blume [verified, disputed]
- Is there a specific situation or POV, not interchangeable stock? — Pattison show-not-tell [verified]; AI-tell commentary [summary-only]

**2. Development / structure (P1–P2)**
- Does each verse advance? No twin verses. — Dodson [verified]; Pattison "Second Verse Hell" [verified]
- **Chorus test:** does every chorus line absorb each verse's new perspective? — Pattison [verified]
- Is backstory present in the song, not only in the writer's head? — Dodson [verified]
- Do sections contrast, and does the bridge do new work? — Pattison "contrast between sections" [verified]

**3. Lyric clarity and consistency (P2)**
- Pronouns: talking *to* versus *about* someone, consistent unless the shift is intentional. — Dodson [verified]
- Time and tense: linear or deliberately moved; tense and POV neutral in the chorus where needed. — Dodson [verified]; Pattison [verified]
- Show vs. tell; an image placed first so it "colors" later lines; not every line must show. — Pattison Bellbird Hill [verified]; TAXI [summary-only]
- Stale metaphors, emotion labels, stock or AI-tell imagery. — Pattison [verified]; BMI [summary-only]
- Productive vs. filler repetition. — Pattison [verified]
- Over-clever or private lines (the creator's "really cool" lines). — Dodson [verified]; BMI [summary-only]

**4. Prosody (P2)**
- Stressed syllables on strong beats; function words off downbeats; multi-syllable primary stress on the strongest position. — Pattison Perfect Match / Setting Words [verified]
- One musical phrase per lyric idea; no phrase splitting a grammatical unit. — Pattison [verified]
- Structure motion fits emotion: line lengths in stresses, rhyme scheme (aabb stops versus abab, xxaa or abba journeys), stable versus unstable. — Pattison [verified]

**5. Melody (P2)**
- Chorus melody distinct and memorable; "can you still sing any part?" — Stolpe [verified]
- Has the melody been rewritten, or is it the first take? — Blume [verified]

**6. Harmony (P3 unless it fights the emotion)**
- Does the music's mood match the words? — Stolpe [verified]; prosody extended to harmony (inference)

**7. Arrangement / production (optional, labelled, P3)**
- Only when asked, or for pitch or sync goals. — NSAI [summary-only]; TAXI categories [secondhand]; Blume's work tape before demo [verified]

### B. Feedback-phrasing rules (for /critique)
1. **Open with meaning and strengths**: what the song seems to be about and one to three specific things that land. — CRP Step 1 [verified]; Pattison "focus on the strengths" [verified]
2. **Answer the writer's own questions first, directly.** Opinions are allowed here because they were asked for. — CRP Step 2 [verified]
3. **Ask neutral questions where intent is unclear**, with no opinion couched in them ("What should the listener feel at the bridge?", not "Isn't the bridge weak?"). — CRP Step 3 [verified]
4. **Rank by impact**: about three P1/P2 notes up front, P3 listed briefly; function before line polish. — Pattison ordering [verified, inference]
5. **Every note = location + observation + mechanism + listener effect + basis + options.** Name the mechanism the way Pattison does ("unstressed 'the' on the downbeat sounds unnatural"). No vague notes. — Pattison [verified]; TAXI complaints [secondhand]; Stolpe [verified]
6. **Demonstrate with variants, don't impose a rewrite**: offer two to four alternatives of the same lines (swap order, re-scheme the rhyme, move the stress) and let the writer choose by feel. — Pattison Bellbird Hill [verified]
7. **Permission-gate taste.** Unsolicited preference notes take the form "I have a taste note about ___, want it?" and accept "no". — CRP Step 4 [verified]
8. **Judge against the writer's goal and the song's own patterns**, not one genre's rules. Present market heuristics (Murphy, title in the chorus) only for a declared commercial goal, labelled as market data, with known counterexamples. — Blume interview [verified]; Murphy [summary-only]
9. **Respect the writer's bar.** How much craft polish (perfect rhyme, strict meter) to apply is their call. Record "kept by choice" and don't re-argue it. — Zollo / Browne [verified]
10. **Keep creator and editor separate.** Don't critique mid-draft unless asked. In editor mode, be honest about high-impact issues. — Dodson [verified]; Newman via Zollo [verified]
11. **Never accuse the writer of using AI.** Flag stock or generic language as a craft issue ("interchangeable", "stale metaphor"). — rule; Pattison [verified]

### C. Final-check checklist (/finalcheck)
Each item gets: pass / flag / "kept by choice — confirm?"
1. **Central idea** stated in one sentence; the title carries it. [Pattison, verified]
2. **Title spotlighted**, and it changes color across verses. (Commercial goal only: arrives early.) [Pattison verified; Murphy summary-only]
3. **Chorus test**: every chorus line absorbs each verse's perspective. [Pattison, verified]
4. **Development**: no twin verses; each verse advances. [Dodson, Pattison, verified]
5. **Backstory** is present in the lyric. [Dodson, verified]
6. **Pronouns, tense, time** consistent or deliberate. [Dodson, Pattison, verified]
7. **Show vs. tell / images first**; stale-metaphor, emotion-label and stock-imagery pass. [Pattison verified; BMI summary-only]
8. **Repetition** is productive, not filler. [Pattison, verified]
9. **Prosody**: stress-to-beat alignment; one musical phrase per lyric idea. [Pattison, verified]
