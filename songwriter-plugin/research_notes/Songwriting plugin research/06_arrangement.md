## 2. Bobby Owsinski: the five arrangement elements and the element limit

### Takeaway
The **five elements (Foundation, Pad, Rhythm, Lead, Fills)** are now confirmed through a peer-reviewed source that cites *The Mixing Engineer's Handbook* (2013). The **limit is "usually four"**. Five work only rarely. That wording is still known only from blog snippets, because Owsinski's own pages blocked automated reading. His "Tension and Release" section remains unread.

### Cited Findings
- **Five elements confirmed [verified secondhand]:** Lavengood writes that *The Mixing Engineer's Handbook* (2013) "names five 'arrangement elements'": the foundation, pad, rhythm, lead and fills. She equates Owsinski's lead with Moore's melodic layer and his fills with her novelty layer (para. 2.6). — [Lavengood 2020, MTO 26.3 (PDF)](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.lavengood.pdf)
- **Foundation**: the rhythm section, usually bass and drums; it can include a rhythm guitar or keys playing the same figure **[snippet]**. — [Owsinski blog, elements](https://bobbyowsinskiblog.com/song-arrangement-elements/); [ProSoundWeb](https://www.prosoundweb.com/in-the-studio-considering-song-arrangement-elements/)
- **Pad**: a long sustained note or chord (historically Hammond organ, then Rhodes; now mostly synths, strings or a guitar power chord) **[snippet]**. — [Owsinski blog, elements](https://bobbyowsinskiblog.com/song-arrangement-elements/)
- **Rhythm**: an instrument playing counter to the Foundation, such as a double-time shaker, backbeat rhythm guitar or congas **[snippet]**. — [Owsinski 2012](https://bobbyowsinski.blogspot.com/2012/08/the-secret-to-great-arrangement.html)
- **Lead**: the lead vocal, lead instrument or solo. **Fill**: plays in the gaps between lead lines, or is a signature line, "an answer to the Lead" **[snippet]**. — [Owsinski 2012](https://bobbyowsinski.blogspot.com/2012/08/the-secret-to-great-arrangement.html)
- **Element limit [snippet]:** the blog excerpt says usually no more than four elements play at once. It adds that three can work very well and that five work together only "very rarely". Instruments in exactly the same rhythm count as one element, and each instrument should have its own frequency range. — [Owsinski blog, elements](https://bobbyowsinskiblog.com/song-arrangement-elements/); [Owsinski, "The 2 Arrangement Rules"](https://bobbyowsinskiblog.com/arrangement-rules/)
- **On "4 or 5":** Owsinski's posts differ. In 2012 he wrote never more than four, with five every once in a while. In 2011 he said more than five at once confuses the listener **[snippet]**. The most recent excerpt (above) leans to four, with five as the rare exception. — [Owsinski 2012](https://bobbyowsinski.blogspot.com/2012/08/the-secret-to-great-arrangement.html); [Owsinski 2011](http://bobbyowsinski.blogspot.com/2011/02/5-elements-of-great-arrangement.html)
- *MEH* 5th edition's arrangement chapter ("Arrangement: Where It All Begins") has sections on the elements, "Tension And Release" and "Conflicting Instruments" **[snippet; contents not read]**. — [Owsinski, MEH 5th ed.](https://bobbyowsinski.com/mixing-engineers-handbook)
- A ProSoundWeb excerpt says the blog material also appears in similar form in *The Mixing Engineer's Handbook* **[snippet]**. — [ProSoundWeb](https://www.prosoundweb.com/in-the-studio-considering-song-arrangement-elements/)
- "10 Steps to Troubleshooting an Arrangement" draws on *How To Make Your Band Sound Great* and *The Music Producer's Handbook*. Only its introduction was ever visible **[snippet]**. — [Owsinski 2015](https://bobbyowsinski.blogspot.com/2015/12/10-steps-to-troubleshooting-arrangement.html)
- "Change something every 4 bars" is not Owsinski's. It is a rule of thumb from Deviant Noise, which warns that it can over-densify a track **[snippet]**. — [Deviant Noise](https://deviantnoise.net/education/music-production/arrangement/)

### Inferences
- **Answer to "4 or 5?":** use **four as the default ceiling**, allow a fifth briefly at peaks, and count same-rhythm instruments as one. This is consistent with every Owsinski version found.
- Because Lavengood maps Fills onto her novelty layer and Lead onto Moore's melodic layer, the plugin can use Owsinski's practical vocabulary with academic backing.

### Gaps
- The text of the "Tension and Release" and "Conflicting Instruments" sections, the ten troubleshooting steps and *The Music Producer's Handbook* arrangement chapter all remain unread. bobbyowsinskiblog.com blocks automated reading, and the book copies on archive.org are lending-only. The plugin may use the general idea of tension and release, but not as Owsinski's content.

## 3. Texture layers, energy mapping, breakdowns and builds

### Takeaway
The academic layer model is now verified. Moore's four functional layers (explicit beat, functional bass, harmonic filler, melodic) plus Lavengood's optional fifth novelty layer map neatly onto Owsinski's elements. Recent *MTO* scholarship (Cobb 2025, building on Butler, Osborn and Nobile) treats songs as **energy trajectories**, using initiation, buildup and arrival or setup, buildup and peak. That gives the plugin's 1–5 energy map a scholarly basis. Carter's rebuild study is still known only from its abstract.

### Cited Findings
- **Moore's four layers [verified secondhand]:** explicit beat, functional bass, melodic, and harmonic filler (Moore 2012, *Song Means*). The beat and bass layers together supply the groove. Moore defines harmonic filler as filling the "registral" space between bass and melody (para. 0.1). — [Lavengood 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.lavengood.pdf)
- **Lavengood's novelty layer [verified]:**
  - It carries melodic pitch content but serves a different function from the melody, sometimes in opposition to it, often as call-and-response.
  - Unlike the melody layer, a novelty layer "is not essential" (para. 2.5).
  - It is used sparingly for color and typically appears in intros, outros and interludes (para. 2.7).
  - Its timbres resist blending with the ensemble (abstract).
  - Examples: the piccolo trumpet in "Penny Lane", the brass in "Sussudio", the tin whistle in "My Heart Will Go On", and DX7 presets such as FLUTE 1 and CALIOPE in Tina Turner's "What's Love Got to Do with It".
  - Related terms: organists' "toy stops", Agazzari's ornament versus foundation instruments (1607), and sound designers' "sweeteners".
  - Lavengood also stresses that functional layers describe what a part does in the texture, not its timbre (para. 2.10).

  — [Lavengood 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.lavengood.pdf)
- **Energy trajectories in scholarship [verified]:**
  - Cobb says EDM-influenced pop scholarship, following Mark Butler's *Unlocking the Groove* (2006), focuses on the "sonic energy" of each section and how energy trajectories build momentum across a verse–prechorus–chorus rotation (para. 1.1).
  - Nobile uses initiation, buildup and arrival. Peres uses setup, buildup and peak. Osborn says "riser" and "drop" for buildup and arrival in EDM-style songs (notes 9, 12, 13).
  - Osborn finds Top-40 EDM songs "almost never" have a viable contrasting bridge (compound AAA form), whereas 20th-century pop-rock more often uses compound AABA.
  - Cobb studies post-2015 songs in which a dramatic textural change, a "groove" switch, marks the final large section. Examples include Billie Eilish's "bad guy" and Maggie Rogers's "Fallingwater", where the second rotation thickens only slightly with one added synth layer.

  — [Cobb 2025, MTO 31.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.25.31.2/mto.25.31.2.cobb.html)
- **Contrast versus memorability [verified, quoting Osborn]:** producers must balance sonic contrast against thematic memorability. Too little contrast makes a song repetitive (para. 1.4). — [Cobb 2025](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.25.31.2/mto.25.31.2.cobb.html)
- **Carter, rebuilds [snippet, abstract only]:**
  - David S. Carter, "Building Bridges: Rebuilds in Popular Song, 1959–2024", *Integral* 38.
  - Subject: a breakdown at the bridge position followed by a gradual return of texture, which often leads to the song's climax.
  - Traced to Ray Charles's "What'd I Say" (1959), with Florence + the Machine's "Hunger" as an example.
  - Argues that pop can be goal-directed.
  - The sample, method and order in which layers return were not read.

  — [Carter, Integral](https://theory.esm.rochester.edu/integral/?p=9827)
- Kyle Adams (*Integral*) ties texture to form in post-millennial pop and credits EDM's influence **[snippet]**. — [Adams, Integral](https://theory.esm.rochester.edu/integral/?p=4948)
- Open Music Theory's "Texture in Pop Music" chapter asks when layers enter and exit relative to form **[snippet; page returned 403 this pass]**. — [OMT](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/texture-in-pop-music/)
- **Practitioner energy maps [snippet]:**
  - A 1–5 per-section energy scale, where the bridge should feel different from what came before *(attribution uncertain)*. — [Sonicbids/Soundfly "Scaffolding"](https://blog.sonicbids.com/scaffolding-structure-map-energy-flow-song)
  - A percentage template: Intro 20, Verse 40, Pre 60, Chorus 90, Bridge 60, Final Chorus 100. — [Jack Righteous](https://jackrighteous.com/en-gb/blogs/music-creation-process-guide/section-energy-mapping-make-chorus-hit-harder)
  - Energy drivers: density, brightness, bass weight, rhythmic complexity, harmonic tension, silence. — [Quosine](https://quosinedsp-site.pages.dev/articles/arrangement-energy-transitions.html)
  - An instruments-by-section arrangement grid. — [Orphiq](https://orphiq.com/resources/how-to-arrange-a-song)
  - Commercial "wave" energy charts. — [Hit Songs Deconstructed](https://accelerator.hitsongsdeconstructed.com/?p=3213)
- **Berklee's core arranging course [verified]:** "Arranging 1: Rhythm Section" frames arranging as supporting a song's form and feel and shaping its "emotional contour" through dynamics, articulation and texture. Its lessons cover parts of a song, adapting the lead sheet, drum and bass notation, bass in pop/rock, funk and fusion, and intros, endings, articulations and dynamics. — [Berklee Online course page](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/arranging-1-rhythm-section)
- A Sound On Sound analysis shows bass, drums and vocals entering and leaving across sections **[snippet; SOS refused by proxy this pass]**. — [SOS, Making Arrangements Pt 1](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/making-arrangements-part-1)

### Inferences
- **Unified vocabulary:**
  - beat = Foundation (drums)
  - functional bass = Foundation (bass)
  - harmonic filler = Pad, or Rhythm when it plays a counter-figure
  - melodic = Lead
  - novelty = Fill or signature line

  The novelty/fill slot is optional and works best sparingly and at the edges of the form (intros, outros, interludes). That fits a plugin default of "keep the signature line for the intro, gaps and outro."
- The scholarly initiation–buildup–arrival triad maps onto verse, pre-chorus and chorus, and justifies an energy-per-section map. The specific numbers (1–5, percentages) remain practitioner conventions.
- Osborn's no-bridge finding means the plugin should not *require* a bridge. Contrast can come from a texture or groove switch instead, as in Cobb's examples.

- Carter's corpus details and layer-return order are unread (Integral not reachable). No perceptual or corpus study giving numerical density curves was found. The OMT chapter text and SOS articles are unread this pass.

## 4. Instrument roles by idiom

### Takeaway
No authoritative published genre-by-genre role table was found, so the genre sketches remain **synthesis**. The general role logic (bass and kick anchor, a pad sustains, rhythm counters, fills answer the lead) rests on Owsinski and Moore/Lavengood. The idiom specifics come from practitioner and forum sources.

### Cited Findings
- **Bass and drums [snippet, attribution uncertain]:** locking means matching the kick, especially on beat 1. Funk bass often syncopates against the kick, while rock bass doubles it. — [Soundfly](https://flypaper.soundfly.com/write/rhythm-section-locking-in-with-bass-and-drums/); [Worship Artistry](https://assets.worshipartistry.com/greenroom/musicianship/skill-building/the-relationship-between-bass-and-drums)
- In Moore's model the bass and beat layers together make the groove **[verified secondhand]**. In the Tina Turner example, the bass moves from roots in slow rhythms to a more linear line in the chorus, a concrete case of the bass changing role by section **[verified]**. — [Lavengood 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.lavengood.pdf)
- **Harmonic filler in practice [verified]:** chordal guitar, an electric-piano preset and strings that "thicken this core texture". Novelty sounds trade call-and-response with the vocal (Level 42 example, para. 2.14). — [Lavengood 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.lavengood.pdf)
- **Berklee [verified]:** the core arranging course teaches bass styles separately for swing, bossa/samba, pop/rock, funk and fusion, showing that bass role is taught as idiom-specific. — [Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/arranging-1-rhythm-section)
- **Country [snippet; forum anecdote]:** the leader or producer assigns intros, turnarounds and fills. A typical layout has steel on verse 1, fiddle on verse 2 and a split solo. Fills stay short, and a player lays out when another is filling. — [Steel Guitar Forum](https://bb.steelguitarforum.com/viewtopic.php?p=2865298)
- **Nashville and Southern studio practice [verified]:** rather than fully written arrangements (New York practice), session players often built the arrangement collectively in the studio. They learned the demo by ear and improvised parts, a "head session". Number charts let them jot the arrangement as fast as it developed. — [Wikipedia, NNS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System)
- **Jam bands [snippet]:** Phish.net distinguishes "type 1" jams (within the song's form) from "type 2" jams (leaving it). Phish steers with hand signals. — [Phish.net](https://phish.net/blog/1311714510/revisiting-jamming-types); [Westword](https://www.westword.com/music/a-guide-to-phishs-on-stage-hand-signals-from-drunken-bar-swing-to-pork-tornado-5672557)
- **Backing vocals [snippet]:** three roles: pad (oohs and aahs), lyric harmony, call-and-response. — [AirGigs](https://blog.airgigs.com/2017/02/the-anatomy-of-a-background-vocal-arrangement-part-2-bgvs/). Staging them toward a bigger last chorus *(attribution uncertain)*. — [iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/recording-backing-vocals-to-bring-your-chorus-to-life)
- **Pop form context [verified]:** recent EDM-influenced pop often drops the contrasting bridge, and contrast comes from groove or texture switches. — [Cobb 2025](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.25.31.2/mto.25.31.2.cobb.html)

### Inferences (genre sketches: synthesis, present as suggestions)
- **Folk-rock / Americana**: acoustic guitar as foundation plus rhythm, roots-based bass with the kick, drums entering late, one colour instrument (mandolin, steel, organ) as pad or fill, harmonies on choruses.
- **Soul / R&B**: bass syncopated against the kick, organ or Rhodes pad, call-and-response BGVs, horn stabs counted as one element.
- **Country**: traded fills, split solos, strict space around the vocal; leader assigns intro, turnaround and fill duties (forum-sourced).
- **Jam**: fixed form plus a named open section, with a jam leader, exit cue and landing point.
- **Indie**: contrast through subtraction, register and texture.
- **Pop**: staged layer entries, pre-chorus as buildup, a stop before the arrival; a bridge optional (Osborn via Cobb).

### Gaps
- No documented source gives a cross-genre role table. The country material is anecdotal. No documented Grateful Dead cue system was found. Kimbra's seven BGV roles are unread.

## 5. Communicating the arrangement: Nashville Number System, cues and demos

### Takeaway
The NNS section is now verified on Wikipedia, with corrections to the first pass:
- **Origin:** the system was developed in the late 1950s by Neal Matthews Jr. of the Jordanaires and adapted for rhythm sections by Charlie McCoy in the early 1960s.
- **Chas Williams:** wrote the standard book, now in its 11th edition (2023).
- **Staccato:** shown with a marcato or a dot.
- **Standardization:** the symbols are explicitly *not* standardized.

The demo-presentation advice is still practitioner snippets.

### Cited Findings (all [verified] from [Wikipedia, NNS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System) unless marked)
- **Origin:**
  - Neal Matthews Jr. developed the system in the late 1950s for writing the Jordanaires' vocal parts, drawing on gospel shape-note notation.
  - Charlie McCoy adapted it in the early 1960s to chart chords for bass, drums, piano and guitar.
  - The Country Music Hall of Fame credits Matthews with popularizing it.
  - *Correction:* the first pass said Chas Williams "compiled and published" the system in 1988. The current article cites Williams as the historian and author (11th ed., 2023), not the originator. The 1988 date is not in the article.
- **Chord numbering:** chords are numbered by the root's scale degree, with the tonic as 1, so a chart works in any key. All chords are major unless marked; minor is shown with "-" or "m".
- **Layout:** charts are usually laid out in lines of four measures. Several chords in one bar are grouped by underlining (a split measure). A boxed measure marks a complex bar, with hash marks for beats. "1/5" means the 1 chord over a 5 bass note.
- **Performance markings:** charts can carry rhythm, duration, articulation, rests, pushes (anticipations) and form changes. A **diamond** marks a sustained chord. A **marcato or a dot** marks a staccato chord. Rests use ordinary notation.
- **Not standardized:** individual players notate differently. Williams reproduces charts of one song by seven session players to show the variation.
- **Level of detail:** a chart may be skeletal, leaving parts and voicings to players, or detailed. Staff notation is occasionally added for passages that must be played exactly as written. A whole song usually fits on one page.
- **Purpose:** the chart gives the harmonic and structural framework without specifying every note, and players improvise voicings and solos from it. Matthews: it lets players "depend on our ears rather than a written arrangement."
- **Oral use:** the article's example of a spoken arrangement is "kick it off from the 5", "hit the 4 on the way down", "watch me for the 2". This is a primary-ish source for spoken cue phrasing.
- de Clercq (2019, *Journal of Music Theory Pedagogy* 33) offers the NNS as a framework for teaching pop harmony. — [Wikipedia citing de Clercq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System); [de Clercq PDF](https://www.midside.com/publications/declercq_2019_jmtp.pdf)
- **Demos and presenting to a band [snippet]:**
  - Leave "about 20 percent for the studio". — [Spotify for Artists](https://artists.spotify.com/en/blog/the-dos-and-donts-of-pre-production-demoing)
  - Resist dictating exactly what players do. — [BMI](https://bmi.com/news/entry/ideas-for-arranging-your-demo)
  - A rough demo should convey form, tempo, feel and core parts, and should be in tune. — [Soundbrenner](https://www.soundbrenner.com/blogs/articles/how-to-record-a-rough-demo-that-actually-improves-your-song)
  - Give parts early, because players learn at different speeds. — [Muzines](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/its-my-song/10908)

### Inferences
- **Cue-word list** (still not from one source). Partly grounded: "kick it off", "hit the [chord]", "watch me" (Wikipedia oral example) and "push" (NNS). From general practice, unverified: "drop / lay out", "bring it down", "build", "stop / hit", "break", "tag", "ride it out", "turnaround", "half-time / double-time", "last time", "four-count back in", "button". Present them with short glosses as common usage, not as a standard.
- The head-session history supports the plugin's stance: give roles, feel, form and dynamics, and let players write their parts. This is how Nashville and Muscle Shoals sessions worked.
- The road map can borrow NNS content without its notation: section, bars, who's in, feel, dynamic, pushes and stops, holds, and the transition. Because the symbols aren't standardized, any optional chart should include a legend.

### Gaps
- Williams's own definitions (tag, turnaround and so on) and any canonical rehearsal cue list are unread. The demo advice is still snippet-level.

## 6. Common arrangement mistakes

### Takeaway
The consensus is unchanged and still mostly snippet-sourced: everyone playing all the time in the same register and rhythm, fills masking the vocal, no contrast, only ever adding, and over-dictating. The new verified support is Osborn's contrast-versus-memorability point (via Cobb) and Owsinski's element limit (snippet).

### Cited Findings
- Too little sonic contrast makes a song repetitive, and too much thematic diversity costs cohesion **[verified, Osborn quoted in Cobb]**. — [Cobb 2025](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.25.31.2/mto.25.31.2.cobb.html)
- More than four (rarely five) elements at once confuses; shared frequency ranges clash **[snippet]**. — [Owsinski](https://bobbyowsinskiblog.com/arrangement-rules/)
- Avoid many musicians playing at once at the same dynamic and pattern **[snippet]**. — [David Santistevan](https://www.davidsantistevan.com/monitor-mix/)
- Ears follow only so many parts, so reduce or alternate them **[snippet]**. — [ProSoundWeb](https://www.prosoundweb.com/a-matter-of-time-or-how-to-fit-all-that-stuff-into-the-mix/)
- Masking examples (a lead fill under every vocal phrase, piano in the voice's range) and the mute test *(attribution uncertain)*. — [Samplesound](https://samplesoundmusic.com/blogs/news/common-mix-issues-that-are-actually-musical-issues); [iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/how-to-write-better-song-arrangements-around-vocals.html)
- "Change something every 4 bars" can over-densify **[snippet]**. — [Deviant Noise](https://deviantnoise.net/education/music-production/arrangement/)
- Rehearsal: agree how each section starts and stops **[snippet]**. — [Sonicbids](https://blog.sonicbids.com/how-to-avoid-the-5-most-common-band-rehearsal-mistakes)

### Inferences
- Singer-songwriter-specific mistakes (synthesis):
  - the band copies the full strum
  - the full band starts at bar 1
  - chorus 1 is as big as the last chorus
  - no gaps for fills
  - open-ended solos
  - an undecided ending
  - forcing a bridge when a texture switch would do (after Osborn and Cobb)

## Implications for the plugin

### Takeaway
Use one vocabulary: **Owsinski's five elements cross-mapped to Moore's four layers plus Lavengood's novelty layer**. This mapping is now verified in a peer-reviewed article. Pair it with a per-section energy map (supported by the initiation–buildup–arrival scholarship) and a critique pass. The pass has a **default ceiling of four simultaneous elements** (same rhythm counts as one), an optional fifth at peaks, frequency space, contrast, and room for players. Never output notation or tab. Do not attribute rules to Rooksby, or tension-and-release specifics to Owsinski.

### Cited Findings (basis)
- Elements ↔ layers mapping: [Lavengood 2020](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.lavengood.pdf). Energy trajectories and the optional bridge: [Cobb 2025](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.25.31.2/mto.25.31.2.cobb.html). NNS conventions and head-session practice: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System). Element limit (snippet): [Owsinski](https://bobbyowsinskiblog.com/song-arrangement-elements/). Emotional contour through dynamics, articulation and texture: [Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/arranging-1-rhythm-section).

### Inferences: design proposals

**A. Section guidance template (plain words)**
```
SECTION: Verse 1  (≈ bars / times through)   FUNCTION: initiation | buildup | arrival | contrast | ending
ENERGY: 2/5 — "intimate, room to breathe"
WHO'S IN (by role): foundation = acoustic gtr + bass roots; pad = —; rhythm = —; lead = vocal; fill/novelty = —
WHO'S WAITING: drums, keys, 2nd guitar, BGVs
FEEL / DYNAMICS: laid back, soft attack, gaps between lines
HITS / PUSHES / HOLDS: e.g. hold the last chord of the section
TRANSITION: drums enter with a light lift into the pre-chorus
ELEMENT COUNT: 2 of max 4 (same rhythm = one)
LEFT TO THE PLAYERS: exact bass line, drum pattern, voicings
```
The FUNCTION field is new. It uses the initiation, buildup and arrival terms (Nobile and others via Cobb) so the user sees *why* each section has its energy.

**B. Energy map**
- Scale: 1 sparse, 2 settled, 3 building, 4 full, 5 peak.
- Levers: add or remove a player; register; subdivision; drum voice; attack; vocal stack; stops and space; half-time or double-time; pad in or out; groove switch.
- Shapes: staircase; breakdown-and-rebuild (Carter, abstract-level); slow burn; flat-intimate; jam bloom; late groove switch (Cobb).
- Default pop/rock template (editable): Intro 2, V1 2, Pre 3, Ch1 4, V2 3, Ch2 4, Bridge 2 or 5, Final Ch 5, Outro 2–3.
- A bridge is optional. Offer a texture or groove switch as an alternative.

**C. Role catalogue**
- Drums: out → pulse → groove → drive → peak.
- Bass: roots → locked with kick → moving or syncopated line (soul/funk) → upper-register push. It can change role by section, as in the Tina Turner example.
- Keys: pad, comping (harmonic filler), or a signature line (novelty).
- Second guitar: contrast the songwriter's part by register, rhythm or sustain. Never duplicate the strum.
- Lead / solo: answers in the gaps; give a defined length and owner.
- Novelty / signature line: sparing, in intros, outros, interludes and the gaps (Lavengood).
- BGVs: pad, lyric harmony or call-and-response, staged toward the last chorus.
- Idiom sketches as in §4, labelled as suggestions.

**D. Critique checks**
1. Element count above 4 in any section (above 5 even at the peak)?
2. Duplicated rhythm across guitar, keys and bass?
3. Register clash with the vocal, or a crowded low end?
4. Build headroom: is everything saved for later, or is chorus 1 already the peak?
5. Contrast: does each section change at least one lever? If there's no bridge, is there a texture or groove switch?
6. Vocal space: do fills and novelty parts sit only in the gaps?
7. Is every transition marked?
8. Solo or jam: length, owner, exit cue, landing.
9. Is the ending decided?
10. Room for players: roles and feel specified, parts left open (the head-session ethos; "about 20% for the studio").
11. Communicability: a one-page road map, plus an optional NNS chart with a symbol legend, since the symbols aren't standardized.
