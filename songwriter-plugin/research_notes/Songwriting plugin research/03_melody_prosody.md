## 1. Jack Perricone: stable/unstable tones, front-/back-heavy phrases, range, contour, motif development, sectional contrast via tessitura

### Takeaway
Two primary Perricone texts were read in full:
- the Chapter 1 excerpt of *Melody in Songwriting* on Berklee Online;
- his own analysis of his song "It Doesn't Get Any Better Than This" in *Berklee Today* (Spring 2008).

They confirm four points:
- Phrases are defined by points of rest or resolution and by breathing.
- Singability depends on how many leaps the melody has, room to breathe, and range, including how fast range changes inside a section.
- Motives are developed by repetition, extension and re-harmonization.
- Bridges contrast through a different starting chord, a plainer rhythm and contour, more uniform phrasing, and a middle-register tessitura against a wide-range A section. Unstable melody tones (scale degree 4) set up a resolution to a stable one (scale degree 3).

**Correction to the first pass:** the "front-heavy / back-heavy" definition I could verify is **Pat Pattison's** (patpattison.com), not Perricone's. Perricone's books use the same terms in their tables of contents, but I could not read his definition. The "octave and a fifth" range figure is still secondhand.

### Cited Findings
- **Melody and phrase definition (Perricone, Ch. 1):**
  - Melody is a succession of pitches in rhythm, and usually the part listeners remember and can perform.
  - A melodic phrase usually ends by resting, holding, or reaching a point of resolution, rhythmically and/or tonally.
  - In vocal music the phrase is tied to natural breathing places, and short phrases group into longer ones.
  - Source: [Berklee Online Take Note, excerpt of *Melody in Songwriting* Ch. 1](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/conjunct-disjunct-melody-basic-definitions/) **[verified]**
- **Conjunct vs. disjunct motion (Perricone):**
  - Conjunct motion moves by step (a 2nd). Disjunct motion moves by leap (larger than a 2nd).
  - Most vocal melodies are mostly conjunct, the most natural to sing.
  - Leaps are what usually give a melody character and a memorable profile, and a mix of the two "usually produces a good result."
  - A melody takes on character through four things: rhythmic structure, contour, tonal makeup and intervallic content.
  - Source: [same excerpt](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/conjunct-disjunct-melody-basic-definitions/) **[verified]**
- **Writing for the voice, Perricone's three checks.** He says it is "absolutely essential" that the writer sings the melody.
  1. How disjunct is it? Too many leaps make it hard or impossible to sing.
  2. Does the singer have time to breathe between phrases?
  3. Is the range too great, and does the range *within a section* change too quickly?
  - He also warns that melody written on instruments, especially piano, is often hard or impossible to sing.
  - Source: [same excerpt](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/conjunct-disjunct-melody-basic-definitions/) **[verified]**
- **Lead-sheet convention:** each syllable goes directly under its note or notes. A slur marks two or more notes on one syllable (a melisma), and an extender line marks tied or slurred last syllables. — [same excerpt](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/conjunct-disjunct-melody-basic-definitions/) **[verified]**. This is relevant to how the plugin should take in lyric-to-note alignment.
- **Motif development in practice (Perricone on his own song):**
  - He states the main motive in bar 1, repeats it in bar 2 under a different chord (Imaj7, then I7), then extends the one-bar phrase to two bars with a varied melody and a fresh chord (bVII7).
  - The A-section phrase lengths run 1, 1, 2, 4, 4 bars.
  - A phrase ending on the downbeat of bar 3 of a four-bar harmonic phrase leaves room for the singer and listener to breathe.
  - Source: [Berklee Today, Spring 2008, Perricone, "It Doesn't Get Any Better Than This"](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2355531) **[verified]**
- **Contour matching meaning (Perricone):** he made the title-line melody move upward "in agreement with the positive feelings" of the lyric. He also aimed for a strong, bluesy hook on the title and pays constant attention to title placement. — [Berklee Today 2008](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2355531) **[verified]**
- **Section contrast and tessitura (Perricone):**
  - The bridge begins on IV, not I.
  - Its rhythm and contour are more straightforward.
  - Its phrase structure (2 + 2 + 4 bars) is more uniform and predictable.
  - The A-section melody spans an octave and a sixth, while the bridge "sits mainly in the middle" of the singer's range.
  - The bridge still belongs to the song through the same key, previously heard harmonies, and the same lyric point of view.
  - Source: [Berklee Today 2008](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2355531) **[verified]**
- **Stable/unstable tones in use (Perricone):** the bridge leads back by slowing the melodic rhythm. It ends on an unstable harmony and the "unstable melody note" Ab (scale degree 4 in Eb), which resolves to G (scale degree 3) at the start of the next A. — [Berklee Today 2008](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2355531) **[verified]**. This is a concrete, verified instance of 4 resolving to 3.
- **Book coverage (TOC):** *Melody in Songwriting* has assignments on stable and unstable tones, tone tendencies, tonally open and closed phrases, and "front-heavy melodic phrases; back-heavy melodic phrases." — [Graham English wiki outline](https://wiki.grahamenglish.net/index.php/Melody_in_Songwriting:_Tools_and_Techniques_for_Writing_Hit_Songs) **[secondhand]**. *Great Songwriting Techniques* (OUP 2018) has a section "Back-heavy and front-heavy phrases" — [Scribd TOC copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/745652101/Toaz-info-Great-Songwriting-Techniques-by-Jack-Perricone-z-Liborgpdf-Pr-51eeca3b421b0608c905ac0ad442369c) **[secondhand]**
- **Front-heavy / back-heavy (verified definition, from Pattison):**
  - Phrases that start on the downbeat, or pick up to it, are front-heavy. They feel solid and stable.
  - Phrases that start after the downbeat are back-heavy. They feel in motion and unstable, and the later they start, the less stable.
  - In 4/4, phrases starting after beat 3 usually act as pickups to the next downbeat, so they count as front-heavy.
  - Source: [patpattison.com, "The Art of Phrasing"](https://www.patpattison.com/art-of-phrasing) **[verified]**. Whether Perricone's book definition is identical is unverified. The earlier KVR forum paraphrase matches Pattison's wording.
- **Range figure "about an octave and a fifth" for writing without a specific singer:** this comes only from unofficial copies of *Great Songwriting Techniques* ([idoc.pub](https://idoc.pub/documents/jack-perricone-great-songwriting-techniques-oxford-university-press-2018pdf-d4p7706ko64p)) **[secondhand]**. A targeted search found no other source for the figure. A verified Perricone example (above) uses an octave and a sixth in one section, so his guidance is clearly not a hard cap.
- **Tessitura as contrast, and "contrast within similarity":** both are still sourced only to the same unofficial GST copies **[secondhand]**. The Berklee Today article *demonstrates* both ideas: the bridge contrasts but "still needs to sound like it belongs" — [Berklee Today 2008](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2355531) **[verified]**.
- **Related Berklee pedagogy (Mark Simos, not Perricone):** phrases cadencing on structural tones of different stability; harmony fitted "after the fact"; repetition, sequence, extension and inversion. — [Berklee Today, Fall 2014](https://www.berklee.edu/berklee-today/fall-2014/composing-independent-melodies) **[not re-read]**
- **Berklee's current Songwriting: Melody course is taught by Jimmy Kachulis, not Perricone.** Its syllabus starts with "Phrase Beginnings/Stressed Words" and moves to chord-tone melodies ("One Chord Tone and Decorations", "Two Chord Tones and Passing Notes"), "Keep the Rhythm, Change the Shape", modes, and melodies against chord changes. — [Berklee Online course page](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/songwriting-melody) **[verified]**

### Inferences
- The stable tones in major are 1, 3 and 5. The unstable ones are 2, 4, 6 and 7, with tendencies 7→1, 4→3, 6→5 and 2→1/3. This is standard tonal theory, and Perricone's verified 4→3 example fits it. His full tendency table was not read, so present the rest as standard theory.
- Perricone's verified checks map directly to computable descriptors, all framed as questions:
  - leap density (the share of intervals larger than a 2nd);
  - breath gaps between phrases (rests or long holds);
  - total range;
  - range change *within* a section.
- His bridge example gives a five-way contrast checklist with primary support: starting chord, rhythmic complexity, contour, phrase-length uniformity, and tessitura.

### Gaps
- I could not read Perricone's own definitions of front-/back-heavy phrases, his contour typology, or his chord-tone vs. non-chord-tone rules. No Berklee Press preview was reachable, and archive.org has no copy.
- The "octave and a fifth" figure is still unverified.

## 2. Pat Pattison: stress-on-beat rules, function vs. content words, lyric vs. melodic phrases, phrase length and meaning

### Takeaway
Pattison's site gives a detailed, operational model, all **[verified]**:
- Match stressed syllables to stressed musical positions.
- Polysyllables carry a fixed "little melody" from the dictionary, but monosyllables are unmarked and take stress from the job they do in the sentence.
- The 4/4 strong-weak hierarchy repeats at every subdivision.
- Anticipations strengthen the beat they anticipate.
- Musical phrase divisions should match lyric idea divisions ("roadmaps").
- Phrase start position (front-/back-heavy) and placement in strong or weak bars create stability or instability.

The "traffic signs, not cargo" phrasing does **not** appear on his site. His actual metaphor is hard-working "sweaty" content words versus function words that "hold signs" and are "directing traffic."

### Cited Findings
- **Core rule:** match stressed notes with stressed syllables and unstressed with unstressed. A mismatch "breaks no mythical rules"; it just sounds unnatural and deflates the emotion of the line. — [patpattison.com, "Perfect Match"](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match) **[verified]**
- **Relative stress within a strong pair:**
  - In a 4/4 bar the first half note is stronger than the second, though both are stressed.
  - "Bridegroom" fits that bar. "Come back" (second syllable stronger) would be poor prosody there.
  - Context changes stress: "making a comeback" is stressed like "bridegroom."
  - Source: [Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match) **[verified]**
- **Polysyllables:** every word of two or more syllables has a primary stress, which gives it a "little melody." Stressed syllables are higher, louder and longer (his "incision" exercise). — [Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match); [Language and Songwriting](https://www.patpattison.com/language-and-songwriting) **[verified]**
- **Monosyllables and the "traffic" attribution:**
  - The dictionary "doesn't mark one-syllable words."
  - Monosyllables are stressed when they "have an important job to do." Nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs "get sweaty because they work hard."
  - Unstressed monosyllables "hold signs to show the workers where to go." Other unstressed words are "standing around directing traffic."
  - Source: [Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match); [Language and Songwriting](https://www.patpattison.com/language-and-songwriting) **[verified]**
  - The phrase "traffic signs, not cargo" (attributed to him by Song Cage and SongForgeAI) was not found on his pages, so treat it as a third-party paraphrase.
- **Polysyllabic function words:** "before" contains a stressed syllable but is a preposition, "a sign holder," so don't put its stress on a hard-working note. Two-syllable prepositions like "before" and "over" fit the stressed eighth-note positions on beats 2 and 4, "not too wet, not too dry." — [Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match) **[verified]**
- **Gray areas:** in strings of monosyllables, context decides. "I" may be stressed if someone else was coming home. The most important words are usually easy to spot; the gray ones are worked out "with a little trial and error." — [Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match) **[verified]**
- **Metric hierarchy:**
  - In 4/4, beats 1 and 3 are stressed and beats 2 and 4 are not.
  - With eighth notes there are four stressed positions per bar, but 1 and 3 still dominate.
  - The strong-weak pattern holds "through all of its subdivisions," down to 32nd notes.
  - Source: [Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match) **[verified]**
- **Anticipation and distance:**
  - An anticipation "adds strength to" the beat it anticipates. Anticipating a downbeat makes it the strongest position in the phrase.
  - A dotted eighth tied to a downbeat can still feel unstressed when "too far away from the downbeat."
  - A note's length can add "a little extra punch."
  - Source: [Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match) **[verified]**
- **What else creates stressed positions:** his Berklee course *Lyric Writing: Writing Lyrics to Music* lists stressed positions caused by four things. Its lessons also cover secondary stresses and "Stressed Notes on Weak Beats."
  1. isolating a note;
  2. a rest before a note;
  3. shorter note values preceding a note;
  4. anticipating a note.
  - Source: [Berklee Online course syllabus](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/lyric-writing-writing-lyrics-to-music) **[verified]**. This corrects the earlier unverified course title "Setting Your Words to Music". That title is actually a page on his site: [patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music](https://www.patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music).
- **Matching later verses:** when writing a second section to an existing melody, line up stressed and unstressed syllables "exactly the same," and also put the most important words in the same places. — [Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match) **[verified]**
- **Worked mis-setting examples ("Unconditionally" critique):**
  - "Oh no" set with "oh" on the downbeat reverses natural stress.
  - "did" on a downbeat is wrong, and the intensifier "too" is shoved onto beat 4.
  - Interrogative "what's" is stressed but relative "what's" is unstressed, so the same word changes stress with its grammar.
  - "inseCUrities" set with its secondary stresses on downbeats and its primary stress on beat 3 is wrong.
  - "the" on a downbeat is wrong, and so is the final "-ly" of "unconditionally" on a downbeat.
  - Source: [patpattison.com, "Setting Words to Music"](https://www.patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music) **[verified]**. This is a strong source of test cases for a checker.
- **Lyric phrases vs. melodic phrases ("roadmaps"):**
  - A melody that repeats a two-part phrase sets up a roadmap of two independent ideas. If the lyric's ideas break elsewhere, "the result is confusion."
  - His three fixes are: change the lyric, change the music, or repeat a word from the end of one line at the start of the next.
  - Source: [patpattison.com, "Lyric and Melodic Phrases"](https://www.patpattison.com/lyric-and-melodic-phrases) **[verified]**
  - Elsewhere he states it as: "Two musical phrases should equal 2 lyric ideas." — [Setting Words to Music](https://www.patpattison.com/setting-words-to-music) **[verified]**
- **Phrase placement and feeling:**
  - Strong and weak bars alternate. Weak-bar phrasing (the strong bar left empty) feels floaty and unstable like back-heavy phrasing, and back-heavy in a weak bar is "even floatier."
  - For lines you really mean, "look for downbeats" and strong bars. For regret or longing, try back-heavy or weak-bar phrasing.
  - Doubling phrase length while changing placement is one of his examples of section contrast.
  - Source: [The Art of Phrasing](https://www.patpattison.com/art-of-phrasing) **[verified]**
- **Line length and structure as stability:**
  - He measures line length "by the number of stressed syllables."
  - Unequal line lengths (3/3/5 stresses) feel unstable and tell the listener to keep going, while a matched pattern sets up expectations through the "Principle of Sequence."
  - Source: [patpattison.com, "Structure Creates Expectations"](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations) **[verified]**
  - His poetry course teaches odd- vs. even-numbered line groups to create tension or resolution. — [patpattison.com, Online Courses](https://www.patpattison.com/online-courses) **[verified]**. This replaces the earlier Obsidian-notes citation.
- **Variation without mis-stressing:** create rhythmic surprise by arranging natural stresses against the established pattern, not by "keeping the same meter and wrenching the words." His mantra is "Preserve the natural shape of the language." — [patpattison.com, "Rhythms and Variations"](https://www.patpattison.com/rhythms-and-variations) **[verified]**
- **Prosody definition and stable/unstable frame:**
  - Prosody is when "all elements work together to support the central message." It is his one exception to "only tools, no rules."
  - Stable vs. unstable is "the most effective vehicle" for prosody. Ask it of the song, the verse and the line.
  - Rhyme type maps onto it: perfect rhyme supports resolution, and assonance gives partial resolution.
  - Source: [Berklee Online Take Note, Pattison, "Prosody in Music and Songwriting"](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/prosody-in-music-and-songwriting/) **[verified]**
- **Critique:** Oxford Songwriting notes call some of his conclusions "very subjective." — [pdfcoffee copy](https://pdfcoffee.com/writing-better-lyrics-notes-on-pattisonx27s-book-oxford-songwriting-pdf-free.html) **[not re-read]**

### Inferences
- Pattison's model is effectively a three-tier stress lexicon:
  1. dictionary stress inside polysyllables;
  2. part-of-speech-driven stress on monosyllables;
  3. a downgrade for polysyllabic function words ("before", "over", "into").
- His "stressed positions" list (isolation, preceding rest, shorter notes before, anticipation, length) means metric strength must be computed from rhythm context, not just beat number.
- "Roadmap" matching is checkable: detect melodic phrase boundaries (rests, long holds, repeated melodic units) and compare them with lyric clause and punctuation boundaries. Flag any lyric clause that crosses a melodic boundary as a question.

### Gaps
- His Berklee course content beyond the syllabus titles was not readable.
- No Pattison text gives a numeric rule for phrase length vs. meaning. His guidance is qualitative: stable vs. unstable, and matching roadmaps.

## 4. Textsetting research (Halle & Lerdahl, Hayes, Hayes & Kaun, Temperley, Smith, Ryan) and the rules a stress checker should implement; limits of CMU stress

### Takeaway
Hayes's full paper ([verified]) gives an implementable, ranked constraint set. It matched native speakers' preferred settings 70.6% of the time, against 23.1% for Halle & Lerdahl's rule-based algorithm. It shows "strategic sacrifices," trading stress match for avoiding lapses, are ubiquitous. The search space is small enough to enumerate by machine. Hayes & Kaun add multi-level stress, phonological-phrase effects and duration matching. Smith (MTO 2024, [verified]) adds three points:
- Phrasal stress is "the only way" to explain mismatches on monosyllables.
- Word-stress mismatches are generally worse than phrase-emphasis ones.
- Syncopation and prosodic dissonance are different things.

Smith also argues against strict numeric scoring. Temperley's anticipation theory is still known only through citations, but Pattison independently confirms that anticipations count as the following strong beat.

### Cited Findings
- **Halle & Lerdahl (1993), as summarized by Hayes:** the first explicit model of productive textsetting. Its Syllabic Distribution Algorithm maps stressed syllables left to right onto strong (S) positions, then fills stressless syllables. — [Hayes 2005 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf) **[verified, secondhand for H&L itself]**
- **Hayes's four competing goals:**
  - (a) stress on strong positions;
  - (b) avoid long lapses, meaning stretches where no syllable starts;
  - (c) avoid extreme syllable compression;
  - (d) align phonological phrase boundaries with line boundaries.
  - Many good settings put stressed syllables in weaker positions to avoid a lapse, sacrificing (a) for (b).
  - Source: [Hayes, Textsetting page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting) **[verified]**
- **Hayes's ten ranked constraints (OT), from strictest to weakest:**
  1. *FILL EXTRA WEAK
  2. *RUN-ON
  3. *LAPSE (three empty grid positions in a row)
  4. *NULL IN S
  5. *STRESSLESS IN S
  6. *STRESSED IN W
  7. *STRESSLESS IN W
  8. STRONG IS LONG
  9. *STRESS IN M
  10. *NULL IN M
  - A stressed syllable is "far" better in a medium (M) than a weak (W) position.
  - Empty positions belong preferably in W.
  - The ranking came from a greedy search, and other rankings give the same outputs.
  - Source: [Hayes 2005 PDF, §5](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf) **[verified]**
- **Accuracy and search size:**
  - The OT grammar matched the consultants' favourite setting in 70.6% of lines, against 23.1% for the Syllabic Distribution Algorithm. Hayes calls 70.6% "not good enough."
  - On a 16-position grid, no line has more than about 13,000 possible settings, so machine search is easy.
  - Source: [Hayes 2005 PDF, §6 and n.9](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf) **[verified]**
  - Discrepancy: the PDF says the grammar ran on "364 lines," while the web page describes the downloadable dataset as 346 lines. — [Hayes Textsetting page](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting) **[verified]**
- **Hayes's proposed improvements:**
  - Stress is multi-level and should be judged relatively, by its "ups" and "downs."
  - Word and phrase boundaries have metrical effects.
  - Duration matching: grid positions track a syllable's natural duration, which depends on pre-boundary lengthening and syllable weight.
  - Speakers are often ambivalent between settings, so a model should output weighted alternatives (stochastic OT).
  - Hayes doubts singers alter stresses to fit a tune; they "deal with the stresses as they stand."
  - Source: [Hayes 2005 PDF, §3.5 and §7](https://brucehayes.org/Textsetting/HayesTextsettingAsConstraintConflict.pdf) **[verified]**
- **Hayes & Kaun (1996):**
  - The corpus is 670 lines of English folksong collected by Cecil Sharp. The Hayes web page says the longer dataset is 640 lines, so the two figures conflict.
  - Stress was annotated on four levels (1–4), plus weight for stressed syllables and five levels of prosodic structure: Word, Clitic Group, Phonological Phrase, Intonational Phrase, Utterance.
  - Transcribers agreed on 99.6% of metrical positions.
  - A setting can win "despite its lexical stress mismatch" when all its rivals do worse elsewhere.
  - The authors allow that function words may be phrased "in a flexible manner."
  - Source: [Hayes & Kaun 1996 PDF](https://brucehayes.org/papers/HayesAndKaunMetrics1996.pdf) **[verified]**
- **Smith, "Prosodic Dissonance," *Music Theory Online* 30.2 (2024):**
  - Definition: any conflict between the prosodic features of the lyric and its musical rendition. This covers syllabic vs. durational or registral stress, spoken vs. sung phrases, and spoken vs. sung intonation.
  - It can interact with rhyme, vowel shape, parallelism and syncopation.
  - Her six-step method: (1) spoken prosody, (2) sung prosody, (3) mismatches, (4) surrounding context, (5) alternate pronunciations or hearings, (6) implications.
  - Source: [MTO 30.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.2/mto.24.30.2.smith.html) **[verified]**
  - Further points:
    - Phrasal stress is "the only way" to account for dissonance on monosyllables.
    - Word-stress mismatches usually upend the phrase, while extra emphasis on a word may just change meaning.
    - The less common a word's default phrasal stress, the more dissonant emphasizing it sounds.
    - She does not advocate strict quantification, because stress varies by dialect and speaker.
    - Source: [MTO §2.1–2.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.2/mto.24.30.2.smith.html) **[verified]**
  - **Syncopation vs. dissonance:**
    - Syncopation is text misaligned with the *meter*. Prosodic dissonance is text stress misaligned with the *melody's* own accents (durational and registral).
    - Example: Billie Eilish's "bad guy" anticipates many beats yet isn't dissonant, because stronger syllables get longer notes.
    - A repeated step-down melodic figure can shift emphasis onto a weak syllable ("cul-TURE" in Lady Gaga's "Applause").
    - Source: [MTO §4.3–4.6](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.2/mto.24.30.2.smith.html) **[verified]**
  - **Section pattern:** in her analyses most dissonances fall in verses, with choruses more consonant. She links this to Temperley's (2007) "loose verse, tight chorus." — [MTO §5.14, §6.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.2/mto.24.30.2.smith.html) **[verified]**
- **Temperley (1999), "Syncopation in Rock" (*Popular Music*):**
  - He argues text setting is the clearest evidence of rock syncopation, with displacement governed by constraints.
  - Known via [Smith MTO 2024](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.2/mto.24.30.2.smith.html) **[verified citation]** and [Language Log](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/~myl/languagelog/archives/005154.html) **[not re-read]**. The original was not read.
  - A rock-melody corpus defines syncopation as accented notes just before strong beats. — [ResearchGate / scite listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332467270_Anticipatory_Syncopation_in_Rock_A_Corpus_Study) **[secondhand]**
  - The de Clercq & Temperley rock corpus annotates lyric stress as 0 (unstressed), 1 (primary), 2 (secondary) and 3 (non-word), under a CC BY 4.0 licence. — [Rock Corpus](https://rockcorpus.midside.com/lyrics_stress.html) **[not re-read]**
- **Ryan (2022):** in a study of 2,371 English pop songs, textsetting is sensitive to natural duration and syllable weight. — [Cambridge abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-language-and-linguistics/article/abs/syllable-weight-and-natural-duration-in-textsetting-popular-music-in-english/50F8885341914C37D6F1BD22A632A0B6) **[not re-read; host blocked]**
- **CMU dictionary limits:**
  - Stress is marked 0/1/2 per vowel, in citation form.
  - Homographs such as RE-cord/re-CORD depend on part of speech.
  - It has no phrasal stress, no function-word destressing (G2P libraries add a separate destress step for function words), and no dialect variation.
  - Sources: [Text.Pronounce docs](https://hackage.haskell.org/package/pronounce-1.2.0.0/docs/Text-Pronounce.html); [piper-plus-g2p source](https://docs.rs/crate/piper-plus-g2p/0.4.0/source/src/english.rs) **[not re-read]**
  - This agrees with Pattison's observation that the dictionary "doesn't mark one-syllable words" ([Perfect Match](https://www.patpattison.com/perfect-match), **[verified]**), and with Smith's point that monosyllable dissonance needs phrasal stress ([MTO](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.2/mto.24.30.2.smith.html), **[verified]**).

### Inferences
- **A checker should score, not judge.** Hayes's ranked constraints plus enumeration make it feasible to compute the best-scoring setting of the user's line against their rhythm grid, and to report *how far* the user's setting is from it. A pass/fail verdict per syllable is the wrong output. Smith's caution against strict quantification argues for showing relative, contextual flags, not a single number.
- **Metric strength should combine four sources:**
  - beat level (S/M/W, with the hierarchy at every subdivision);
  - anticipation, shifting an onset just before a strong beat onto that beat (Pattison; Temperley as cited);
  - local duration ("strong is long"; Pattison's "length gives… punch");
  - registral or pitch-peak accent and isolation by rests (Pattison's course; Smith).
- **Monosyllables need phrasal stress, not CMU stress.** A part-of-speech plus function-word layer is the minimum. Smith and Pattison both show that the same word ("what's") changes stress with its grammatical role.

### Gaps
- Temperley's original article, Ryan 2022's full text and the Halle & Lerdahl original were not read.
- No source gives validated numeric weights for pop (as opposed to folk) textsetting.

## 5. Key choice by tessitura

### Takeaway
Tessitura is the most-used part of a range, its "heart," not its extremes ([verified]). Perricone's verified example shows a section-level tessitura difference used deliberately for contrast, with a wide A section against a middle-register bridge. Practical key-choice advice (test the highest chorus line, use the best-tone ceiling, capo only raises pitch) still rests on first-pass, non-primary sources.

### Cited Findings
- **Tessitura definition:**
  - Tessitura is decided not by the extremes of a range but by the share of the range most used, the "heart" of a range.
  - It also concerns how the pitches are arranged, not only their span.
  - Source: [Wikipedia: Tessitura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessitura) **[verified]**
- **Perricone's contrast example:** the A section has very low and high notes (an octave and a sixth). The bridge sits mainly in the middle of the singer's range. — [Berklee Today 2008](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2355531) **[verified]**
- **Range within a section:** Perricone asks whether the range within a section "change[s] too quickly." — [Berklee Take Note excerpt](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/conjunct-disjunct-melody-basic-definitions/) **[verified]**
- **Best-tone ceiling:** the ceiling that matters is the highest note sung with the tone quality you want — [Audio Masterclass](https://www.audiomasterclass.com/blog/how-to-choose-the-best-key-for-your-song). Test the chorus's highest line and adjust the key — [Secrets of Songwriting](https://www.secretsofsongwriting.com/2019/01/29/the-human-voice-and-key-choice/). Both **[not re-read]**.
- **Capo:** a capo only transposes upward, and high capo positions can sound thin. — [Good Guitarist](https://goodguitarist.com/make-songs-easier-to-sing-on-guitar); [Riffhard](https://www.riffhard.com/how-to-find-your-singing-key-on-the-guitar) **[not re-read]**
- **Typical singable range:** about an octave for "practically anyone"; an octave to an octave and a third for most singers. — [Secrets of Songwriting](https://www.secretsofsongwriting.com/2014/02/28/how-large-a-range-should-a-good-melody-encompass/) **[search snippet]**. Perricone's "octave and a fifth" figure is **[secondhand]** (§1).

### Inferences
- Rank keys by the duration-weighted pitch centre (tessitura) against the singer's comfortable zone, subject to two limits: peak at or below the best-tone ceiling, and lowest note at or above the comfortable floor. This follows the "heart of the range" definition but is my method, not a cited standard.
- Report tessitura per section, so the user sees intended contrast (Perricone) separately from key fit.

### Gaps
- I found no vocal-pedagogy source that quantifies tessitura with a duration-weighted mean.
- Vowel/singability findings (first-pass §4) were not re-verified in this pass. The allowed hosts held no relevant primary source. Those findings stay as informal pedagogy, unattributed to the three authors. Smith notes vowel shape interacts with prosodic dissonance ([MTO](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.2/mto.24.30.2.smith.html), **[verified]**) but gives no rules.

## Implications for the plugin

### A. Melody coaching questions (the plugin asks; it never writes notes)
1. **Phrase endings and stability:** "Which phrases end on 1/3/5 (arrival) and which on 2/4/6/7 (open)? Does the bridge end on an unstable tone that the next section resolves?" Perricone's verified 4→3 example supports this.
2. **Singability (Perricone's three verified checks):** "How many leaps? Can the singer breathe between phrases? Is the range wide, and does it jump quickly *within* a section?"
3. **Contour and meaning:** "Does the title line's direction agree with its feeling?" Perricone raised the title melody to match positive feeling.
4. **Motif:** "What's your one-bar idea? Do you repeat it, re-harmonize it, extend it?" Perricone's 1, 1, 2, 4, 4 bar unfolding is the model.
5. **Phrase placement (Pattison, verified):** "Does each line start on the downbeat (front-heavy, stable) or after it (back-heavy, unstable)? Strong bar or weak bar? Does that match how sure the line is?" Attribute these terms to Pattison. Mention that Perricone uses them too, without quoting his definition.
6. **Roadmaps (Pattison, verified):** "Your melody splits this line into two phrases. Does the lyric split there too?" The fixes are to change the lyric, change the music, or repeat a word across the break.
7. **Sectional contrast (Perricone, verified bridge example):** starting chord, rhythmic plainness, contour, phrase uniformity, and tessitura (middle vs. wide). Ask what still ties the section to the song: key, familiar chords, point of view.
8. **Harmony prompt (Webb, interviews only):** "If the melody feels stuck, change what's under it." Do not attribute range or hook rules to Webb.

### B. Prosody script rules
1. **Metric strength model:**
   - 4/4 hierarchy: beat 1 > beat 3 > beats 2/4 > off-beat eighths > sixteenths, repeating at every subdivision (Pattison).
   - Add strength for an anticipation (an onset an eighth or sixteenth before a strong beat counts as that beat), for isolation or a preceding rest, for shorter notes before it, for long duration, and for a local pitch peak (Pattison's course syllabus; Smith).
   - A tie over a strong beat from far away does *not* inherit it (Pattison).
2. **Stress lexicon, three tiers:**
   1. CMU primary/secondary/unstressed inside polysyllables. This is the high-confidence check.
   2. Monosyllables classed by part of speech as content words (stress-preferring) or function words (stress-neutral). Ignore CMU's citation stress for them.
   3. Polysyllabic function words ("before", "over", "into", "about") demoted, so their stressed syllable fits M positions, not S (Pattison).
   - Special cases: intensifiers ("too", "so", "very") and negatives get stressed. Interrogative wh-words are stressed and relative ones unstressed, so use part-of-speech or parse context (Pattison).
3. **Flag order (soft, ranked, after Hayes):**
   1. A lexical stress inversion inside a word, such as a 0 on S while that word's 1 sits on W ("inseCUrities", "-ly" on a downbeat). Strongest flag.
   2. A content word on W while an adjacent function word holds S ("the" on the downbeat, "blink" on beat 4).
   3. A lapse of three or more empty positions within a line.
   4. Extreme compression (syllables on extra-weak positions).
   5. A reduced syllable on a long held note.
   - A stressed syllable on M is a minor issue; on W it is a significant one (Hayes).
4. **Score against the best alternative:** for short lines, enumerate the possible settings on the user's grid (Hayes: at most about 13,000 for 16 positions) with the ranked constraints, and show where the user's setting loses. Say this is a model with about 70% agreement on folk data, not ground truth.
5. **Don't confuse syncopation with mismatch (Smith):** if a stressed syllable is off the beat but gets the melody's local duration or pitch accent, treat it as syncopation and don't flag it.
6. **Context and emphasis:** always let the user mark contrastive focus ("*I* came home"). Hayes and Pattison both allow meaning to shift monosyllable stress, and Smith says unusual emphasis is dissonant only when semantically irrelevant.
7. **Matching verses (Pattison):** compare corresponding lines for stress pattern *and* for important-word placement, not just syllable count. Allow ±1 syllable for pickups and elisions. Prompt "1 or 2 syllables as sung?" for words like fire, hour, every, family, our, being, really.
8. **Roadmap check:** detect melodic phrase breaks (rests, long holds, repeated melodic units). Flag lyric clauses that run across a break, and breaks that split a grammatical unit (Pattison's "Unconditionally" example).
9. **Structure descriptors (Pattison):** report line length *in stresses* and odd vs. even line counts per section as stability descriptors. Do not prescribe.
10. **Section summary (Smith):** report where flags cluster. Verses tolerate more tension than choruses in her analyses, so frame chorus flags as more important.

### C. Key-fitting rules
1. Collect the singer's comfortable low, comfortable high and best-tone ceiling.
2. Compute per section: the lowest note, the highest note, and the tessitura as a duration-weighted centre. This is the "heart" of the range (Wikipedia); the weighting method is my own inference.
3. Keep only keys where the peak is at or below the best-tone ceiling and the low is at or above the floor. Rank them by how well the tessitura sits in the comfortable zone. Warn when lowering the key for a chorus or bridge peak pushes verse lows out.
4. Report range per section and a "changes too quickly within a section" flag (Perricone, verified).
5. Guitar: give the capo fret and open-shape key. Note that a capo only raises pitch and that high positions can thin the tone (non-primary sources).

### D. Limits the plugin must state
- CMU provides citation lexical stress only. Monosyllable and phrasal stress come from a part-of-speech heuristic and can be wrong. Dialects differ (Smith).
- Textsetting constraints are violable. Hayes's best grammar matched native speakers about 70% of the time, and on folk verse, not pop. Treat a flag as a question.
- Syncopation is idiomatic in pop, and anticipations belong to the following beat. The checker may still misread groove-driven settings.
