## 1. Pattison: form follows function, stability, and structure as expectation

### Takeaway
Confirmed from his own text. Pattison treats "form follows function" as two principles:
- **Prosody:** form should support meaning.
- **Contrast:** sections that look alike should do the same job, and sections that look different should do different jobs.

So all verses share a form, and the chorus must change rhyme scheme, phrase lengths, number of phrases or phrase rhythms, "maybe all four". A second article shows how line lengths (counted in stresses) and rhyme set up expectations that a song can fulfil, deny or interrupt to mirror its emotion.

### Cited Findings
- **Two senses of the principle.** Pattison derives two principles from "form follows function" **[verified]** — [Pattison, "Building The Perfect Beast: Form Follows Function," StrongWriter](https://strongwriter.com/?p=288):
  - **Prosody:** design form from what you want it to do.
  - **Contrast:** "same design, same function; different design, different function."
- **Verses share form; the chorus changes it.** Verses all develop "the plot, characters, or situations", so they share a form. Moving to a chorus function ("commentary, summary") should change the rhyme scheme, phrase lengths, number of phrases, or phrase rhythms. He treats the classroom rules "all verses should have the same rhyme scheme" and "change the rhyme scheme at the chorus" as consequences of this principle **[verified]** — [StrongWriter](https://strongwriter.com/?p=288)
- **Design tools** are "phrase lengths, rhythms and rhymes". For intense passages he suggests close-together rhymes (end and internal) and short phrases. His example is a verse with an accelerating run of consecutive rhymes **[verified]** — [StrongWriter](https://strongwriter.com/?p=288)
- **Contrast must start immediately.** His key heuristic is that the first phrase of a second section "must make a difference immediately". His "Southern Comfort" case study counts stresses: the verse and chorus are both Common Meter (4/3 and 3/3 stresses) with the same number of phrases and alternating rhymes, so there is "no contrast". He says a bridge that contrasts this late arrives too late, because listeners have "wandered off" **[verified]** — [StrongWriter](https://strongwriter.com/?p=288)
- **Fix by re-proportioning.** His rewrite balances six verse lines against two instead of two 4-line Common Meter halves. He then cites Beth Nielsen Chapman's "Years":
  - the verse lines linger and are relaxed;
  - the chorus speeds up with shorter phrases;
  - the final line "And they go so fast" takes one bar instead of the expected span.

  So contrast supports meaning **[verified]** — [StrongWriter](https://strongwriter.com/?p=288)
- **Structure creates expectations.** In "Structure Creates Expectations" he analyses Chanelle Davis's "Valentine" **[verified]** — [Pattison, "Structure Creates Expectations"](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations):
  - His **Principle of Sequence**: once line 3 mimics line 1, we expect line 4 to mimic line 2.
  - Line lengths of 3/3/5 stresses "feel unstable and tell us to keep going".
  - Rhyme can give mixed "stop/go" signals that contradict the line lengths.
  - A short rhyming close after a long build feels "a little disappointing", which suits a song about a disappointing Valentine.
- **Interrupted bridge as meaning** (same "Valentine" analysis) **[verified]** — [patpattison.com](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations):
  - The 3-line bridge (3/3/4 stresses, rhymed aab) "is screaming to be matched" but stops and jumps back to verse 1.
  - Its unrhymed third line "targets" sonically to the title word on the return. He cross-references "strategic positions" and "sonic targeting" in *The Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure*.
  - Repeating verse 1 instead of writing a new verse 3 creates a character stuck going "backwards time and again".
- **The book covers these topics.** The table of contents of *Writing Better Lyrics* (2nd ed., 2009) includes these chapters **[verified: TOC only]** — [Internet Archive record](https://archive.org/details/writingbetterlyr0000patt):
  - "Verse development and power positions"
  - "Spotlighting with common meter"
  - "Managing couplets"
  - "Prosody: structure as film score"
  - "Understanding motion"
  - "Form follows function: building the perfect beast"
  - "The great balancing act"
  - "Song forms: (im)potent packages" I and II

  This confirms that "power positions" is Pattison's own term. Its definition (commonly glossed as the first and last lines of a section) was not read in his text **[definition secondhand]**.
- **Odd vs even line counts:** even counts feel settled and odd counts feel unstable. The ABAB vs ABBA stability ranking is also still only in third-party book summaries **[secondhand]** — [sobrief summary](https://sobrief.com/books/writing-better-lyrics). The verified "Valentine" analysis is consistent with it: unmatched line lengths and unmatched rhymes create forward pull. It frames the idea as unmet *matching* (sequence), not parity as such.
- **Paul Simon bridge line-length examples** ("Still Crazy…", "Train in the Distance") **[secondhand; soundonsound.com blocked by proxy]** — [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/pat-pattison-writing-better-lyrics)

### Inferences
- Pattison's operational unit is **stresses per line** plus **rhyme pairing**, not syllables. An analyzer should count stressed syllables per line, detect matched pairs (sequence), and flag unmatched lines as "open".
- A machine check follows directly from the "first phrase must differ" rule: compare the first line of the chorus with the verse norm (stress count, rhythm, rhyme sound, line length). If it matches, warn that contrast arrives late or not at all.
- In Pattison, asymmetry is a meaning tool, not decoration. The "Valentine" bridge is deliberately incomplete because the story is incomplete.

### Gaps
- *Songwriting: Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure* (Berklee Press) was not readable. Its exact definitions of "strategic positions" and "sonic targeting" are known here only through Pattison's cross-reference.

## 2. Perricone (Great Songwriting Techniques, OUP 2018): contrast, prechorus, bridge, forms

### Takeaway
Still mostly unverified. No primary text of *Great Songwriting Techniques* could be read: archive.org has no copy, and Berklee pages carry only an excerpt from his earlier *Melody in Songwriting*. The prechorus and bridge claims below remain secondhand. Berklee faculty material by Andrea Stolpe, now verified, independently supports the same contrast logic.

### Cited Findings
- **Bibliographic details:** OUP, 2018, 379 pp. — [Cambridge *Popular Music* review listing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/popular-music/article/abs/great-songwriting-techniques-by-jack-perricone-new-york-oxford-university-press-2018-379-pp-isbn-9780199967674/4FA19C37F3C5FA3D7038D4F0E69C35F7)
- **Prechorus** builds tonal or rhythmic tension, moves away from tonic and avoids a tonic cadence **[secondhand; unofficial upload]** — [Scribd upload](https://www.scribd.com/document/745652101/Toaz-info-Great-Songwriting-Techniques-by-Jack-Perricone-z-Liborgpdf-Pr-51eeca3b421b0608c905ac0ad442369c)
- **Bridge** is chiefly contrast, made from several devices while keeping some A material. It matters less after repeated verse/chorus cycles **[secondhand]** — same source
- **Perricone's own Berklee Press text** (*Melody in Songwriting*, ch. 1) **[verified]** — [Berklee Online Take Note excerpt](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/conjunct-disjunct-melody-basic-definitions/):
  - A melodic phrase "usually encompasses a complete musical statement" and ends by resting, holding or resolving, tied to breath.
  - Most vocal melody is conjunct, but leaps give a melody "a memorable profile".
  - Writers should sing their melodies and check range, breath room and range changes within a section.
- **Stolpe on bridges** (Berklee Online) **[verified]** — [Stolpe, "Learn How to Use Contrast, Character, and Clarity to Strengthen Your Song's Bridge"](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/writing-bridges-for-your-songs-can-be-much-easier/):
  - **Start from dynamics.** If the second chorus was the peak, the bridge can extend it, exceed it or drop below it.
  - **To exceed it,** use a new or borrowed chord (she lists ♭III, ♭VII, II), faster chord changes, higher pitches, wider intervals, shorter notes, or anticipated downbeats.
  - **To drop below it,** use slower harmonic rhythm or a single chord, a much lower vocal, or lots of rests.
  - **Lyrically,** the bridge can look to the future or show the character's challenge or transformation.
  - **If a bridge won't come,** the verse/chorus may lack a clear idea or a repeated motif ("hard to contrast with a musical piece whose parts already contrast").
  - **Sometimes the best bridge is a partial repeat** of the chorus or pre-chorus.
- **Stolpe on contrast within sections** ("Hard Place", H.E.R.) **[verified]** — [Stolpe, Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/songwriters-toolbox-3-lessons-h-e-r-s-hard-place-can-teach-us-about-songwriting/):
  - **Verse:** one-bar phrases starting after the downbeat, short notes, non-chord-tone starts (uncertainty).
  - **Pre-chorus:** downbeat starts, long notes, chord-tone starts, one four-bar phrase (security).
  - **Chorus:** two-bar phrases starting before the downbeat on non-chord tones.
  - Phrase length, phrase position and chord-tone vs non-chord-tone starts are her named contrast levers.
- **Course titles:** Berklee Online's *Lyric Writing 2* lists lessons on "Structural Contrast", "The Prechorus" and "Tools That Transition" (titles only) — [Berklee Online course page](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/lyric-writing-2)

### Inferences
- Even without Perricone's text, the Berklee school's contrast levers are now concrete and checkable per section: phrase length, phrase start position relative to the downbeat, chord-tone vs non-chord-tone phrase starts, register, harmonic rhythm, and a borrowed or new chord in the bridge.

### Gaps
- Perricone's forms catalogue, his exact prechorus wording, and whether he uses "lift" for the prechorus remain unverified. The book needs to be read directly (print or an authorised e-book).

## 3. Blume: hook types, title placement, time to chorus; commercial timing data

### Takeaway
Blume's book structure is now verified from the 2004 table of contents. It has explicit sections on song structure, catchy titles, "making it obvious where the title goes without the lyric", signature licks, symmetrical phrases, nonsense syllables, modulations and "magic moments". His column-level claims (BMI) remain secondhand because bmi.com was blocked. Léveillé Gauvin's study design is now confirmed in more detail. Its percentages still come from press coverage.

### Cited Findings
- **Blume, *6 Steps to Songwriting Success* (rev. ed., 2004), TOC items** **[verified: TOC only]** — [Internet Archive record](https://archive.org/details/6stepstosongwrit0000blum_i5d1):
  - "Identifying the most successful song structures and why they work"
  - "Developing great song ideas and catchy titles" / "Keeping a hook book"
  - "Writing the big payoff lines"
  - "Making it obvious where the title goes without the lyric"
  - "Altering the range, varying the rhythms, and inserting pauses"
  - "Writing symmetrical phrases"
  - "Learning to use nonsense syllables"
  - "Unexpected note or chord" ("magic moments")
  - "Using signature licks"
  - "Incorporating modulations"
  - "Writing the dreaded second verse"

  So Blume treats the melody itself as a signal of title placement, and treats symmetry as a default to vary.
- **Title craft from Blume's class:** he usually writes the title first; the title is almost always in the chorus; the verse sets up the title so the chorus feels like the answer **[secondhand transcription]** — [TAXI Transmitter](https://taxi.com/transmitter/2411/why-my-song-got-20-placements-with-hit-songwriter-jason-blume)
- **BMI columns** **[secondhand; bmi.com not reachable]** — [BMI hooks column (mirror)](https://www.wearemusicnation.ae/en/knowledge-hub/34/hooks:-how-many-are-enough):
  - signature lick = an instrumental hook distinct from the vocal melody, typically in the intro, turnaround or outro;
  - rhythmic hooks (e.g., the "Believer" drum pattern);
  - hooks can sit in any section;
  - title placement varies by genre.
- **Léveillé Gauvin, "Drawing listener attention in popular music: Testing five musical features arising from the theory of attention economy,"** *Musicae Scientiae* 22(3): 291–304 (online 2017, print 2018). It covers 303 US top-10 singles from 1986–2015. The five features are **[secondhand: catalogue + press coverage; paper not read]** — [KB catalogue](https://ibl.kb.nl/article/0000000110640/22/3/4/lang/uk); [Ohio State news release](https://news.osu.edu/has-music-streaming-killed-the-instrumental-intro/); [PRS M Magazine](https://prsformusic.com/m-magazine/news/streaming-making-pop-songs-faster-shorter-new-study-reveals):
  1. number of words in the title
  2. main tempo
  3. time before the voice enters
  4. time before the title is first sung
  5. lyrical self-focus

  Reported results:
  - **Voice entry:** intros fell from about 20 seconds in the mid-1980s to about 5 seconds; the "78%" figure comes from coverage.
  - **Title timing:** the title arrives earlier.
  - **Tempo:** songs got faster.
  - **Titles:** they got shorter.
  - **Self-focus:** no clear trend.

  Exact figures differ slightly between reports.
- **Time to first chorus** (median ~32 s in 1958–69, rising into the 1980s; 2024 number ones ~45 s) **[secondhand, industry blog citing Hit Songs Deconstructed]** — [Song Cage](https://songcage.com/blog/how-long-should-a-song-be/)
- **Historical song length:** median length in Summach's Top-20 corpus rose from about 150 s (early 1960s) to 250 s (late 1980s). Prechorus songs were no longer than verse-chorus songs without one **[verified]** — [Summach, MTO 17.3, ¶25](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- **Chorus-first openings:** Stroud notes that recent tracks increasingly put the chorus first (citing Ensign 2016) **[verified mention]** — [Stroud, MTO 28.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.2/mto.22.28.2.stroud.html)

### Inferences
- "Make it obvious where the title goes without the lyric" is a melodic test the plugin can run. The title line should be a melodic or rhythmic outlier (peak, long note, rhythmic break) or sit in a strategic position (first or last line of the chorus).
- Treat time-to-title and time-to-chorus norms as context-dependent advice, not rules.

### Gaps
- Blume's numbered hook taxonomy and any explicit "first chorus within X seconds" rule could not be read in the book itself. Léveillé Gauvin's paper (DOI 10.1177/1029864917698010) was not read in full.

## 4. Common forms: types, when each fits, each section's job

### Takeaway
The verified scholarly core is a three-family taxonomy plus sub-types:
- strophic (AAA, often with a refrain);
- AABA / 32-bar;
- verse-chorus, sub-typed by Covach as simple vs contrasting, with compound AABA as the large-scale grouping.

Temperley argues that virtually all verse-chorus songs are compound AABA. By the early-to-mid 1980s verse-chorus dominated the charts. OMT supplies precise section-function definitions, including the "climb".

### Cited Findings
- **Three dominant song types.** Summach finds strophic, AABA and verse-chorus dominated the Billboard Top 20 from the late 1950s "and they still dominate today" **[verified]** — [Summach ¶6](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- **Verse-chorus dominance.**
  - Summach's dissertation is reported as showing roughly 90% verse-chorus Top-20 structures by 1985, and verse-chorus forms are "arguably all" #1 hits by 1982 **[verified as de Clercq's report of Summach 2012]** — [de Clercq, MTO 23.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.de_clercq.html)
  - Temperley: simple verse and AABA became much less common after 1970 and are used mostly as nostalgic references **[verified quote of Temperley 2018, 151–2, in Hudson]** — [Hudson, MTO 27.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.1/mto.21.27.1.hudson.html)
- **Covach vs Temperley.**
  - Covach defines compound AABA as a subtype of verse-chorus; Temperley says the categories should be identical because virtually all verse-chorus songs are compound AABA **[verified via Hudson]** — [Hudson ¶2.0.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.1/mto.21.27.1.hudson.html)
  - Covach is quoted: in compound AABA "each A section contains at least one verse and a chorus" **[verified quote in de Clercq]** — [de Clercq](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.de_clercq.html)
  - Covach's "simple verse-chorus" means verse and chorus share the same harmonic content **[verified via de Clercq]**
  - Covach's five-type list (simple verse, simple verse-chorus, contrasting verse-chorus, AABA, compound AABA) is from the primer, which was not reachable **[secondhand]** — [Covach 2005 PDF](http://personal.kent.edu/~sbirch/Theory/21341%20CMT/Form%20in%20Rock%20Music.pdf)
- **Temperley's default verse-chorus layout** (2018, 153): two verse-chorus pairs, then usually an instrumental, a contrasting bridge or both, then a third pair or just a chorus, often with an extra final chorus **[verified quote in Hudson]**
  - Short AABA forms extend as AABABA, while compound AABA rarely does.
  - The final compound A is often abridged or altered, so Hudson proposes the label "compound AABA′".
  - In a metal corpus of 195 tracks, 87% are compound AABA or close variants **[verified]** — [Hudson](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.1/mto.21.27.1.hudson.html)
  - Stephenson calls the form "Verse-Chorus-Bridge form" because the bridge is a default **[verified via Hudson]**
- **Strophic and AABA (OMT)** **[verified]** — [OMT, "AABA Form and Strophic Form"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/aaba-form/):
  - Strophic = repeated strophes, more common in blues, early hip-hop and early rock'n'roll.
  - A strophe presents the primary lyric and music and gives "a point at which the song might satisfyingly end".
  - srdc is "by far the most common" strophe pattern; blues is the common three-part pattern.
  - The AABA bridge withholds A, emphasises non-tonic harmony, often ends on V, and must be followed by A. In verse-chorus songs the bridge has more freedom to contrast without building toward the chorus return.
  - An AABA song normally runs a full AABA, then repeats all of it or just BA, with no new lyrics after the first cycle.
- **Verse-chorus (OMT)** **[verified]** — [OMT, "Verse-Chorus Form"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/verse-chorus-form/):
  - **Order:** verse → prechorus → chorus → postchorus is fixed; together they make a cycle, and not all need be present.
  - **Verses** are lyric-variant and tend to begin on tonic. They were harmonically closed until the 1960s and increasingly open afterwards.
  - **Choruses** are lyric-invariant and carry the title or lyrical hook. Their heightened intensity means "a more dense or active instrumental texture; prominent background vocals; and/or a higher register melody" (quoting Summach 2012).
  - **The bridge** usually appears once, before the last (pre)chorus, *replacing* the verse and/or prechorus in that cycle. It may be absent.
  - **A postchorus** only closes the cycle (after Spicer 2011).
  - **A climb** is a one-phrase, prechorus-function ending of a verse (e.g., "Come On Eileen").
- **Refrain vs chorus, and module size.** A refrain is a lyric-invariant passage within a section, usually a tail refrain at the end, sometimes a head refrain. Modules typically span 8–24 bars, so a 4-bar invariant passage is a refrain, not a chorus. A new module cannot begin until the current pattern completes **[verified]** — [Summach ¶7–8](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html); [OMT intro chapter](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/intro-to-form-in-popular-music/)
- **Auxiliary sections and closing rhetoric (OMT)** **[verified]** — [OMT intro](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/intro-to-form-in-popular-music/); [OMT AABA](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/aaba-form/):
  - **Intros** are short, mostly instrumental and preview core material, often by layering.
  - **Outros** reuse earlier material and lose energy; a **coda** brings new material.
  - **Closing rhetoric** includes immediate repetition of a core section, thinning texture, late-song intensification, fadeout, and finally closing an open section harmonically.
- **Through-composed forms (Osborn 2011, MTO 17.3)** **[verified]** — [Osborn, "Understanding Through-Composition in Post-Rock, Math-Metal, and Other Post-Millennial Rock Genres"](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.osborn.html):
  - **Definition:** no "discrete, thematically identifiable section is recapitulated".
  - **Climax placement:** he observes that the most memorable part, the climax, is often withheld until the ending to give closure.
  - **Context:** the article links specific through-composed types (e.g., One-Part and Multi-Part Polythematic) to experimental rock genres. "Independent choruses" and "independent verses" also occur.
- **Terminal climax (Osborn 2013, *Music Theory Spectrum* 35/1: 23–47).** Common in post-1990 rock: a thematically independent climax appears once at the song's end and balances against the chorus. There are two-part, three-part and extended archetypes **[secondhand: abstract/search summary; full text not reachable]** — [KU ScholarWorks record](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/3d577385-1583-4411-ba38-59a7e3a734f4). de Clercq confirms the term is Osborn's coinage, made for sections that fit no conventional label **[verified]**

### Inferences
- When each form fits (synthesis; the verified sources describe the forms, not when to use them):
  - **Strophic / verse-refrain:** narrative songs where the story is the point (folk, country, blues, early or cypher-style hip-hop).
  - **AABA:** melody-led songs with a title refrain.
  - **Simple verse-chorus:** groove or vamp songs where verse and chorus share harmony.
  - **Contrasting verse-chorus or verse-prechorus-chorus:** goal-directed builds.
  - **Compound AABA′ with an abridged final A:** the default for band rock and pop.
  - **Terminal climax:** songs that build to one cathartic peak.

## 5. Asymmetry: why perfect symmetry can weaken a song, and sourced techniques

### Takeaway
There is now substantial verified support. Pattison shows imbalance and interruption carrying meaning. Nobile and Stroud document how modern pop deliberately moves the climax away from the chorus. Summach and Nobile document prechorus devices that break regular phrase rhythm. Hudson notes that compound-form reprises are usually abridged. Blume's TOC treats symmetrical phrases as a craft default to be varied.

### Cited Findings
- **Symmetry kills contrast.** Pattison's "Southern Comfort": four back-to-back Common Meter systems across verse and chorus produce "ho hum structure" **[verified]** — [StrongWriter](https://strongwriter.com/?p=288)
- **Mismatched line lengths and rhymes create pull; an interrupted bridge enacts unresolved feeling.** The "Valentine" bridge is cut short and returns to verse 1 **[verified]** — [patpattison.com](https://www.patpattison.com/structure-creates-expectations)
- **One-bar truncation for meaning.** In "Years" the last chorus line takes one bar where the earlier lines took longer spans **[verified]** — [StrongWriter](https://strongwriter.com/?p=288)
- **Phrase fragmentation and shortened units in the prechorus.**
  - "Talking in Your Sleep" moves from 2-bar lyric + 2-bar rest to 1+1, then a 2-bar line with no rest; the kick pattern shortens from four beats to two **[verified]** — [Summach ¶2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
  - The Bee Gees' "Tragedy" prechorus breaks tonic stasis, surges higher, adds support vocals, fragments the lyric into 2-bar then 1-bar units, and ends in "a dramatic, extended pregnant pause" **[verified]** — [Summach ¶28](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- **Drop-out before the chorus.** Since the 1960s the accompaniment has often dropped out in the prechorus's final bar to highlight the chorus arrival **[verified]** — [Nobile, MTO 28.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.3/mto.22.28.3.nobile.html)
- **Anti-telos chorus and drop** **[verified]** — [Nobile 2022](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.3/mto.22.28.3.nobile.html):
  - The build projects a climax, and the chorus withholds it, landing on the song's lowest energy.
  - A drop or instrumental postchorus then often provides the sonic peak, separating the lyrical-melodic high point from the sonic climax (e.g., "Closer").
  - Sometimes the chorus appears twice: first low, then full.
- **Anthem-type postchorus:** the chorus builds and the climax arrives after it, in the postchorus (e.g., "Party Rock Anthem", "I Need Your Love") **[verified]** — [Stroud](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.2/mto.22.28.2.stroud.html)
- **Telescoped sections.** Blends compress form: a prechorus/chorus blend, or a chorus/link blend that goes straight back to the verse **[verified]** — [de Clercq 2017](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.de_clercq.html)
- **Abridged or altered final A in compound AABA** **[verified]** — [Hudson](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.21.27.1/mto.21.27.1.hudson.html)
- **Stolpe:** a bridge that is a partial repeat of the chorus or pre-chorus can be the strongest choice late in a song **[verified]** — [Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/writing-bridges-for-your-songs-can-be-much-easier/)
- **Blume's TOC** lists "Writing symmetrical phrases" alongside "Altering the range, varying the rhythms, and inserting pauses" and "Unexpected note or chord" **[verified TOC; content unread]** — [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/6stepstosongwrit0000blum_i5d1)

### Inferences
- Symmetry is the baseline that makes deviation audible. Sourced deviations are:
  - truncated or extended final lines;
  - unmatched line counts or rhymes;
  - an interrupted bridge;
  - prechorus fragmentation;
  - a pre-chorus drop-out or pause;
  - an anti-telos chorus with a later climax;
  - an anthem postchorus;
  - telescoped (blended) sections;
  - an abridged final cycle;
  - a terminal climax.

### Gaps
- Still no rigorous listener-preference study comparing symmetric and asymmetric forms. False endings and double choruses remain unsourced craft heuristics.

## 6. Musicology on pop/rock form: Summach, Nobile, de Clercq, Stroud, Covach, Everett

### Takeaway
The verified details below correct one attribution from the earlier pass. The phrase "energy gain" is **OMT's** (Lavengood's) summary of prechorus function. Summach's own wording is momentum, "forward formal urgency", and a "more strongly teleological" three-section form.

### Cited Findings
- **Summach 2011, corpus and method.** He draws norms from 700 songs, the Billboard Annual Top 20s for 1955–1989. He warns that the findings may not apply to genres weak on the charts or rising after 1989, such as progressive rock and rap **[verified]** — [Summach ¶5](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- **Summach on prechorus function.** Momentum devices include **[verified]** — [¶3, ¶12](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html):
  - changes of groove, lyric phrasing, unit length, dynamics, register, instrumentation, timbre and harmonic progression;
  - faster harmonic rhythm.

  Momentum building moves from being a transitional effect at the end of a verse to "the function of an entire formal section". In the srdc "departure" (the prechorus's ancestor), momentum comes from accelerating harmonic rhythm, fragmentation and an emphasised subdominant. The subdominant emphasis occurs in about half the pop/rock sentences he examined.
- **Summach on origins.** The prechorus arose where strophic and verse-chorus forms converged on srdc. It was not a simple extension or insertion **[verified]** — [¶3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
  - The dating is internally inconsistent: the abstract says "mid-1960s", ¶2 says prechoruses "started to appear" in the early 1960s, and ¶24 says they showed up consistently from the mid-to-late 1960s.
- **Summach, prechorus incidence by period (Example 26)** **[verified]** — [Summach ¶24, ¶26–27](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html):
  - **1965–69:** verse-chorus songs held about 8 of 20 annual slots, and about a third of them had a prechorus.
  - **Late 1980s:** verse-chorus held "a near monopoly", and about 60% had prechoruses.
  - **Dip around 1970:** linked to funk's stasis and relaxed singer-songwriter styles.
  - **Dip in 1979:** of 12 disco songs only one had a prechorus. In 1978, six of eight Bee Gees songs had one.
- **Summach, sentential strophes.** More than a quarter of the 700 songs contain a sentential (srdc) module, and strophes are sentential almost half the time. The share was 60% in 1964, fell to an average 43% in 1965–69, then averaged about 60% until 1989 **[verified]** — [¶10, n.13, ¶26](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- **Summach on verse vs chorus roles.** The chorus is the focal point: "most engaging" material, an unchanging lyric, added parts. Verses advance the narrative and keep intensity lower, giving "a baseline of musical intensity" against which the chorus sounds climactic **[verified]** — [¶1, ¶25](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)
- **Nobile, *Form as Harmony in Rock Music* (OUP 2020), per Barna's review** **[verified review]** — [Barna, MTO 28.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html):
  - **Functional circuit:** T–PD–D–T across a formal unit.
  - **Sectional verses** complete a circuit (often aaba, srdc or blues). **Initiating verses** don't, typically prolong tonic, and "must lead to a chorus".
  - **Sectional choruses** are autonomous. **Continuation choruses** often begin off-tonic and cadence by the end. **Telos choruses** prompt listeners to "rock out", usually over a tonic prolongation.
  - **Layouts:** sectional vs continuous verse-chorus, plus verse-prechorus-chorus.
  - **Scope:** the repertoire ends in 1991, which Nobile justifies by the post-1991 shift away from harmony as the main driver of form.
  - The "listen closely" gloss for continuation choruses was **not** found in Barna and remains unverified.
- **Nobile 2022, "telos principle"** **[verified]** — [Nobile, MTO 28.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.3/mto.22.28.3.nobile.html):
  - In verse-prechorus-chorus form, the three sections map one-to-one onto **initiation, buildup and arrival**.
  - With a prechorus, the goal is the *beginning* of the chorus. Without one, the goal generally arrives at the *end* of the chorus.
  - In continuous verse-chorus the chorus does both buildup and arrival; in sectional verse-chorus each section does all three.
  - He confirms Summach's derivation: sr became the verse, the expanded d the prechorus, and c the chorus.
  - **Three style periods:**
    - 1960s–80s: harmonic and thematic teleology;
    - 1991–2010: texture, timbre and voice drive the trajectory, and T–PD–D–T is often absent;
    - 2010s: subversions, including anti-telos choruses, drops, and verse–prechorus–drop cycles ("Gangnam Style", "Uptown Funk").
  - Prechoruses "generally exhibit fragmentation" into 1–2-bar groups.
- **de Clercq 2017** ("Embracing Ambiguity in the Analysis of Form in Pop/Rock Music, 1982–1991") **[verified]** — [MTO 23.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.de_clercq.html):
  - **Prototype view:** a section label is compared with "a set of weighted attributes", not checked against necessary conditions.
  - **"Blend"** is borrowed from linguistics, as in "brunch", for passages with two section roles.
  - **Three ambiguity types:** verse ambiguity (weak differentiation), chorus ambiguity (role blends), and bridge ambiguity (the label's different hierarchical meanings).
  - **Examples:** prechorus/chorus, chorus/link and bridge/chorus blends.
  - **Prechorus status** can depend on the "strength of prechorus attributes and the disposition of the analyst".
  - **Interverse:** Endrinal's label for a bridge-like section, sub-typed independent or dependent and continuous or sectional.
  - The 2012 dissertation itself remains unread.
- **Stroud 2022 (codetta and anthem postchoruses)** **[verified]** — [MTO 28.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.2/mto.22.28.2.stroud.html):
  - **Corpus:** 1,335 Billboard Top-40 songs from 2010–2015, coded by two listeners (Stroud and Ensign) as unambiguous or ambiguous.
  - **Codetta type:** the chorus is the climax, and the postchorus adds closure, often by fragmenting and repeating short motives or vocables on tonic (e.g., "Roar", "Undo It").
  - **Anthem type** is "an independent, memorable, and climactic" section after a *building* chorus, tied to EDM strategy and close to Barna's "dance chorus".
  - Repeated short motives or vocables are so typical of postchoruses that they can decide the label.
- **Everett:** the srdc labels (statement, restatement/response, departure, conclusion) come from Everett 1999, *The Beatles as Musicians* **[verified via Summach and OMT]**. *Foundations of Rock* (2009) is cited as a standard introduction **[verified mention in de Clercq]** but was not read.

### Inferences
- Use de Clercq's model in the analyzer: score sections against weighted attribute sets (verse, prechorus, chorus, postchorus, bridge, link) and report blends.
- Use Nobile's initiation/buildup/arrival as the energy-trajectory model for critique, and Stroud's codetta/anthem split to classify what follows the chorus.

### Gaps
- Summach's Example 26 bar chart gives five-year percentages that the text doesn't list in full. Only the 1965–69 (~1/3) and late-1980s (~60%) figures are stated in prose.

## 7. Hip-hop and jam-band form

### Takeaway
Hip-hop form now has a dedicated corpus study: Duinker 2021. Its findings come from search summaries, and the journal host was not allowed. OMT, verified, links strophic hip-hop to the cypher. I found no peer-reviewed form scholarship on jam bands on the allowed hosts. Only books (Olsson, Malvinni) and fan analysis surfaced.

### Cited Findings
- **Strophic hip-hop and the cypher.** OMT relates strophic form in hip-hop to the cypher, where MCs take turns over a repeating beat (citing Duinker 2020). It lists early hip-hop among strophic genres **[verified]** — [OMT AABA/Strophic](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/aaba-form/)
- **Duinker, "Song Form and the Mainstreaming of Hip-Hop Music"** (*Current Musicology* 107) **[secondhand: abstract via search]** — [Columbia journal page](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/currentmusicology/article/download/7177/3992/14952):
  - **Corpus:** 160 songs from 1979 onward.
  - **Section types:** verse, hook and instrumental.
  - **Paradigms:** strophic and verse-hook.
  - **Trend:** the hook gains centrality, with more verse-hook songs, more sung hooks (often by guest singers) and earlier first hooks.
  - **Measures:** hooks are tracked by first appearance and by share of duration.
- **Jam-band form** **[secondhand]** — [search summary pointing to Olsson and Malvinni](https://allmusicbooks.com/node/18964); [Phish.net blog](https://phish.net/blog/1311714510/revisiting-jamming-types):
  - Olsson (*Listening for the Secret*) describes the Grateful Dead's "Framework": play most of the song's form, then a long jam before the closing chorus.
  - Fan usage separates "Type 1" jams (inside the song's structure) from "Type 2" jams (leaving it); the poster admits the terms are imprecise.

### Inferences
- For hip-hop, the plugin's form model should include **verse-hook** and **strophic/cypher** paradigms, with long verses (often 16 bars, a convention not sourced here) and the hook as the title carrier. For jam-oriented songs, model a compact head plus an open section with a cue-based return, and treat the climax as possibly late, near a terminal climax.

### Gaps
- Duinker's statistics and Krims's *Rap Music and the Poetics of Identity* were not read. No scholarly jam-band form taxonomy was found on the allowed hosts.

## 8. Describing section energy and contrast section by section

### Takeaway
Verified sources now give a concrete, multi-domain marker set:
- **Chorus intensity** (Summach 2012 via OMT).
- **Prechorus momentum devices** (Summach 2011).
- **Phrase length, phrase position and chord-tone starts** (Stolpe).
- **Stresses per line and rhyme matching** (Pattison).
- **Harmonic closure and functional circuits** (Nobile).
- **The initiation/buildup/arrival trajectory**, including where the climax goes (Nobile 2022; Stroud).

### Cited Findings
- **Chorus intensity:** denser or more active texture, prominent backing vocals, higher-register melody **[verified]** — [OMT](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/verse-chorus-form/)
- **Section boundary and energy cues** in OMT's U2 "Pride" example: a rhyme closing the quatrain, a drum fill, a more active and doubled guitar, added backing vocals **[verified]** — [OMT intro](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/intro-to-form-in-popular-music/)
- **Momentum devices:** groove, lyric phrasing, unit length, dynamics, register, instrumentation, timbre, harmonic progression and rhythm **[verified]** — [Summach ¶3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.3/mto.11.17.3.summach.html)

### Implications for the plugin

*(Synthesis and design. The source behind each rule is in brackets: [V] = verified this pass, [S] = secondhand. Unbracketed items are craft heuristics.)*

**Corrections from the earlier pass**
- **"Energy gain"** is Open Music Theory's summary term. Attribute it to OMT, summarising Summach. Summach's own terms are momentum and teleology. [V]
- **Prechorus emergence:** Summach dates it to the early-to-mid 1960s (his text is inconsistent), consistent use from the mid-to-late 1960s, and standard by the mid-1980s. The data are about one-third of verse-chorus hits in 1965–69 versus about 60% in the late 1980s, with dips around 1970 and 1979. [V]
- **Nobile's continuation chorus:** drop the "listen closely" gloss. It is unverified. The telos chorus "rock out" gloss is verified. [V]
- **Léveillé Gauvin:** the paper is 2017 online / 2018 print (22/3). There are five features, and voice-entry time is distinct from title time. Self-focus showed no trend. [S]
- **Temperley via Hudson:** compound AABA is effectively the verse-chorus default. The final A is usually abridged (AABA′). [V]

**A. Form-option catalogue (with section jobs)**
1. **Strophic (AAA) / verse-refrain:** each strophe is complete and closed, with a tail (or head) refrain carrying the title. Use it for story-first songs. Hip-hop strophic/cypher variant. [V OMT; Summach]
2. **AABA (32-bar):** A holds the title and main melody. B withholds A, sits off-tonic and often ends on V. After the first AABA, repeat all of it or only BA, with no new lyrics. [V OMT]
3. **Simple verse-chorus:** verse and chorus share the harmony. Contrast comes from melody, lyric invariance, texture and register. Suits groove and vamp songs. [V de Clercq on Covach; Summach on "Good Times"]
4. **Contrasting verse-chorus (+ bridge):** the default layout is VC VC (instrumental/bridge) VC or just C, plus an optional extra chorus. The bridge appears once and replaces a verse or prechorus. [V Temperley via Hudson; OMT]
5. **Verse-prechorus-chorus(-postchorus):**
   - **Verse** = initiation.
   - **Prechorus** = buildup: fragmentation into 1–2-bar units, faster harmonic rhythm, rising register, open ending, optional drop-out or pause in the last bar.
   - **Chorus** = arrival, with the goal at its downbeat.
   - **Postchorus** = codetta (closure, vocables on tonic) or anthem (the real climax after a building chorus). [V Summach; Nobile 2022; Stroud]
6. **Compound AABA′:** verse+chorus units as A, then B, then an abridged A′. [V Hudson; Covach via de Clercq]
7. **Verse-hook (hip-hop):** long verses carry the content and the hook carries the title. The trend is toward an earlier, often sung hook. [S Duinker]
8. **Anti-telos / drop forms (EDM-pop):** the chorus withholds the expected peak, and a drop or instrumental postchorus supplies it. [V Nobile 2022]
9. **Terminal climax / through-composed:** a single, thematically new peak at the end, with no recapitulated sections in fully through-composed forms. [S Osborn 2013; V Osborn 2011; term V via de Clercq]
10. **Extended/jam:** a head (any of the forms above), then an open section, then a cued return to the closing chorus. [S Olsson "Framework"; otherwise heuristic]

**B. Hook-placement rules of thumb**
- **Title in the chorus, in a strategic position (first or last line).** Make the verse lead to it. The melody should make the title's location obvious even without words: a peak, a long note or a rhythmic break. [V Blume TOC; S Blume class transcription; V Pattison "sonic targeting" cross-reference]
- **In strophic, AABA and folk/country forms,** carry the title as a tail refrain. [V OMT; Summach]
- **Use a signature lick** (instrumental hook distinct from the vocal melody) in the intro, turnaround or outro, plus rhythmic hooks. [V Blume TOC "Using signature licks"; S BMI details]
- **Use postchorus vocables or repeated short motives** as a second hook ("na-na", "ro-o-oar"). [V Stroud]
- **Time-to-voice and time-to-title:** shorter is the long-run hit trend (intros down to about 5 s by 2015, titles earlier). Present this as context-dependent (single vs album vs live). [S Léveillé Gauvin coverage]
- **Chorus-first openings** are a recognised recent option. [V Stroud citing Ensign]

**C. Structure-critique checks**
1. **Function match (Pattison):**
   - Do all verses share form (stresses per line, line count, rhyme scheme)?
   - Does the chorus change at least one of rhyme scheme, phrase lengths, phrase count or phrase rhythms?
   - Does the chorus's *first* line already differ? [V]
2. **Common-Meter monotony:** flag verse and chorus that both run the same stress pattern (e.g., 4/3/4/3 then 3/3/3/3) with the same phrase count. [V Pattison "Southern Comfort"]
3. **Expectation audit:** mark unmatched lines and rhymes as open, and check that open or closed structure fits the meaning at that point (e.g., an unresolved feeling suits an open section). [V Pattison "Valentine"]
4. **Prechorus buildup:** look for fragmentation, faster harmonic rhythm, rising register, added voices, and an off-tonic or open ending. Optionally a drop-out or pause into the chorus. Flag a tonic cadence at the prechorus end. [V Summach; Nobile; S Perricone]
5. **Verse type (Nobile):** a sectional (closed) verse followed by a sectional chorus means each must earn its own arrival. An initiating verse gives forward pull. [V Barna]
6. **Bridge:**
   - Does it contrast dynamically (exceed or undercut the second chorus)?
   - Does it use a new or borrowed chord or a change in harmonic rhythm?
   - Does it take a new lyric angle (future, challenge or transformation)?
   - Does it appear once and lead to the final (pre)chorus? In AABA, does it end on V?
   - Also allow "partial chorus repeat" bridges. [V Stolpe; OMT]
7. **Climax location:** is the peak in the chorus (telos), the postchorus (anthem), a drop (anti-telos) or a terminal climax? Flag songs where every cycle has identical energy and closing rhetoric is missing (no late intensification, thinning, repetition or final closure). [V Nobile; Stroud; OMT; S Osborn]
8. **Symmetry audit:** if every section has identical length and scheme, suggest one deliberate deviation tied to meaning:
   - truncated or extended last line;
   - unmatched line;
   - interrupted bridge;
   - prechorus fragmentation or pause;
   - abridged final cycle;
   - telescoped prechorus/chorus. [V Pattison; Summach; Hudson; de Clercq]
9. **Context check:** time to voice, title and chorus against the declared release context. [S Léveillé Gauvin; S HSD]

**D. What a stability/structure analyzer should measure (per section)**
- **Lyric:**
  - line count;
  - **stressed syllables per line** (Pattison's unit), mean and variance;
  - line matching (sequence) and rhyme pairing, open vs closed;
  - rhyme type;
  - title position (first or last line; tail or head refrain). [V Pattison]
- **Melodic:**
  - phrase lengths in bars;
  - phrase start position (before, on or after the downbeat);
  - chord-tone vs non-chord-tone phrase starts;
  - register (mean and peak) vs the previous section;
  - conjunct vs disjunct motion;
  - motif reuse. [V Stolpe; Perricone excerpt]
- **Harmonic:**
  - on- or off-tonic start;
  - open or closed end;
  - completes a T–PD–D–T circuit or not (sectional vs initiating);
  - harmonic rhythm;
  - subdominant emphasis in the departure;
  - borrowed chords. [V OMT; Barna/Nobile; Summach; Stolpe]
- **Rhythmic/arrangement:**
  - groove or kick-pattern change;
  - lyric/rest alternation rate (fragmentation);
  - texture density;
  - backing vocals;
  - drop-outs and pauses. [V Summach; OMT]
- **Derived:**
  - per-section attribute scores for verse, prechorus, chorus, postchorus, bridge and link, with **blends** and confidence [V de Clercq prototype view];
  - initiation/buildup/arrival role per section and climax location [V Nobile 2022];
  - postchorus type, codetta or anthem [V Stroud];
  - form-family guess (strophic, AABA, simple VC, contrasting VC, VPC, compound AABA′, verse-hook, drop, terminal climax);
  - symmetry index;
  - seconds to voice, title and first chorus.
