## 1. Walter Everett: rock's tonal systems, mixture, double-plagal, departures from common practice

### Takeaway
Everett's Table 1 has **six numbered systems with nine sub-types** (1a, 1b, 2, 3a, 3b, 4, 5, 6a, 6b). They are ordered by how far each departs from common-practice harmony and voice leading. He insists that ♭VII–IV–I (the "double-plagal cadence") is a *chromatic* descending-fourths function, not a modal one. The earlier notes' outline was broadly right, but Systems 1 and 3 each have a/b sub-types, and System 3b covers modulation (including the truck-driver's modulation).

### Cited Findings
**Table 1, verbatim categories (paraphrased) [verified]** — [Everett Table 1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.4/w_everett_table1.html)
- **1a:** Major-mode systems with common-practice harmony and voice leading. May be inflected by minor-mode or chromatic mixture.
- **1b:** The same, in minor, possibly inflected by major-mode or chromatic mixture.
- **2:** Diatonic modal systems. Voice leading is common-practice, but harmonic behavior sometimes is not.
- **3a:** Major-mode (or modal) systems with "mixture from modal scale degrees." Common-practice behavior is common but not necessary.
- **3b:** Major-mode systems with "progressive structures." Common-practice behavior holds at lower levels but not higher ones.
- **4:** "Blues-based rock: minor-pentatonic-inflected major-mode systems." Common-practice behavior may sit at deeper levels or in the accompaniment.
- **5:** "Triad-doubled or power-chord minor-pentatonic systems unique to rock": I–♭III–IV–V–♭VII. Common-practice behavior is often irrelevant at the surface.
- **6a:** Chromatically inflected, triad- or power-chord-doubled pentatonic systems of early metal.
- **6b:** Chromatically related scale degrees with little pentatonic basis. Common-practice behavior is irrelevant at deeper levels too.
- (The table page's header reads "Tonal Rhythms," apparently a typo for "Tonal Systems.")

**Example songs per system, from the article text [verified]** — [Everett MTO 10.4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.4/mto.04.10.4.w_everett.html)
- **1a/1b:** The greatest share of chart pop falls into Type 1. Pure-minor examples are rarer; Ozzy Osbourne's "Revelation (Mother Earth)" is one. Billy Joel's "She's Always a Woman" is graphed as fully common-practice, and Everett says most of Joel's writing is closer to Brahms than to Howlin' Wolf (¶7). "Julia" and "The Long and Winding Road" show non-traditional upper voices within normal tonality (¶8).
- **2:** "The Sounds of Silence" is an Aeolian example "with no harmonic function at all" but has clear contrapuntal descent. "Tomorrow Never Knows" is Mixolydian, with I–♭VII–I neighbor motion over a tonic pedal (¶10).
- **3a:**
  - "P.S. I Love You" is in D major with an Aeolian-drawn refrain cadence using ♭VI and ♭VII.
  - "Brown Sugar" has a C-major verse and chorus; its instrumental break adds ♭III and a ♭VI–♭VII–I cadence (¶12).
  - Quasi-modal scales also count as 3a: Björk's "Army of Me" (Phrygian), Beck's "Get Real Paid," and Radiohead's "Everything in Its Right Place." The last combines a major lower tetrachord with an Aeolian upper tetrachord (¶13).
  - "Pretty Ballerina" by the Left Banke has surface roots that look minor-pentatonic, but it is classified as 3a (¶22).
- **3b:** The "truck-driver's modulation" (successive half- or whole-step lifts), with Mariah Carey's "I Don't Wanna Cry" as an example (¶14). Experimental modulations appear in "Still Crazy After All These Years" and XTC's "I'd Like That" (¶15).
- **4:**
  - The blues is "essentially major-mode." In "School Days," melody and lead are pentatonic while bass and piano boogie in major. "The Thrill Is Gone" is a rare minor exception (¶16).
  - Structural harmony lives in the backing, not in the soloistic melody (¶16).
  - In the blues V–IV–I, IV "softens" an authentic cadence rather than carrying harmonic value (¶18).
- **5:**
  - Each minor-pentatonic degree carries a major triad. The resulting chords "are not functionally related" and the patterns "are thoroughly melodic" (¶19).
  - Examples (¶20–21): the I–♭VII neighbor in "Bo Diddley" and "My Generation"; chords over a tonic pedal in "On Broadway," "Uptight" and "Got to Get You Into My Life"; the I–♭III–IV of the "Sgt. Pepper" chorus; "Cult of Personality"; the "In the Midnight Hour" intro ♭VII–V–IV–♭III; the "Proud Mary" riff; "All Day and All of the Night."
- **6a:** "Spanish Castle Magic," the ♭V-to-IV move in "Smoke on the Water," "Electric Funeral," and "My Michelle" (¶24).
- **6b:** "Them Bones" (Alice in Chains), "Fish Bowl Man" (King's X), and "All Mine" (Portishead), where the tonic rests largely on vocal assertion (¶25).

**Double-plagal claim [verified]** — [Everett MTO 10.4 ¶11](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.4/mto.04.10.4.w_everett.html)
- Everett calls ♭VII–IV–I "the double-plagal cadence." He says it is "often incorrectly said to have a modal basis." It is variously labeled Aeolian, Dorian or Mixolydian "amid apparent confusion."
- Lineage given:
  - First appearance: the intro of the Four Tops' "Baby I Need Your Loving" (late summer 1964).
  - Spread into rock in early 1965: "Nowhere to Run," "I Can't Explain," "The Last Time" and "The Night Before."
- He compares it to descending-fifth chains of applied dominants (the "Sweet Georgia Brown" verse). Both are chromatic functions, using sharps in the fifths case and flats in the "double-, triple, or quadri-plagal" case.
- Extended fourth chains occur in "Hey Joe," the "A Day in the Life" bridge and the "Jumpin' Jack Flash" chorus. Such a sequence "establishes neither scale nor tonal center, so no mode can be suggested."
- Footnote 9 points to his fuller treatment in *The Beatles as Musicians* (Everett 2000, pp. 323–26). **CORRECTION:** this means the term dates to at least 2000, not the 2008 date given by the earlier secondhand source.
- OMT calls "double-plagal" "Walter Everett's term" and glosses it as IV/IV–IV–I. It shows "Hey Joe" as ♭VI–♭III–♭VII–IV–I in E — [OMT Plagal progressions](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-plagal.html) [verified]

**Rival readings [verified via Temperley]** — [Temperley MTO 17.1 ¶3.9](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.1/mto.11.17.1.temperley.html)
- Doll (2007) reads ♭VII–IV–I like Everett's "softened" blues cadence, with ♭VII functioning as dominant and IV interpolated.
- Temperley rejects the interpolation view as a general account, because IV is approached from many chords.

**Method and critique**
- The essay grounds its classification in Schenkerian voice leading and harmony (¶5–9) [verified].
- Part II scores 100 Billboard top-four hits from 1957–58 and 382 album tracks from 1999–2000. Each track gets twelve 1–10 criteria, giving 60 points for voice leading and 60 for harmony.
- Mean scores: 43.7/56.6 for 1957–58 vs. about 29/33 for 1999–2000 (¶27–29) [verified].
- A truck-driver's modulation cost an automatic ten points in harmony (¶29) [verified].
- An informal 2005 blog critique says the scoring method was under-described — [Musical Perceptions blog](https://musicalperceptions.blogspot.com/2005/01/journal-club-tonal-systems-in-rock.html) [secondhand, informal]
- The essay closes with Beck's "Lonesome Tears" as a hard case (¶33) [verified].
- *The Foundations of Rock* (OUP 2009) covers harmony in chapters 8–11 — [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/49249) [secondhand; full text not read]

### Inferences
- Use the nine sub-types as an analyzer classifier, collapsed for users into "classical-style major/minor (1)," "modal (2)," "major with modal color or key changes (3)," "blues (4)," "pentatonic power-chord (5)" and "chromatic/metal (6)."
- Under Everett, ♭VII–IV–I and "Hey Joe"-type chains need not imply any mode. The plugin should describe them as "stepping home by fourths." It should not call them "Mixolydian" unless melody and scale content support it.
- Everett places blues structure in the *backing*, not the melody. A chord analyzer should therefore read the rhythm-section chords and not be swayed by blue notes in the vocal.

### Gaps
- *Foundations of Rock* chapter terminology is still unverified.
- *The Beatles as Musicians* pp. 323–26 was not read.

## 2. Kostka & Payne (*Tonal Harmony*): fundamentals worth keeping, and what doesn't transfer

### Takeaway
No full text of Kostka & Payne was accessible; the archive.org copies are lending-only. Chapter titles are verified from publisher TOCs. The keep/drop advice rests on rock-specific evidence that *is* now verified:
- Temperley's corpus data show V–IV is common.
- IV is the leading pre-tonic chord overall.
- Melodic–harmonic divorce is idiomatic.
- Rock chords are mostly root-position.

### Cited Findings
- Publisher TOCs list:
  - "Secondary Functions 1/2" as Ch. 16–17 (6th and 7th editions).
  - "Mode Mixture" (6th) and "Mode Mixture and the Neapolitan" (7th).

  Sources: [McGraw-Hill 6th ed. TOC](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/0073401358/information_center_view0/table_of_contents.html); [7th ed. TOC](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/0078025141/information_center_view0/table_of_contents.html) [secondhand from the earlier pass; not re-fetched]
- archive.org holds the 1995, 2000, 2004, 2009 and 2013 (6th edition) printings, all lending-restricted. No TOC is in the metadata — [archive.org advanced search](https://archive.org/advancedsearch.php?q=title%3A%28tonal+harmony%29+AND+creator%3A%28kostka%29&fl%5B%5D=identifier&fl%5B%5D=title&fl%5B%5D=year&rows=20&output=json) [verified catalogue only]
- In the RS 5x20 corpus, V→IV occurs 392 times, against 514 for IV→V. The "retrogression" is therefore almost as common as the classical direction — [Temperley MTO 17.1, Table 2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.1/mto.11.17.1.temperley.html) [verified]
- Ascending and descending fifth root motions are "roughly equally common overall" in rock — [Temperley MTO 17.1 ¶3.8](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.1/mto.11.17.1.temperley.html) [verified]
- The V–IV–I turnaround's root movement is "considered nontraditional" from a Western-harmony standpoint and has been called "backward." Stephenson (2002, p. 147) is cited — [Wikipedia, V–IV–I turnaround](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues_cadence) [verified page; Stephenson not read]
- OMT: pop/rock chords "are almost always root-position triads or seventh chords." Classical IV–IV6 therefore becomes IV–vi, a bass-driven move that "breaks the rules" of classical syntax. OMT says no published rock harmony theory is equal to Quinn's functional theory — [OMT Harmony in pop/rock](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony.html) [verified]
- Melodic–harmonic divorce (Moore 1995; Temperley 2007; Nobile 2015) is used as a working analytic concept. Spicer, for example, applies it to "Jane Says" — [Spicer MTO 23.2 ¶13](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.2/mto.17.23.2.spicer.html) [verified]
- Nobile does keep T–PD–D–T, as a "functional circuit," and requires a cadence to close one. Other rock theorists (Temperley, Stephenson, Doll) treat cadence as punctuation — [Barna review of Nobile, MTO 28.1 ¶4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html) [verified]

### Inferences
- **Keep:**
  - Function vocabulary (home / away / pulling home), which Nobile shows still organizes rock at section level.
  - Cadence names.
  - Secondary dominants (V/V, V/vi).
  - Mode mixture.
  - Common-tone and stepwise-bass ideas.
- **Drop as rules:**
  - "V must go to I" and "V–IV is wrong."
  - SATB parallel-fifth bans.
  - Melody notes must fit the chord.
  - Obligatory leading tone.
  - Harmonic minor as default.
  - Inversion-based syntax (pop prefers root position plus slash-chord pedals).
- **[BK]** Kostka & Payne's grouping is tonic (I, vi/iii), predominant (ii, IV) and dominant (V, vii°), with a circle-of-fifths "normal progression" chart. This is consistent with the book's tradition but not verified this pass.

### Gaps
- No access to the K&P chapter on harmonic progression or its function chart. Chapter numbers vary by edition.

## 3. Rikky Rooksby: keys, three-chord tricks, borrowed chords, modal and guitar-based writing

### Takeaway
The Library of Congress TOC on archive.org confirms that *The Songwriting Sourcebook* (2003) is organized as a ladder:
1. Three-chord songs
2. Four-chord songs
3. Five- and six-chord songs
4. Flat-degree chords
5. "Reverse polarity"
6. Majors/minors
7. Minor keys
8. Chord types
9. Key changes

"Reverse polarity" is a whole chapter, not an aside. The key-table "columns" detail is still secondhand.

### Cited Findings
- *The Songwriting Sourcebook: How to Turn Chords into Great Songs* (Backbeat, 2003, 224 pp.).
  - Contents: "The three-chord song — the four-chord song — songs with five and six chords — adding flat degree chords — reverse polarity — playing with majors and minors — songs in a minor key — working with chord types — writing songs that change key — a miscellany of techniques."
  - Catalogue summary: it teaches the logic of chord changes for intro, verse, bridge or chorus.

  Source: [archive.org metadata](https://archive.org/metadata/songwritingsourc0000rook) [verified catalogue]
- *How to Write Songs on Guitar* (Backbeat, 2000, 192 pp.) is held lending-only, with no TOC in its metadata — [archive.org metadata](https://archive.org/metadata/howtowritesongso0000rook) [verified catalogue]
- Its three-chord trick is "the commonest songwriting device," tied to 12-bar blues and 1950s rock'n'roll. Variations use any three chords in a key plus a blues chord. The key table puts three flattened chords (♭III, ♭VI, ♭VII) in columns 7–9, beside the six diatonic columns — [OCR scan, sydneyguitarlessons.com PDF](https://sydneyguitarlessons.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/how_to_write_songs_on_guitar.pdf); [Nick Haynes review](https://nickhaynesmusic.com/book-reviews/write-songs-guitar-rikky-rooksby/) [secondhand; host not on allowlist, not re-checked]
- **"Reverse polarity"** swaps a chord's major/minor quality; the earlier pass's reading is that it means using, for example, II or III major, or minor IV/V, in place of the diatonic quality. The chapter title is verified; this definition is **[secondhand]**, and the next chapter, "playing with majors and minors," is consistent with it.
- *Chord Master* (2003) contents:
  - Chords for beginners, triads, moveable shapes.
  - "Chords for songwriters," "Chords for musical styles," "Chords from famous songs."
  - A "chord matrix" of 40+ chord types, from 5ths, sus2/sus4 and add9 up to 13ths.
  - An appendix on slash chords.

  Source: [archive.org metadata](https://archive.org/metadata/chordmasterhowto0000rook) [verified catalogue]
- Related Rooksby titles on archive.org: *Riffs* (2010), *Melody* (2004), *Arranging Songs* (2007), *Lyrics* (2006), *Inside Classic Rock Tracks* (2001), and *How to Write Songs on Keyboards* (2005) — [archive.org search](https://archive.org/advancedsearch.php?q=creator%3A%28rooksby%29&fl%5B%5D=identifier&fl%5B%5D=title&fl%5B%5D=year&rows=30&output=json) [verified catalogue]

### Inferences
- Rooksby's chapter ladder makes a good coaching curriculum: start at three chords, add a fourth, then "flat-degree" color, then quality swaps (reverse polarity), then minor keys, then key changes. Each rung is a menu the plugin can offer.
- The "diatonic six + flat three" table maps directly to the corpus. ♭VII (8.1%) and ♭VI (4.0%) are the most frequent chromatic roots in Temperley's Table 1 (see Q4). That makes the three flat columns the evidence-based first step outside the key.

### Gaps
- Rooksby's exact key tables, his specific key-choice advice (singer range vs. open-string idiom), and his precise definition of reverse polarity could not be read; the books are lending-restricted.

## 4. Other key scholarship: corpus statistics, Doll, Biamonte, Spicer, Richards, Nobile

### Takeaway
Corpus counts (verified):
- **I is 32.8%** of chords, **IV 22.6%**, **V 16.3%** and **♭VII 8.1%**.
- **IV→I (1,162) is the single most common transition**, ahead of I→IV (1,052) and V→I (788).

**CORRECTION:** at *section-ending* cadences (choruses), **V is still the most common pre-tonic chord (32% of songs) and IV second (18.5%)**. Only **2 of 37** plagal cadences use minor iv. Doll's "rogue dominant" is a dominant with **lowered 7̂** (e.g. ♭VII, v), not IV. Spicer's absent-tonic examples are "Jane Says," "I'll Be Around" and "Rock With You," not "Last Friday Night."

### Cited Findings

**Temperley, "The Cadential IV in Rock" (MTO 17.1, 2011) [verified]** — [MTO 17.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.1/mto.11.17.1.temperley.html)
- Sample: the "RS 200," the 99 corpus songs plus the next 101 highest-ranked songs.
- A **sectional cadence** is the end of the chorus vocal line coinciding with a move to I. **118 of 200 songs (59%)** have one.
- **Table 4, pre-tonic chord at sectional cadences:**

  | Pre-tonic chord | Songs | Share of all 200 |
  |---|---|---|
  | V | 64 | 32% |
  | IV | 37 | 18.5% |
  | ♭VII | 7 | 3.5% |
  | II | 6 | 3% |
  | ♭VI | 2 | 1% |
  | VI | 1 | 0.5% |
  | VII | 1 | 0.5% |

- Temperley argues IV is understated, because many V-cadence songs are rock only "by an extremely broad definition." His examples include "Georgia on My Mind" and "The Times They Are A-Changin'" (¶3.5).
- **Minor iv:** "only two out of the 37 plagal cadences have minor IV chords." Even in minor-tonic songs, the cadential IV is usually major; "Stairway to Heaven" is the example (¶3.6, ¶5.4).
- **Named devices:**
  - **Plagal stop cadence:** IV–I, a stop in the instruments just before I, and the vocal phrase ending on I as the next section begins. "Barracuda" is the model (¶4.1–4.6).
  - **Grand plagal cadence:** an emphasized IV–I occurring once near the end, usually with a fermata. Examples include "Whole Lotta Love," "Back in Black" and "Get Back" (§5).
  - **Deceptive IV:** IV in place of an expected I (§6).
  - **Tonicized IV:** ♭VII–IV heard as IV–I of IV, as in "Love Hurts" and "Bargain" (§7).
- Summary of IV's three functions (¶7.5): pre-tonic, deceptive, and local tonal center.

**de Clercq, country "NN 200" corpus [secondhand; midside.com not reachable]**
- About 82% of chord instances are I, IV or V, and 98% of songs have major tonics. About half the songs use four or fewer chords — [de Clercq 2021 SMT text](https://www.midside.com/presentations/declercq_2021_smt_miig_text.pdf)

**Christopher Doll, *Hearing Harmony* (2017) [verified via de Clercq review]** — [de Clercq, MTO 24.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.18.24.1/mto.18.24.1.declercq.html)
- Doll ties function to scale-degree content:
  - 6̂ (either version) means subdominant.
  - 7̂ (raised or lowered) and 2̂ mean dominant.
  - 1̂ means tonic, and 3̂ means "mediant."
- **"Lead dominant"** has major-scale 7̂; **"rogue dominant"** has lowered 7̂ (¶4). **CORRECTION:** IV is not a rogue dominant. ♭VII and v (which carry ♭7̂) are the typical cases [inference from the definition].
- Syntax uses "pre-tonic," "pre-pretonic" and so on, with Greek letters: alpha = tonic, beta = pre-tonic, gamma = pre-pretonic. Prefixes hyper (dominant), hypo (subdominant) and medial (mediant) refine these. V/V in V/V–V–I is "hyper gamma" (¶5).
- Function is "experiential": a chord can be pre-tonic even if it doesn't resolve (n. 4).
- **Schemas:**
  - Ch. 3 covers "short and slot schemas" in angle-bracket root notation, e.g. <II–V–I>.
  - Ch. 4 has nine pentatonic schemas, such as <IV–♭III–I>.
  - Ch. 4 also has fourteen meta-schemas: chromatic lines such as 5–♯5–6, harmonizable as I–III–vi, I–♭VI–IV or I–I+–IV.
  - Chapters 5–6 cover transformations, ambiguity and substitution.
  - "Werewolves of London" and "Sweet Home Alabama" share the loop D–C–G. "Werewolves" centers on G, while "Alabama" supports both D and G (¶6–9).
- Doll's frequency claims are intuition, not corpus-based (n. 6).
- Doll's names for the Axis rotations: **"journey"** for vi–IV–I–V (after "Any Way You Want It") and **"zombie"** for i–♭VI–♭III–♭VII (after the Cranberries) — [Richards MTO 23.3 ¶3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.richards.html) [verified]
- The meta-schema names "dip," "drop" and "slide" from the earlier pass remain **[secondhand]**; they were not in the review text.

**Nicole Biamonte, "Triadic Modal and Pentatonic Patterns in Rock Music," *Music Theory Spectrum* 32/2 (2010) [PDF not reachable]**
- Spicer describes it as a "useful catalogue" of common triadic modal and pentatonic patterns. It treats ♭VI–♭VII–i as the prime Aeolian pattern, the "Aeolian cadence" — [Spicer MTO 23.2 ¶11](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.2/mto.17.23.2.spicer.html) [verified via Spicer]
- de Clercq aligns Doll's scale-degree function with Biamonte's 2010 approach — [de Clercq MTO 24.1 ¶4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.18.24.1/mto.18.24.1.declercq.html) [verified]
- The double-plagal discussion and position-based function come from the abstract and an OUP summary — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/mts/article-abstract/32/2/95/1036808) [secondhand]

**Mark Spicer, "Fragile, Emergent, and Absent Tonics" (MTO 23.2, 2017) [verified]** — [MTO 23.2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.2/mto.17.23.2.spicer.html)
- Definitions (abstract):
  - **Fragile:** the tonic is present but weakened, by inversion or by mid-phrase placement.
  - **Emergent:** the tonic is withheld for a triumphant arrival, usually at the chorus.
  - **Absent:** the promised tonic "never actually materializes."
- **Examples:**
  - **Emergent:** Hall & Oates' "She's Gone" (dominant-prolonging verses; E arrives at the chorus but in fragile first inversion), and Prince's "Little Red Corvette." Spicer hears the verse as IV–V–vi–IV⁷ in D♭ rather than ♭VI–♭VII–i in B♭ minor, with I arriving on "-vette" (¶2–3, ¶11).
  - **Fragile:** "Human" by the Human League, a bass climbing 4̂ to 1̂ that slips back, called the **"Sisyphus effect"** (¶15).
  - **Absent:** "Jane Says" (G–A heard as IV–V in D, never resolving), the Spinners' "I'll Be Around" (♭VI–♭VII with absent i in G♯ minor), and "Rock With You" (heard in D♭ major) (¶13–14, ¶26–28).
  - **CORRECTION:** "Last Friday Night" does not appear in the article.
- **Soul dominant:** IV over 5̂ in the bass, conflating subdominant and dominant, and "especially prevalent" in 1970s soul (¶3).
- **Two-chord vamps (Example 5):**
  - Major–minor a whole step apart can be I–ii ("Don't Let Me Down") or ♭VII–i ("Relax"), depending on accent, pedal, melody and which chord comes first (¶12).
  - "Get Lucky" is heard as a Dorian loop i⁷–♭III–v⁷–IV, "an expansion of the Dorian shuttle i–IV" (¶25).
- Tagg's terms, adopted: **"shuttles"** for two chords, **"loops"** for three or more. Shuttles and loops, plus fadeouts, are what most set pop tonal design apart from art music (¶16).
- Spicer does not hear Clement's I–II shuttles as Lydian. "Man on the Moon" is discussed (¶17–18).

**Mark Richards, "Tonal Ambiguity in Popular Music's Axis Progressions" (MTO 23.3, 2017) [verified]** — [MTO 23.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.richards.html)
- Axis-a/F/C/G are the four rotations of a–F–C–G. Fixed Roman numerals are "unsuitable labels," so he uses Murphy's letter labels (¶2).
- **Corpus:** Billboard Year-End Hot 100, 1990–2016 (2,715 songs).
  - All forms are rare in the early 1990s and peak in 2008–12.
  - The C-form is usually most frequent, but the a-form eclipses it in 2009–14.
  - The F-form barely exists before 2000 and briefly overtakes the C-form in 2012.
  - The G-form appears in only **10 of 2,715** songs (¶7–8).
- **Tonality cues:**
  - "First-chord rule."
  - A **"major-mode bias"** among listeners.
  - Tonality rests "primarily on melodically emphasizing" tonic-triad notes, via harmonic, temporal, rhetorical, contextual and poetic emphasis (¶9–14).
  - Minor-tonic modes, especially Aeolian, rival major after 2000 (¶9).
- **Paired songs:**
  - Aeolian: "Don't Forget Me" (RHCP).
  - Major via bias: "Say Something" and "Self Esteem."
  - Near-identical Sia songs with opposite readings: "Cheap Thrills" (major) and "Never Give Up" (Aeolian) (¶15–23).
  - "Modal migration" names wavering within a song (¶24).

**Drew Nobile, *Form as Harmony in Rock Music* (2020) [verified via Barna review]** — [Barna, MTO 28.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html)
- A **functional circuit** is T–PD–D–T spanning a formal unit. A cadence must close a circuit (¶4).
- **Verses** are "sectional" (complete a circuit; AABA, SRDC or blues) or "initiating" (no cadence; tonic-prolonging; must lead to a chorus).
- **Choruses** are "sectional," "continuation" (often start off tonic, cadence at the end) or "telos" ("rock out," usually over tonic prolongation) (¶5).
- **Layouts:** sectional vs. continuous verse–chorus, and verse–prechorus–chorus (¶8).
- "Teleology in Verse–Prechorus–Chorus Form" (MTO 28.3) on the anti-telos chorus followed by a "drop" — [MTO 28.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.3/mto.22.28.3.nobile.html) [secondhand; page returned 503]

**Temperley, "Scalar Shift" (MTO 17.4)** — [MTO 17.4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.4/mto.11.17.4.temperley.html) [secondhand; page returned 503]

### Inferences
- **Two base rates for ranking suggestions:**
  - At loop level, IV↔I is the default motion.
  - At chorus-end level, V–I is still the most common cadence and IV–I a strong second.
- The plugin should present both as "classic."
- ♭VII→I (386) and ♭VI→I (208) give chromatic "home" moves corpus backing as real options.
- Doll's scale-degree function gives a clean rule: any chord with 7̂ or ♭7̂ (V, v, ♭VII, iii) can "pull home"; any chord with 6̂ or ♭6̂ (IV, ii, iv, ♭VI) "moves away."
- Spicer and Nobile supply named, teachable devices: emergent tonic, soul dominant, Sisyphus loop, plagal stop, grand plagal, telos chorus.

- The country corpus figures remain secondhand.

## 5. Roman numeral conventions for popular music and plain-English explanation

### Takeaway
The legacy OMT site supports the earlier recommendations:
- Roman numerals keyed to the bass, with chromatic roots flagged as ♭VII, ♭VI, ♭III.
- Minor-key patterns labeled from the minor tonic (♭VI–♭VII–i).
- Explicit dual readings for ambiguous loops.

Richards adds letter-name labels as an alternative when the tonic is truly ambiguous.

### Cited Findings
- **OMT's bass-to-chord chart.** Most bass notes go with one chord:
  - do = I, re = II, fa = IV, sol = V.
  - la/le = VI, te = VII.
  - mi/me = [III] or I⁶; la can be IV⁶; ti = V⁶.
  - "Most of the time, all you need is the scale degree of the bass."

  Source: [OMT Harmony in pop/rock](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony.html) [verified]
- **OMT's legacy schema list** (OMT writes chord *roots* in uppercase regardless of quality) [verified] — [OMT Harmony in pop/rock](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony.html):
  - "50s doo-wop": I–VI–IV–V / I–VI–II–V.
  - "Singer/Songwriter": VI–IV–I–V in major, or I–VI–III–VII in minor. Variants: I–V–VI–IV ("With or Without You") and the "deceptive" IV–I–V–VI.
  - "Puff": I–III–IV, which begins a phrase.
  - 12- and 16-bar blues.
  - "Pachelbel": I–V–VI–III–IV–I–IV–V, plus a stepwise-bass version and a truncated one.
  - "Lament": I–VII–VI–V.
  - Minor circle of fifths: I–IV–VII–III.
  - Plagal: I–IV, I–♭VII–IV–I, and ♭VI–♭III–♭VII–IV–I.
- **Schema pages** [verified]:
  - Doo-wop rotates; "Viva la Vida" starts on IV — [OMT doo-wop](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-dooWop.html)
  - Singer/Songwriter: common from the mid-1990s (McLachlan, Jewel, Osborne). Its mode ambiguity is exploited in Heart's "What About Love" (D minor intro, F major chorus) — [OMT Singer/Songwriter](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-sscp.html)
  - Lament: "Thoughts of a Dying Atheist" — [OMT lament](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-lament.html)
- **OMT on minor-mode function** [verified] — [OMT Harmonic functions in minor](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-minor.html):
  - In recent pop minor, ♭VI–♭VII–i (le–te–do) largely replaces iv–V–i (fa–sol–do).
  - This makes minor songs resemble the relative major.
  - U2's "One" pivots ♭VI–♭VII in the minor verse into IV–V of the relative-major chorus. OMT calls this two-key approach increasingly common.
- **OMT's Everett summaries** [verified] — [OMT System 2](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-EverettSystem2.html); [System 3](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-EverettSystem3.html); [System 4](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-EverettSystem4.html); [System 5](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-EverettSystem5.html)
  - System 2: ♭VII is the primary "dominant." Examples: "Scarborough Fair" (Dorian), "Sounds of Silence" (Aeolian), U2's "In God's Country" (I–v Mixolydian), and "Sunday Bloody Sunday" (Aeolian, with a double-plagal tag in the parallel major).
  - System 3: "Everything in Its Right Place" borrows v, ♭VI and ♭VII. "Brown Sugar" uses ♭VI–♭VII–I.
  - System 4: V–IV–I in "Let It Be."
  - System 5: "Sgt. Pepper" chorus I–♭III–IV. A "rotated" form replaces V with ♭VI: "Smells Like Teen Spirit" I–IV–♭III–♭VI. A Phrygian metal variant adds ♭II.
- The Pressbooks edition (viva.pressbooks.pub) has newer schema chapters (lowercase-quality numerals; "Puff" variants; "modal schemas" with the "subtonic shuttle"). It was **not readable** this pass (CloudFront 403), so those details remain [secondhand from the earlier pass].
- Richards uses letter labels (a, F, C, G) instead of Roman numerals for ambiguous loops — [MTO 23.3 ¶2](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.richards.html) [verified]
- Doll writes schemas as root strings in angle brackets, ignoring chord quality — [de Clercq MTO 24.1 ¶6](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.18.24.1/mto.18.24.1.declercq.html) [verified]
- Temperley's corpus tables count roots only (I covers i), and ♭V/♯IV are merged — [MTO 17.1 ¶3.3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.1/mto.11.17.1.temperley.html) [verified]

### Inferences
- **Display:** a Roman numeral with case for quality (I, vi, ♭VII), a Nashville number (1, 6m, ♭7) and the chord symbol.
- **Storage:** keep a root-only, quality-free layer internally (as Temperley and Doll do) for pattern matching. That way I–vi–IV–V and I–VI–IV–V match the same schema.
- **Minor/Aeolian:** label from the minor tonic. Offer the relative-major reading when Richards-type cues are mixed.
- **Plain-English glossary seeds:**
  - ♭VII = "the chord a whole step below home."
  - Double plagal = "stepping home in two fourths."
  - Borrowed iv = "a minor-key shadow of IV."
  - Plagal = "the 'amen' move," the name Wikipedia gives from hymn endings — [Wikipedia, Plagal cadence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagal_cadence) [verified]
  - Soul dominant = "IV over the V bass note: pulling home, softly."
  - Aeolian cadence = "♭VI–♭VII–i, the minor-key climb home."

### Gaps
- No single authoritative style guide for pop numerals exists. The OMT legacy uppercase-only convention conflicts with its newer Pressbooks edition.

## 6. How good teachers coach progressions: options, contrast, harmonic rhythm, pedals, avoided cadences, loops vs. functional progressions; soul/gospel/jam

### Takeaway
The scholarship read in this pass supplies concrete, named coaching devices:
- **Loop vs. circuit:** Tagg's shuttles and loops vs. Nobile's functional circuits.
- **Tonic withholding:** Spicer's emergent and absent tonic.
- **Section-end gestures:** Temperley's plagal stop, grand plagal and deceptive IV.
- **The soul toolkit:** I–IV shuttles, the soul dominant, Dorian i–IV shuttles.

Course pages from Berklee and others give the pedagogical framing for contrast and harmonic rhythm. Jam-band harmony remains unsourced.

### Cited Findings
- **Harmonic-rhythm teaching:**
  - Berklee's *Songwriting: Writing the Music* teaches changing harmonic rhythm for contrast and prosody — [Coursera lecture](https://www.coursera.org/lecture/songwriting-writing-the-music/create-contrast-with-harmonic-rhythm-sr7oP) [secondhand from earlier pass]
  - Berklee Online's *Songwriting Tools and Techniques* has a unit titled "Harmonic Rhythm: Contrast, Highlighting, and Prosody" — [Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/songwriting-tools-and-techniques) [secondhand from earlier pass]
- American Songwriter advises contrast between sections in melody, harmony or rhythm — [American Songwriter](https://americansongwriter.com/?p=3924) [secondhand from earlier pass]
- A dominant pedal at the end of a verse sets up a chorus that starts on I — [LinkedIn Learning](https://lynda.com/Songwriting-tutorials/pedal-point/360622/448050-4.html) [secondhand from earlier pass]
- **Loops vs. circuits** [verified]:
  - Short goalless shuttles and loops, plus fadeouts, define much pop tonal design — [Spicer ¶16](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.2/mto.17.23.2.spicer.html)
  - Nobile's "initiating" verses don't cadence and need the chorus to close the circuit — [Barna ¶5](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.22.28.1/mto.22.28.1.barna.html)
- **Withholding and closing devices** [verified]:
  - Emergent tonic: "She's Gone," "Little Red Corvette" — [Spicer](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.2/mto.17.23.2.spicer.html)
  - Plagal stop cadence and grand plagal cadence; deceptive IV that can be saved for the final chorus ("Think for Yourself," "Strawberry Fields Forever") — [Temperley ¶4–6](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.1/mto.11.17.1.temperley.html)
- **Truck-driver's modulation** [verified]:
  - Everett calls it Type 3b and says it can be an arranger "trying to salvage a weak idea" — [Everett ¶14](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.4/mto.04.10.4.w_everett.html)
  - In "She's Gone," three successive semitone lifts lead to a chromatic-mediant final chorus — [Spicer ¶4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.2/mto.17.23.2.spicer.html)
- **Soul/gospel harmony** [verified]:
  - I–IV alternation, perhaps from the 5–6 shuffle-guitar figure: "Soul Man," "In the Midnight Hour," "After the Gold Rush" — [OMT Plagal](https://openmusictheory.github.io/popRockHarmony-plagal.html)
  - Gospel and blues repeat plagal cadences through sections rather than only at phrase ends — [Wikipedia, Plagal cadence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagal_cadence)
  - The soul dominant (IV/5) has origins in gospel piano and early-20th-century American popular song (n. 3). It sometimes became a color chord in the late 1970s (n. 29) — [Spicer](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.2/mto.17.23.2.spicer.html)
  - The fa-la-do-over-sol sonority (IV over 5̂) appears in gospel-tinged keyboard writing by McCartney and Stevie Wonder ("Knocks Me Off My Feet") — [Everett ¶18](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.4/mto.04.10.4.w_everett.html)
  - The Dorian i–IV shuttle and its loop expansion appear in "Get Lucky" — [Spicer ¶25](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.2/mto.17.23.2.spicer.html)
  - Soul vamps over a tonic pedal appear in "Uptight" — [Everett ¶20](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.4/mto.04.10.4.w_everett.html)
- Capuzzo's "sectional tonality" — [Capuzzo 2009 PDF](https://libres.uncg.edu/ir/uncg/f/G_Capuzzo_Sectional_2009.pdf) [secondhand; abstract only]

### Inferences
- **Coaching move set** (offer as menus with trade-offs, never as single answers):
  1. **Contrast:** change the chorus's first chord or its harmonic rhythm.
  2. **Pedal:** a dominant pedal, or a soul dominant (IV/5), into the chorus.
  3. **Withhold I** in the verse for an emergent-tonic chorus.
  4. **End the chorus** on V–I (most common), IV–I (rock), or a plagal stop (rest the band on IV, sing through to I).
  5. **Save a deceptive IV or vi** for the last chorus.
  6. **Loop vs. circuit:** "a loop that never closes feels hypnotic; a phrase that cadences feels like arrival."
  7. **Key lift:** use sparingly; Everett warns it can read as a patch over weak material.
- **Soul/gospel vocabulary:** I–IV shuttles, IV/5, i7–IV7 Dorian vamps, and repeated plagal "amen" endings.

## 7. Implications for the plugin

### Takeaway
Classify each *section's* tonal system first, using Everett's sub-types. Then label with dual notation, including a root-only layer. Then coach with corpus-ranked, named options. Two corrections change the defaults:
- V–I remains the top chorus-ending cadence, so do not over-promote plagal.
- "Rogue dominant" means a ♭7̂ chord such as ♭VII, not IV.

### Cited Findings
(Synthesis of sources verified above; no new sources.)
- Six systems in nine sub-types — [Everett Table 1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.4/w_everett_table1.html)
- IV→I is the most common transition, while V is the top sectional-cadence pre-tonic (32% vs. 18.5%) — [Temperley MTO 17.1](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.11.17.1/mto.11.17.1.temperley.html)
- ♭VII–IV–I is chromatic, not necessarily modal — [Everett ¶11](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.4/mto.04.10.4.w_everett.html)
- Axis-loop tonality depends on melody; letter labels suit truly ambiguous loops — [Richards](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.3/mto.17.23.3.richards.html)
- Fragile, emergent and absent tonics; the soul dominant — [Spicer](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.17.23.2/mto.17.23.2.spicer.html)

### Inferences
**A. Analyzer: system classification and labels**
1. **Type 1a/1b, functional major/minor:** T/PD/D, secondary dominants, deceptive V–vi, cadence detection (V–I and IV–I both "authentic-strength" in pop).
2. **Type 3a, major plus modal mixture:** borrowed iv, ♭VI, ♭VII, ♭III, v. Label the source, and detect ♭VI–♭VII–I.
3. **Type 2, diatonic modal:**
   - Mixolydian I–♭VII–IV, Dorian i–IV, Aeolian i–♭VI–♭VII.
   - Phrygian ♭II–i is mostly metal (OMT System 5 note); Lydian I–II is rare and disputed (Spicer vs. Clement).
   - Recognize ♭VII–I and ♭VI–♭VII–i as arrivals.
4. **Pop minor:** default to Aeolian. Recognize the ♭VI–♭VII pivot to the relative-major chorus (OMT, "One").
5. **Type 4, blues:** 12- and 16-bar forms, stable I7/IV7/V7, the V–IV–I "softened" cadence. Read structure from the backing, not the vocal blue notes.
6. **Type 5, pentatonic power-chord:** I–♭III–IV–♭VII and the rotated I–IV–♭III–♭VI. Treat these as non-functional melodic patterns and report "no 3rd" for power chords.
7. **Type 6a/6b, chromatic:** name root motions (half-step, ♭V passing). Accept a tonic "by assertion."
8. **Type 3b, progressive and modulation:** sectional keys, truck-driver lifts, chromatic-mediant final choruses.
9. **Ambiguous loops and shuttles:** two readings with confidence scores, using Richards's cues:
   - Melodic emphasis on tonic-triad notes.
   - First and last chord.
   - Phrase-final notes.
   - A major-mode bias as tiebreaker.

   Detect emergent, absent and fragile tonics per section.
10. **Data model:** store root-only strings (Doll/Temperley) for schema matching, plus a quality layer, Roman, Nashville and chord symbol. Detect slash chords and pedals, including IV/5 (soul dominant).

**B. Catalogue of progression families, with base-rate tags**
- **Classic (corpus-top):**
  - I–IV shuttle; I–V–IV–I and the V–IV–I turnaround.
  - I–IV–V; 12- and 16-bar blues; country 1–4–1–5.
  - V–I and IV–I chorus cadences.
- **Common loops (named):**
  - Doo-wop I–vi–IV–V and its rotation IV–V–I–vi.
  - I–vi–ii–V.
  - Axis/Singer-Songwriter: I–V–vi–IV (C-form), vi–IV–I–V ("journey"/a-form), IV–I–V–vi (F-form/deceptive), i–♭VI–♭III–♭VII ("zombie").
  - Pachelbel.
  - Puff I–iii–IV.
- **Plagal and modal:**
  - I–♭VII shuttle; double plagal ♭VII–IV–I; extended ♭VI–♭III–♭VII–IV–I ("Hey Joe").
  - I–♭III–IV ("Sgt. Pepper" / "Smoke on the Water").
- **Aeolian/minor:** ♭VI–♭VII–i (Aeolian cadence), i–♭VII–♭VI–V (lament), minor circle i–iv–♭VII–♭III.
- **Soul/gospel/funk:** I–IV vamp, soul dominant IV/5, Dorian i7–IV7 shuttle and the "Get Lucky" loop, repeated "amen" endings.
- **Tension devices:**
  - Dominant or tonic pedal; withheld (emergent) tonic.
  - Sisyphus loop (a climb to I that slips back).
  - Plagal stop; grand plagal; deceptive IV or vi in the final chorus.
  - Half-cadence section endings.
- **Rare / adventurous (corpus <1%):** ♭II (0.5%), VII (0.4%), ♯IV/♭V (0.3%), and the Axis G-form (10 of 2,715 songs).

**C. Explanation templates**
- "IV–I is the most common chord move in classic rock, so it feels natural. At the very end of a chorus, though, V–I is still the most common landing; pick by how final you want it."
- "♭VII–IV–I steps home by two fourths. It doesn't make the song 'Mixolydian'; it borrows two flat chords."
- "Your loop can sit in C major or A minor. Your melody decides: land phrases on C or E for major, on A for minor."
- "Try holding back the home chord until the chorus; hit songs like 'She's Gone' make the chorus feel like arrival that way."
- Every suggestion should say *why*, *how it feels*, and *how common* it is. The user plays and chooses; the plugin never writes the parts.

**D. Pitfalls**
1. Forcing common-practice rules onto Type 4–6 music.
2. Over-claiming modes from ♭VII.
3. Harmonic minor as the default.
4. False certainty on Axis loops.
5. Flagging melodic–harmonic divorce as an error.
6. Applying SATB voice-leading rules.
7. Ignoring sections and the bass.
8. Single-answer prescriptions.
9. Over-promoting plagal endings. The corpus says V–I still wins at chorus ends.
10. Using song examples that are still tagged [secondhand] in user-facing text.

### Gaps
- Full transition probabilities (for example, separating major IV from minor iv) need the de Clercq–Temperley data files, which are hosted on midside.com and not reachable.
- Jam-band harmony is unsourced.
- The OMT Pressbooks schema chapters, Biamonte's full text, Rooksby's tables and Kostka & Payne's function chart remain unverified.
