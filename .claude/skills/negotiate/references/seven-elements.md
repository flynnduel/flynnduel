# The 7 Elements of Negotiation — Deep Reference

**Methodology**: Negotiation based on interests (Negociacion basada en intereses) / The 7 Elements Framework (Metodologia de los 7 elementos)

**Credit**: Metodologia desarrollada por Club de Negociacion (clubdenegociacion.com) — Consultoria Francisco Gutierrez

## Overview

The 7 Elements Framework provides a comprehensive, structured approach to preparing for and conducting any negotiation. Rather than relying on tactics, tricks, or positional bargaining, this methodology treats negotiation as a problem-solving process where both parties can achieve outcomes superior to their alternatives.

The seven elements are: **Interests**, **MAAN** (Best Alternative), **Options**, **Legitimacy**, **Commitments**, **Communication**, and **Relationship**. Each element is both a preparation tool and a real-time diagnostic lens. When a negotiation stalls, one or more of these elements is typically the bottleneck.

These elements are not sequential steps. They are interdependent dimensions that must be managed simultaneously. Mastery comes from the ability to shift attention fluidly between them as the negotiation evolves.

## 1. Interests (Intereses)

### Definition

Interests are the underlying needs, desires, concerns, fears, and motivations that drive a party's behavior in a negotiation. They are the **why** behind what someone asks for. Positions (posiciones) are what people say they want. Interests are why they want it.

### Why It Matters

Most negotiations fail or produce suboptimal outcomes because the parties argue over positions without ever understanding each other's interests. Two siblings fighting over an orange (the classic example) each demand the whole fruit. Their positions are incompatible. But one wants the juice to drink, and the other wants the rind to bake. Their interests are perfectly compatible — both can get 100% of what they need.

This is not a rare edge case. In most real negotiations, interests diverge from positions in ways that create room for vastly better agreements — but only if you uncover those interests.

### The Iceberg Metaphor

Think of a negotiation as an iceberg. The visible 10% above the waterline represents positions — the stated demands, numbers, and proposals. The hidden 90% below represents interests — the reasons, constraints, fears, aspirations, and pressures driving those positions.

Amateur negotiators engage only with the tip. Skilled negotiators dive below the surface.

### How to Uncover Interests

**Ask "why" questions (preguntas de "por que"):**
- "Why is that important to you?" (Por que es importante para ti?)
- "What problem are you trying to solve?" (Que problema intentas resolver?)
- "What would achieving that allow you to do?" (Que te permitiria lograr eso?)
- "What are you most concerned about?" (Que es lo que mas te preocupa?)
- "If I could give you exactly what you're asking, what would that change for you?"

**Ask "why not" questions:**
- "What concerns do you have about this proposal?"
- "What would make this unacceptable?"
- "Why hasn't this been resolved before?"

**Listen for emotional intensity.** When someone's voice changes, when they repeat a point, when they become defensive — you are near a core interest.

**Map your own interests first.** Before any negotiation, list every interest you have: financial, reputational, relational, operational, emotional, temporal. Rank them. This prevents you from trading away something important for something trivial.

### Common Mistakes

- **Confusing positions with interests.** "I need $80,000 salary" is a position. The interests might include financial security, recognition of worth, ability to cover specific expenses, or competitive equity with peers.
- **Assuming shared interests don't exist.** Parties in conflict often share more interests than they realize: both want a resolution, both want to preserve reputation, both want to avoid litigation costs.
- **Ignoring emotional and relational interests.** People negotiate for respect, acknowledgment, autonomy, and fairness — not just money and terms.
- **Projecting your interests onto the other side.** What matters to you may be irrelevant to them. Ask, don't assume.

### Practical Technique: The Interest Map

Create a four-quadrant grid:

| | **My Interests** | **Their Interests** |
|---|---|---|
| **Shared** | (interests we both have) | (interests we both have) |
| **Different** | (interests only I have) | (interests only they have) |

Shared interests are the foundation of agreement. Different-but-compatible interests are the raw material for creative options. Conflicting interests require legitimacy criteria or MAAN leverage.

### Example Scenario

A software developer negotiating with a client over project scope. The client's position: "We need all 12 features by March." The developer's position: "We can only deliver 8 by March."

Uncovering interests reveals: the client has a board presentation in March and needs a demo-ready product (not all 12 features). The developer is concerned about code quality and team burnout. The resolution: deliver the 5 most visible features polished for the demo, with the remaining 7 on a realistic timeline. Both get what they actually need.

## 2. MAAN (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement / Mejor Alternativa a un Acuerdo Negociado)

### Definition

Your MAAN is the best course of action you can take **if this negotiation fails entirely** — if you walk away with no deal. It is NOT something the counterparty offers you. It is your independent alternative, outside of and apart from this negotiation.

### Why It Matters

Your MAAN is the single most important source of power in any negotiation. It determines your walkaway point. A negotiator with a strong MAAN can afford to be patient, make principled demands, and reject bad deals. A negotiator with a weak MAAN is desperate and vulnerable.

**The fundamental rule**: Never accept an agreement worse than your MAAN. Any deal that gives you less than what you can achieve on your own is, by definition, a bad deal.

### Key Concepts

**Reservation price (precio de reserva):** The worst deal you would still accept, derived from your MAAN. If your MAAN to a salary negotiation is a competing offer of $75,000, your reservation price for this negotiation is approximately $75,000 (adjusted for non-monetary factors like commute, culture, growth).

**ZOPA (Zone of Possible Agreement / Zona de Posible Acuerdo):** The overlap between your reservation price and theirs. If you won't accept less than $75K and they won't pay more than $90K, the ZOPA is $75K-$90K. If there is no overlap, no deal is possible — and you should pursue your MAAN.

### Common Mistakes

- **Treating the counterparty's offer as your MAAN.** Their offer is not your alternative. Your alternative is what exists independently of them.
- **Having no MAAN.** Entering a negotiation without developing alternatives is entering without power. Always cultivate alternatives before negotiating.
- **Overestimating your MAAN.** Be honest and realistic. An untested MAAN is a fantasy, not a strategy.
- **Revealing your MAAN prematurely.** If your MAAN is weak, revealing it destroys your leverage. If it is strong, revealing it can be powerful — but only at the right moment.

### Practical Technique

Before every negotiation, complete this sentence: "If I walk away from this table with no deal at all, the best thing I can do is ___________." Then pressure-test that answer: Is it real? Is it actionable? What does it cost? What does it yield?

## 3. Options (Opciones)

### Definition

Options are possible agreements or components of agreements that could satisfy the interests of both parties. They are the creative proposals, packages, and solutions that negotiators generate to solve the problem. Options are not the same as alternatives (MAAN). Options exist *within* the negotiation. Alternatives exist *outside* it.

### Why It Matters

Most negotiations get stuck because parties see only two options: my proposal or yours. The goal of option generation is to invent a range of possibilities before deciding. This is the difference between splitting the pie (dividing a fixed resource) and expanding the pie (creating additional value).

### Techniques for Generating Options

**Brainstorming (lluvia de ideas):** Before the negotiation, generate as many options as possible without evaluating them. Quantity before quality. Suspend judgment. Wild ideas often lead to practical ones.

**The "What If" technique:** In the negotiation itself, use hypothetical framing to test ideas without committing: "What if we structured the payment differently?" "What if we adjusted the timeline instead of the scope?" This lowers defensiveness because proposals are exploratory, not binding.

**Package proposals (propuestas en paquete):** Instead of negotiating issue by issue (which forces win-lose on each issue), bundle multiple issues into packages. "What if I gave you X on delivery timeline, you gave me Y on payment terms, and we split the difference on Z?"

**Exploit differences in:**
- **Valuation** — things that cost you little but are worth a lot to them (and vice versa)
- **Risk tolerance** — risk-averse parties prefer guarantees; risk-tolerant parties prefer upside
- **Time preference** — some parties value speed; others prefer deliberation
- **Forecasts** — if you disagree about the future, create contingent agreements

### Common Mistakes

- **Premature evaluation.** Killing ideas too early starves the negotiation of creative solutions.
- **Single-issue negotiation.** Negotiating only on price is almost always distributive. Add issues to create trades.
- **Anchoring on the first proposal.** The first offer should not define the solution space. Keep generating options even after initial proposals are on the table.

### Example Scenario

A landlord and tenant disagree on rent increase. Positional bargaining: landlord wants 15% increase, tenant wants 0%. Options approach: longer lease term in exchange for smaller increase, tenant handles minor maintenance in exchange for stable rent, staggered increases tied to building improvements, revenue-sharing arrangement if tenant's business grows. The negotiation shifts from a zero-sum fight to collaborative problem-solving.

## 4. Legitimacy (Legitimidad)

### Definition

Legitimacy refers to external standards, benchmarks, precedents, laws, market rates, expert opinions, and objective criteria that can be used to evaluate the fairness of a proposal. An agreement is legitimate when both parties can explain why it is fair by reference to something outside their own preferences.

### Why It Matters

"Fair" is not defined by how you feel. It is defined by criteria (criterios objetivos). When both parties bring external standards to the table, they move from a contest of wills to a principled discussion about what is reasonable.

Legitimacy is also the antidote to arbitrary demands. When someone says "take it or leave it," asking "on what basis?" shifts the conversation from power to principle.

### How to Use Legitimacy

**Research before the negotiation:**
- Market rates and comparable transactions (comparables del mercado)
- Industry standards and benchmarks
- Legal requirements and regulatory frameworks
- Precedents from previous deals
- Expert valuations and appraisals
- Published data and statistics

**In the negotiation:**
- "The industry standard for this type of engagement is..."
- "Comparable properties in this area are priced at..."
- "The labor code establishes that..."
- "According to [published source], the typical range is..."

**Use the "multiple criteria" approach.** Rarely is there a single legitimate standard. Identify several and use the range they define as the framework for discussion.

### Common Mistakes

- **Using legitimacy only when it favors you.** If you cite market rate when it supports your position but ignore it when it doesn't, you lose credibility.
- **Confusing legitimacy with persuasion.** You are not trying to convince the other side that your standard is "right." You are trying to find standards both sides consider fair.
- **Ignoring the other side's criteria.** They will have their own benchmarks. Engage with them. Dismissing their criteria guarantees resistance.

### Example Scenario

Salary negotiation. Instead of "I want $90K because I deserve it," the candidate says: "According to industry salary surveys for this role in this market, the range is $82K-$95K. Given my 5 years of experience and the specialized certification I hold, I'd suggest a figure in the upper portion of that range." This is harder to dismiss than a naked demand.

## 5. Commitments (Compromisos)

### Definition

Commitments are the specific, concrete, operational agreements that come out of a negotiation. They answer: Who does what, by when, under what conditions, and what happens if they don't?

### Why It Matters

A negotiation is only as good as the commitments it produces. Vague agreements ("we'll figure it out," "let's stay flexible," "we'll revisit this later") are not agreements at all. They are invitations for future conflict.

Well-structured commitments are:
- **Specific** — precisely defined actions or deliverables
- **Measurable** — objective criteria for completion
- **Time-bound** — clear deadlines
- **Assigned** — each commitment has an owner
- **Enforceable** — consequences for non-compliance are defined

### Practical Techniques

**Draft the agreement during the negotiation.** Don't wait until the end to write things down. Summarize agreements as you go: "So we've agreed that X will happen by Y date — is that correct?"

**Build in milestones and checkpoints.** For complex deals, break implementation into phases with review points.

**Define dispute resolution in advance.** What happens if there's a disagreement about whether a commitment was met? Arbitration? Mediation? Escalation to specific individuals?

**Make commitments reciprocal.** One-sided commitments breed resentment. Ensure both sides have skin in the game.

### Common Mistakes

- **Leaving ambiguity in key terms.** "Reasonable effort," "timely delivery," and "market rate" mean different things to different people. Define them.
- **Assuming goodwill replaces structure.** Even with the best intentions, people forget, priorities shift, and organizations change. Structure survives where goodwill fades.
- **Over-committing.** Promising more than you can deliver destroys trust faster than tough but honest negotiation.

### Example Scenario

After a partnership negotiation, instead of "we'll collaborate on marketing," the commitment is: "Partner A will produce 2 joint marketing assets per quarter. Partner B will distribute them to their email list of 10,000+ within 5 business days of receipt. Both parties will review campaign results quarterly on the first Friday of each quarter."

## 6. Communication (Comunicacion)

### Definition

Communication in negotiation encompasses not just what you say, but how you listen, how you ask questions, how you manage emotions (your own and theirs), and how you signal openness to problem-solving rather than combat.

### Why It Matters

Most negotiation failures are communication failures. Parties who could have reached agreement don't because they spoke past each other, assumed the worst, escalated emotionally, or failed to listen.

The ratio of listening to talking is the simplest diagnostic of negotiation skill. Amateurs talk 70% of the time. Experts listen 70% of the time.

### Key Communication Skills

**Active listening (escucha activa):** Full attention. No interrupting. No formulating your response while they speak. Demonstrate understanding before responding.

**Paraphrasing (parafraseo):** Repeat back what you heard in your own words: "So what I'm hearing is that your main concern is timeline certainty — is that right?" This achieves two things: it confirms understanding and it makes the other side feel heard.

**Open questions (preguntas abiertas):** Questions that cannot be answered with yes/no: "How do you see this working?" "What would be most helpful for you?" "What challenges are you facing with the current arrangement?"

**"I" statements (declaraciones en primera persona):** "I'm concerned that..." rather than "You always..." This reduces defensiveness. It communicates your experience without attacking their character.

**Acknowledging emotions (reconocer emociones):** "I can see this is frustrating" or "It sounds like you've invested a lot in this." Acknowledging emotions is not agreeing with someone's position. It is recognizing their humanity.

**Managing your own emotions:** Know your triggers. Take breaks when needed. Preparation reduces anxiety. A prepared negotiator is a calm negotiator.

### Non-Verbal Cues

- Eye contact communicates engagement
- Open body posture signals receptivity
- Nodding encourages continued sharing
- Mirroring builds rapport unconsciously
- Silence after a question gives space to think deeply

### Common Mistakes

- **Listening to reply rather than to understand.** If you are mentally preparing your counter-argument while they speak, you are not listening.
- **Asking leading questions.** "Don't you think it's fair that..." is not a question. It is a disguised assertion.
- **Ignoring emotional signals.** When the conversation shifts from content to emotion, staying on content is ineffective. Address the emotion first.
- **Over-communicating your position.** Repeating your demands louder or more often does not make them more persuasive.

## 7. Relationship (Relacion)

### Definition

The relationship element addresses the quality of the working dynamic between the negotiating parties — trust, respect, credibility, rapport, and the ability to work together effectively, now and in the future.

### Why It Matters

Separating the people from the problem (separar las personas del problema) is one of the most important principles in interest-based negotiation. You can be firm on substance while being respectful and constructive with the person. In fact, being tough on the problem and soft on the people produces better outcomes than being either a doormat or a bully.

### The Principle: Be Unconditionally Constructive (Ser Incondicionalmente Constructivo)

This does not mean being "nice" or making concessions to preserve the relationship. It means:

- **Even if they are unreasonable, be reasonable.**
- **Even if they don't listen, listen to them.**
- **Even if they are deceptive, be trustworthy (not trusting).**
- **Even if they try to coerce, use persuasion and legitimacy.**
- **Even if they reject your interests, take their interests seriously.**

Being unconditionally constructive is not weakness. It is strategic discipline. It creates the conditions for the other side to reciprocate, and even when they don't, it protects your reputation and credibility.

### Trust-Building Techniques

- **Follow through on small commitments.** Trust is built incrementally. If you say you'll send the document by Tuesday, send it by Monday.
- **Acknowledge their constraints.** "I understand you're under pressure from your board" shows you see them as a person, not an obstacle.
- **Share information strategically.** Gradual, reciprocal disclosure builds trust. Sharing everything immediately can be naive. Sharing nothing is adversarial.
- **Separate the relationship from the negotiation substance.** If you disagree on price, don't let that disagreement poison your regard for the person. Humans have a tendency to dislike people who disagree with us. Resist it deliberately.

### Long-Term vs. Transactional

In a one-time transaction, relationship investment has limited returns. In ongoing relationships — between business partners, colleagues, vendors and clients, neighbors — the relationship IS the negotiation. How you negotiate today determines whether they negotiate with you tomorrow.

Research consistently shows that negotiators who invest in relationships achieve better cumulative outcomes over repeated interactions than those who maximize short-term gains.

### Common Mistakes

- **Sacrificing substance to preserve the relationship.** This is not what "be soft on people" means. You can say "no" respectfully. You can disagree warmly.
- **Confusing the relationship with the deal.** "We're friends, so give me a better price" conflates two separate things. Good relationships deserve good deals, but the deal should be justified by interests and legitimacy, not by guilt.
- **Retaliating when they behave badly.** If they are hostile, being hostile back escalates. Being unconditionally constructive does not mean accepting bad behavior — it means addressing it with firmness and respect rather than counter-aggression.

### Example Scenario

A supplier delivers late and a key client is angry. The competitive approach: threaten to switch suppliers, demand penalties, withhold payment. The interest-based approach: "This late delivery caused [specific problem]. I want to understand what happened on your end, and I need us to find a way to prevent this going forward. Here's what I need from you..." This addresses the problem without damaging a valuable long-term supplier relationship.

## How the 7 Elements Work Together

The elements are a system, not a checklist. In practice:

- **Interests** tell you what to aim for.
- **MAAN** tells you when to walk away.
- **Options** tell you how to get creative.
- **Legitimacy** tells you how to evaluate proposals.
- **Commitments** tell you how to close.
- **Communication** is the vehicle for all of the above.
- **Relationship** is the context in which all of the above operates.

A preparation framework for any negotiation should address all seven elements for both yourself and the other party. A diagnostic framework for a stalled negotiation should ask: which element is the bottleneck?

---

## Preparation Checklist

Before any negotiation, prepare on all 7 elements:

1. **Interests**: What are my interests? What are theirs? Which are shared? Which are different?
2. **MAAN**: What is my best alternative? What is theirs? How can I improve mine?
3. **Options**: What creative solutions could satisfy multiple interests? What trades are possible?
4. **Legitimacy**: What external standards support my proposals? What standards might they cite?
5. **Commitments**: What specific outcomes do I want? What am I willing to commit to?
6. **Communication**: How will I listen effectively? What questions will I ask? What tone will I set?
7. **Relationship**: What is the current state of the relationship? How do I want it to be after the negotiation?
